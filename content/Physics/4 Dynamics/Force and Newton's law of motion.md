#unit3 #force_and_newton_law_of_motion #grade9 
### _force_

- is a pull or a push of an obj
- **forces** in general can be grouped into 4:

    1. **electro magnetic force** - deals with charged obj

    2. **Gravitational force** - Force due to gravity.

    3. **Strong nuclear force** - Deals with interaction within the nuclei of an atom.

    4. **Weak nuclear force** - Dealing with interaction within the nucleus.

- *Force* also can be categorized into *Contact* and *Non-contact*.

  **Contact force** 
  - Forces where objects are required to touch.
  - **Example**: Frictional force

  **Non-contact force**
  - Is force where objects are not required to touch.
  - **Example**: Magnetic force, Electrostatic force, Gravitational force

- *All forces are vectors*.
- The SI unit is **Newton (N)**.

- *Sir Isaac Newton* conducted three laws describing how force and motion are related.

  1. **Newton's first law (Law of Inertia)**

  2. **Newton's second law (Law of Acceleration)**

  3. **Newton's third law (Law of Action and Reaction)**

###  **Newtons first law**
  - States an object will remain at rest or traveling at a **Constant velocity** unless an  external force acts upon it,

- _A force is always required to move an object_
    - _to speed up_
    - _to slow down_
    - _or to change direction_

- _If the object is not doing any of these then there is no force acting on the object._

## **Mass and Inertia**

- **Inertia** *is defined as the measure of resistance of a body to change their state of motion.*

- _The property of an object to remain at rest or moving at a steady speed in a straight line._

>[!note] _The Inertia of a body depends on its mass_

- _The greater the mass of an object_ _The greater its inertia they are directly proportional_

_The word Inertia came from the Latin word "iners" meaning idle or lazy.

# _Hooke's Law_

- Robert Hooke was a _Physicist_ who  was _interested_ in the effect of force _had_ on _spring_.

>[!Hooke's Law]
> The _greater_ the force _applied_ (load) to the _spring_, the _greater_ the _extension_ will be.

- The force _applied_ to the _spring_ is directly proportional to the _extension_.

- When you _apply_ double the force, the spring would extend twice the _first_.

- **Load** - the force _applied_ to the _spring_.

- **Extension** - Increasing in length of a spring.

- **Spring Balance** - A device used to measure _force_ via the _extension_ of the _spring_.

    - **Spring Constant** - a measure of stiffness of a spring (Symbol _k_). 

    -  **Stiffness** is the _amount of force required to stretch or compress a_ Spring.

A stiff spring might have a spring constant of 1000 _N/m_.

- You can _determine spring constant_ by using **force-extension** graph.
- Gradient line _equal to the spring constant_.
    - Gradient is the slope of the line on this graph.
- $K$ is measured in $N/m$.

## Elastic Limit

**Elastic Deformation** means when forces are removed, the object will return to the original length (force is proportional to the extension.)

 >[!defination] 
 >a spring will only stretch elastically up to a certain point, that is called **elastic limit**.
 
- After the elastic limit, the deformation is called **plastic**.
- **Plastic Deformation** means when forces are removed, the object will not return to the original length because the force is no longer proportional to the extension. It has been permanently stretched.

- **Deformation**: The change in shape, it could be either _temporary_ or _permanent_.

- If the applied force is small, the deformation is _temporary_.
- If the applied force is too long, the deformation is _permanent_.

>[!note]  The force applied is directly proportional to the extension of a spring up to the _**elastic limit**_.

## Newton - Second Law

- The overall force acting on any object is referred to as the **resultant force**. This is often called **net force** or $F_{net}$
- It is defined as, **the vector produced when two or more forces act upon an object**.
- It is calculated by **vector** _addition_.
- If forces are in parallel, it is easier to determine the resultant vector. However,
- If the forces are not in parallel, we then use the parallelogram rule.
- **Equilibrant force**: Sometimes it is helpful to use equilibrant force. The force required to cancel out the resultant force will result in there being no resultant force acting on the body.

- For any system is always equal in magnitude to the resultant force. But it acts in the opposite direction.
- **Example:** Two forces are acting on a boat. One force of 400 N is due to current in the river, acting downstream. The other force due to the propeller has a magnitude of 500 N and acts at an angle of 50° to the river bank. Determine the resultant force acting on the boat.
    First, resolve 500N into horizontal and vertical components.  

    - $VC = \sin \theta = \dfrac{Opp}{hyp}$  
    - $Opp = \sin \theta \times hyp$  
    - $Opp = \sin 50^\circ \times 500N$
    
      $F = 383N \uparrow$ 

$$ H.C = \cos \theta = \frac{adj}{hyp} \Rightarrow adj = \cos \theta \cdot hyp $$

$$ adj = 500N \times \cos 50^\circ = 321N $$

Second, add the horizontal or vertical force to give you resultant horizontal/vertical force.

$$ F_{\text{net horizontal}} = 321N + 400 = 721N $$

Third, use the Pythagoras’s theorem to determine the magnitude of the $F_{net}.$

$$ F_{\text{net horizontal}} = 721N $$

$$ F_{\text{net vertical}} = 383N \uparrow $$

$$ F_{\text{net}}^2 = 383N^2 + 721N^2 $$

$$ F_{\text{net}}^2 = 666,530 $$

$$ F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{666,530} $$

$$ F_{\text{net}} = 816N $$

**Fourth**, find the resultant force's direction.

$$ F_{\text{net horizontal}} = 721N $$

$$ F_{\text{net vertical}} = 383N \uparrow $$

$\tan \theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$

$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\right) $$

$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{383}{721}\right) $$

$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}(0.53) $$

$$ \theta = 63^\circ $$

**The angle is $90^\circ - 63^\circ = 27^\circ$

## **Force and Acceleration**

- A force is required to make an object:
    - *to speed up*
    - *to slow down*
    - *to change direction*

- If an object is doing any of this, we can say it is *accelerating*.

- If there is a *resultant* force acting on an object, then that object will *accelerate*. The force is said to be *unbalanced*.

- If there is no resultant force acting on an object, then the object will not accelerate. The force is said to be *balanced*.

>[!Newton's Second Law]
> states that *the force applied is directly proportional to the acceleration*.

- The greater the mass, the lower the acceleration.

- The acceleration occurs in the direction of the force.

This gives us:
$$
F_\text{net} = m \cdot a \quad \text{or} \quad F = ma
$$

**Example:**
1. $m = 1200 \text{kg}$
2. $\vec{a} = 2 \text{m/s}^2$

$$
F_\text{net} = ? \quad \Rightarrow \quad F = ma = 1200 \text{kg} \times 2 \text{m/s}^2 = 2400 \text{N}
$$

Therefore, $F_\text{net} = 2400 \text{N}$.



 **$F_{net} = ma$** ***In Several Forces***

- *If several forces acting on an obj are in order to determine its acceleration*,
- *we must first determine the resultant force*
- *To determine the acceleration we use $F = ma$*.
  
$$F = ma \quad \Rightarrow \quad a = \frac{F}{m}$$

**Example 1**:
  
$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
 5\text{kg} & \rightarrow & 10\text{N} & \rightarrow 30\text{N} \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{10 + 30}{5} = \frac{40 \text{N}}{5 \text{kg}} = \underline{8 \, m/s^2} \text{ to the right.}$$

*Forces in the same direction are added*.

**Example 2**:

$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
 40\text{N} & \leftarrow &  3\text{kg} & \rightarrow 60\text{N} \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{60 - 40}{3} = \frac{20}{3} = 6.6 \, m/s^2$$

\- *Forces in the opposite direction, subtract.*

**Example 3**:

$$F_{net} = ma = 60 \times 2.0$$
$$F_{net} = 120$$
$$F_{net} = F_1 + F_2$$
$$120 = 40 + F_2$$
$$F_2 = 80 \, \text{N}$$

## **Mass and Weight**

- Mass and weight are completely different terms. Let's see how.

### *Mass*

- *Mass* is defined as the amount of substance in a body or measure of quantity of matter.
- A **scalar quantity**.
- The measure of a body depends on its mass which means- measure of an object's *inertia*.
- SI unit is *kilogram (kg)*.
- It is the same everywhere.
  
  $$Mass = Volume \times Density \rightarrow M = V \times ρ$$

### **Weight**

- The force expressed by the object due to the gravitational pull of the Earth.
* It is a vector physical quality.
* SI unit is **Newton (N)**.
* It is directed to the center of the Earth. 
* It varies place to place due to the variation of gravity on the Earth surface.

* **$Weight = mass \times gravity = W = m \times g$**

### True Weightlessness and Apparent Weightlessness

*Apparent weightlessness* is when only one force acting is **your weight**.

**True weightlessness** is when your weight is zero.

* Take a person of mass of 56 kg, his weight would be:

$$W = mg$$

$$W = 56 \times 10$$

$$W = 560N$$

* If he is accelerating upwards at 2 m/s² it would be:

$$F = ma$$

$$F = 56 \times 2$$

$$F = 112N$$

So the contact or the apparent force should by **672N (560+112)**

### Net Force Considering Weight

*Example:* A train with a mass of 15,000 kg. If its Engines provide a force of 200,000 N. What is **$(\vec{a})$**?

- **F = ma**

- $a = \frac{F_{net}}{m}$

  $a = \frac{200,000}{15,000}$

 $a = 13.3 \, \text{m/s}^2 \quad \text{this is wrong!}$

- Always remember to use free-body diagrams. This really helps to identify the forces acting on the object.

## _Frictional Force_

- Friction is **contact force** generated when one object tries to slide to the other.

- It always tends to act in the **opposite motion.** There are two types of friction:

  - **Static friction**

  - **Kinetic friction**

### **Static Friction**

- It is a kind of friction, trying to move against each other but not yet moving.
- It is represented by a symbol **μS**.
- The value of static friction or the force before sliding occurs is called _limiting friction_.

### **Kinetic Friction**

- It is a kind of friction that occurs when two objects slide over each other.
- It always acts in the _opposite direction_.
- It is also called _dynamic friction_.
- It is represented by a symbol **μK**.
- It is the friction force after limiting friction.

**The factor affecting the friction are:**

1. Roughness of the surface
2. Weight of an object

**Friction can be calculated by using this formula:**

$$f = \mu N$$

_where_

- **f** is friction force
- **μ** is coefficient of friction
- **N** is normal contact force

>[!note]  **μ** depends on the roughness of surfaces.

* If the block is horizontal and there is no vertical acceleration, then the normal contact force is equal to the weight i.e. **$N = W = mg$**.

## Friction and Inclined Plane

### Inclined plane

are a sloping surface or ramp.
- If an object is resting on an inclined plane:
    - normal contact force is reduced.
    - weight acts downwards.
    - friction force is induced.

>[!Note] $$F_{N} = mg \cos \theta$$

$$F = \text{force}$$
$$\sin \theta = \text{angle}$$
$$W = \text{weight}$$

- Frictional force is directly proportional to the normal force.
$$Ff \propto Fn$$
$$Ff = \mu Fn$$

## Newton's Third Law

- States that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
$$-F_A = F_R$$

* It is also sometimes called Newton's Pair or Action and reaction pair. It is necessary to remember that they are equal and opposite.
