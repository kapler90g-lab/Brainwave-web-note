## 3. making of physics knowledge
One way of understanding the concept of knowledge is to look at the different ways in which we acquire knowledge. There are two types of knowledge from two entirely different sources.
First, there is knowledge through experience: seeing something, hearing about something, feeling something called a **posterior knowledge**. 
Second, there is knowledge that does not come from experience, but 
perhaps instead is intuitively supplied from reason itself, such as logical and mathematical truths called
**a priori knowledge.**

### 1. **A Priori Knowledge**

#### 1. **Definition**:

A priori knowledge is knowledge that is independent of experience. It is based on reasoning, logic, or deduction and is known before any empirical verification ![[empirical verification|.]]

#### 3. **Explanation**:

- **A priori knowledge** refers to information that can be gained through logic or reason alone, without the need for sensory or experiential data.
- It is typically associated with **analytic truths** or **necessary truths**—statements that are true by their nature and cannot be denied without contradiction.
- This form of knowledge is considered **universal and necessary**. For example, the proposition "All bachelors are unmarried" is a priori because it is true by definition.

#### 4. **Example**:

- **Mathematics**: The statement "2 + 2 = 4" is an example of a priori knowledge. This fact does not require any empirical observation to be known as true.
- **Logic**: [[The law of non-contradiction]], which states that "A cannot be both A and not-A at the same time," is an a priori truth known purely by reason.

#### 5. **Conclusion**:

A priori knowledge is crucial in fields like mathematics and logic, where truths can be discovered independently of observation or experience. It provides a foundation for certain knowledge that remains true under all circumstances.


---

### 2. **A Posteriori Knowledge**

#### 1. **Definition**:

A posteriori knowledge is knowledge that depends on [[empirical verification]] or sensory experience. It is derived after direct observation or interaction with the world.


#### 3. **Explanation**:

- **A posteriori knowledge** is based on observations, experiments, and sensory data. It often involves **synthetic truths**, which are contingent on the way the world actually is.
- Unlike a priori knowledge, which is known through reasoning alone, a posteriori knowledge requires real-world experience and can vary depending on circumstances.
- For example, the proposition "Water boils at 100°C" is a posteriori knowledge because it is derived from experimental observation under specific conditions.

- Experiential (a posteriori) knowledge is of many types. Four of them are **sensory perception, introspection, memory, and testimony**: these are the four main ways of acquiring knowledge through experience.
   -  **sensory perception**: is the most dominant source of posterior knowledge that is That is gathered through our perceptions like through seeing hearing touching and feeling and etc
   - **introspection**: is the act of looking within oneself to observe one’s mental and emotional processes. When someone offers an introspective description.
   - **Memory**: a memory is like a recording device that captures events that one can experience more or less in the order that they occur.
- **Testimony**: Testimonies from written sources are usually more reliable than oral sources, but much depends on the integrity of the author, publisher, and the methods of fact-gathering.


#### 4. **Example**:

- **Science**: The fact that "The Earth revolves around the Sun" is an example of a posteriori knowledge. This truth was discovered through empirical observation and evidence.
- **Everyday Life**: The statement "It is raining outside" is a posteriori knowledge, as it requires sensory verification (e.g., seeing or hearing the rain).

#### 5. **Conclusion**:

A posteriori knowledge plays a crucial role in the sciences and everyday reasoning, as it relies on empirical data that reflects the contingent state of the world. While it provides detailed and contextual information, it is also subject to change based on new evidence.


---

### 3. **Comparison of A Priori and A Posteriori Knowledge**

#### 1. **Definition**:

A comparison between a priori and a posteriori knowledge involves analyzing the distinction between knowledge gained through pure reasoning (a priori) and knowledge gained through experience (a posteriori).


#### 3. **Explanation**:

- **A priori knowledge** is independent of experience and known through reason alone, while **a posteriori knowledge** is dependent on [[empirical verification]] and sensory data.
- Philosophers like **Immanuel Kant** have explored the relationship between these two types of knowledge, suggesting that some knowledge may be a blend of both, where concepts might be known a priori but verified a posteriori.
- In mathematics and logic, a priori knowledge is dominant, whereas in the natural sciences, a posteriori knowledge is fundamental to understanding the physical world.

#### 4. **Example**:

- **A priori**: "All triangles have three sides" is a universally true statement derived from the definition of a triangle, and it does not require physical triangles to verify.
- **A posteriori**: "All swans are white" was historically considered true until empirical observation in Australia revealed black swans. This illustrates the fallibility of a posteriori knowledge based on limited experience.

#### 5. **Conclusion**:

Both a priori and a posteriori knowledge play essential roles in our understanding of the world. A priori knowledge provides certainty and universality, while a posteriori knowledge offers contextually accurate information that can evolve with new evidence. 

### the scientific method
is The scientific method is an order of serious to acquire knowledge based on experimental evidences

**Example**:

1. **Observation**: "The sky appears blue during the day."
2. **Question**: "Why is the sky blue?"
3. **Research**: You learn that light scattering in the atmosphere affects colors.
4. **Hypothesis**: The sky is blue because molecules in the air scatter blue light from the sun more than other colors.
5. **Experiment**: You observe the sky at different times and places, recording its color.
6. **Analysis**: Data shows the sky consistently appears blue due to light scattering.
7. **Conclusion**: Your hypothesis is supported: the blue color results from the scattering of shorter blue wavelengths of light.
