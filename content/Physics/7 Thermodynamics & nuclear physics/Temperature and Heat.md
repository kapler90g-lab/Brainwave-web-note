## Temperature and Heat

### Temperature
- is the measure of the **Hotness** and **Coldness** of a body.
- SI Unit: **K**
- It is the effect.
### Heat
- It is the transfer of  *temp.* from high to low. *(from warm to cool)*
- SI Unit: **J**
- It is a form of **Energy**
- It is the **Cause**
- Temp. depends on the measurement of the *average* **KE** of a particle.
- Heat is the sum of all the **KE** and **U**,
  
$$
\Delta Q = \sum E_K + \sum U
$$

### Temperature Scales
There are three major Temp. Scales. These are,
- **Celsius Scale (°C)**
- **The Fahrenheit (°F)**
- **Kelvin (K)**

1. **Celsius Scale (°C)**
   - Uses a fixed point of 0 °C *(freezing point)* and 100 °C *(boiling point)*.

2. **The Kelvin Scale (K)**
   - Uses a fixed point of **absolute zero** and greater.

3. **The Fahrenheit Scale (°F)**
   - Uses a fixed point of **ice (32°F)** and **normal body temp (98°F)**.

- **KE** is directly proportional to **temp.**
- Solid molecules **do not move**. They *vibrate* at fixed points.
- Fluids (liquid and gases) molecules **move freely**.
- When **temp.** increases, the substances **increase in size** known as **Expansion.**
- The expulsion of substances on heating is known as **Thermal Expansion.**
- Heat flows from a *Hotter body* to a *Colder body*.

### Thermal Equilibrium
When there is the movement of heat energy from one substance to the other, substances they are in contact. This contact is known as **Thermal Contact**.

- *Heat Flow from Hotter to Colder Objects*
- When a *Hotter Substance* loses heat energy and gains it, the **Other** Substance (which is in thermal contact with it) will be equal at the same rate. This is known as **Thermal Equilibrium**

  - If two bodies are at **Thermal Equilibrium**, they have the same temperature.
  
- This can be explained by the two laws of thermodynamics,

### First Law of Thermodynamics

- Also known as **Law of Conservation of Energy**.

- It states that: *Energy is neither created nor destroyed, just transferred*. If a gas (or others) have a certain **Internal Energy** (the sum of all $\Sigma KE + \Sigma PE$) of a body, will equal to the heat energy it has gained plus the work done by the gas.

  $$ \Delta U = \Delta Q + \Delta W $$

### Second Law of Thermodynamics

- The second law of Thermodynamics states that:

  - Heat generally cannot spontaneously move from a colder object to a hotter object without work being done.

## Expansion of Solids

### Linear Expansion of Solids (1D)

- When a solid expands by its length, it is known as **Linear Expansion**.

- The rod of a temperature change $\Delta T$, and increases in length ($\Delta l$) is the difference between the starting length $l_1$ and the final length $l_2$.

  Mathematically,
  
  $$ \Delta l = l_2 - l_1 $$
  
  - Fractional Increase in Length 
  
    $$ \frac{\Delta l}{l_1} $$

  - The fractional increase per unit temperature (${}^\circ C$ or K), is given a symbol $\alpha$ (alpha). It can be found as
  
    $$ \alpha = \frac{\frac{\Delta l}{l_1}}{\Delta T} $$

    $$ \alpha = \frac{\Delta l}{l_1 \Delta T} \ \text{(formula for F.l.E)} $$

    - $\alpha$ is also known as **Coefficient of Linear Expansion**.
    
    - To find $\Delta l$, we say:
    
        $$ \alpha = \frac{l_{\Delta}}{ l_1 \Delta T }$$
  
  $$ \Delta l = \alpha l_{\Delta} \Delta T $$

### Surface (Area) Expansion of Solids (2D)

- As a solid expands, it causes an increase in temperature and length and width from $A_0$ to $A$.

**mathematically** $\Delta A = A_H - A_C$

If Fractional Increase in Surface area is $\beta$ (beta):

$$\beta = \Delta A / A_C T_\Delta$$

**Relation between $\beta$ and $\alpha$**

- $\beta = \Delta A / A_CT_\Delta$
- $\Delta A = A_C \beta T_\Delta$
- $A_H - A_C = A_C \beta T_\Delta$
- Solving for $A_H$,
- $A_H = A_C \beta T_\Delta + A_C$

$$A_H = A_C (1 + \beta T_\Delta)$$

So, lets Bring the linear expansion equation to make it proportional with this,

Let:

$A_H = l_H^2$

$l_H = l_C (1 + \alpha \Delta T)$

$A_H = l_h^2=l_c^2 (1 + \alpha^2 \Delta T^2)$

$A_H = l_h^2 = l_C^2 (1 + 2 \alpha \Delta T + \alpha^2 \Delta T^2)$ 

Since $\alpha^2 \Delta T^2$ is almost zero,

$A_H = l_h^2 = l_C^2 (1 + 2 \alpha \Delta T)$


$A_C = l_C^2 (Substituting)$


$A_H = A_C (1 + 2\alpha \Delta T)$

Since:

$2\alpha \Delta T = \beta \Delta T$

So,

$\beta = 2 \alpha$



$\therefore A_H = A_C (1 +  \beta \Delta T)$
