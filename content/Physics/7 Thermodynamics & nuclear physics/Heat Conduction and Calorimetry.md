## the concept of heat transfer

**Heat and temperature** are two different quantities.

- Heat is the measure of thermal change and never a property possessed by an object.
- While temperature is a fundamental physical quantity that measure the average kinetic energy of a substance.

In general, when two objects of different temperature are in **thermal contact** heat will flow from the hotter object to the cold one, this spontaneous transfer of heat from a hot body to a cold one is called **Heat transfer**.
- Since heat is energy, its SI-unit is joules(J).
- Eventually two objects in a thermal contact will reach equal temperature and no net heat transfer occurs this state is known as **thermal equilibrium**.

## Heat and Internal Energy

Heat is not something that can be stored in a body, there is no such thing as **stored heat energy**. heat is identified and detected when it crosses boundaries.

When an object is heated its temperature rises, since the average kinetic energy of its particles increases meaning the energy stored in the object increases this energy stored in an object is known as the **Internal energy**.

- **The Internal energy** is equal to the sum of the **kinetic energy** due to molecular motion and internal **potential energy** due to molecular attractive forces or bonds.

Heat and work are two Different ways of transferring energy from one system to another.

- heat is the transfer **thermal energy** between two systems.
- Work is the transfer of **Mechanical energy** between two systems.

If we have a gas in a movable piston and heat is being supplied to the gases, then:
- The temperature of the gases increases increasing the internal energy
- and some of the energy is used by the gases to move the piston.
- Thus heat supplied is converted partly into **internal energy** and partly into **work done**.

## Heat transfer mechanisms

As we have discussed earlier heat is always transferred from a hot object to a cold one until thermal equilibrium is reached.

Thermal energy can be transferred from a hotter area to a cooler area through one of the following mechanisms:

- **conduction**
- **convection**
- **radiation**
![[Pasted image 20250713102237.png]]
### **Conduction**

The transfer of heat from one body to another through the collision of neighboring particles is known as **Conduction**.

- During conduction no actual drift or movement of molecules in the direction of heat flow is occurring rather the molecules will vibrate in a fixed position.
- Heat conduction mostly occurs in **solids** as they have free electrons and positionally fixed molecules.

Materials such as metals that conduct heat well are called **Good conductors**, while materials that are a poor conductor of heat are known as **insulators**.
- **Good insulators** are **bad conductors** and vice versa.
- Insulators do not stop the flow of heat they rather slow the transfer considerably

### **Convection**

The transfer of heat as a result of currents of moving gases or fluids is known as **Heat convection**.
- heat convection mostly occurs in gases and liquids and not solids as convection requires the movement of the actual molecules rather than vibrating in a fixed position.

### **Radiation**

The transfer of heat between two bodies as a result of electromagnetic radiations such as light is known as **Radiation**.
- The heat transferred by radiations is known as **radiant heat**.
- Radiant heat doesn't require any medium to carry it unlike the other two form of heat transfer.

## Heat capacity and specific heat capacity

### **Heat capacity**

The amount of heat supplied to change the temperature of a substance by $1 \degree C$ or $1K$ is known as **Heat capacity**.

mathematically,$$ C = \frac{Q}{\Delta T} $$

where $Q$ is the heat transferred to the object, $C$ is the heat capacity of the object in $J/ \degree C$ or $J/K$ and $\Delta T$ is the change in temperature of the object.

Heat capacity helps us to differentiate objects that heat up or cool down fast and the higher the heat capacity the more heat energy it requires to change the temperature of that object.

Take a 10kg water and a 100kg water, 100kg water requires more heat energy to change the temperature than the 10kg water as a result the heat capacity of the 100kg water is much larger than the 10kg water thus **the heat capacity of an object not only depends on the property of that object but also on its mass**, thus even within the same substances there is a difference in heat quantity that comes as a result of mass difference, therefore, we have to define a quantity that depends only on the property of the object without including the mass, that is where **specific heat capacity** comes into play.

### **Specific heat capacity**

**Specific heat capacity(c)** is the amount of heat energy absorbed or released to change the temperature of a 1kg substance by $1K$ or $1 \degree C$.

Mathematically, $$ c = \frac{C}{m} = \frac{Q}{m \Delta T}$$

- the larger the change in temperature the more energy is needed.

- the greater the mass the more energy is needed.

- the higher the specific heat capacity the more energy is needed.

**Specific heat capacity** is the heat capacity without the mass dependency and only the property of the object.

- metals mostly have a lower specific heat while water have a higher specific heat than most substances.

- a good insulator has a higher specific heat as it absorbs more heat before it heats up.

## *Thermal Expansion*

*Thermal Expansion* is the change in size of matter in response to changes in its temperature.

- Except most cases, most objects expand when heated and contract when cooled.
- and it is also affected by the property of the substance - like in solids and liquids, and gases.
- Gases expand much more than liquids or solids.
- This happens due to the intermolecular forces of attraction is very strong in solids, intermediate in liquids and very weak in gases.
- It can be classified into three, based on their dimensions: 
	- **linear expansion (lengthwise)**,
	- **surface (area) expansion**, and 
	- **volume expansion**.

### 1. Linear Expansion

- Changes in length along one dimension (length) due to changes in temperature.
- Suppose an object has an initial length $L_0$ at some initial temperature $T_0$. Let the length change to $L$ when the temperature changes to $T$.
- If $\Delta T$ is too large than the $\Delta L$ also grows proportionally bigger.

$$\Delta L = L_0 \alpha \Delta T \quad \text{or} \quad L = L_0 (1 + \alpha \Delta T)$$

where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of **thermal expansion** with a unit per degree Celsius or per Kelvin and is called fractional increase in length.

### 2. Area Expansion

- It is the change in the area along two dimensions due to changes in temperature.
- Suppose an object has an initial area of $A_0$ at some initial temperature $T_0$. Let the area change to $A$ when the temperature changes to $T$.

$$\Delta A = A_0 \beta \Delta T$$

where $\beta$ is the **fractional change** in area over the change in temperature $\left(\frac{\Delta A}{A} {\Delta T}\right)$ and is called the coefficient of **surface (area) expansion**.

## Change of Phase

- Most substances can exist in **different states**.
  - Example: Water can exist in **3** states $\rightarrow$ like in solids (Ice), liquid (Water), gas (Vapor).

- All these phases arise bc of different Temperature.
- Phases of matter are interchangeable matter can change from one state to another by adding or taking heat from an object. 
- When matter is supplied with heat, it obtains changes in its forms of **internal energy** or temperature.

- At phase changes the heat supplied, is used to change the molecular structures to break the bond between molecules. Keeping, the temperature constant. 
- The transition from one phase to another is called phase change or transition. For instance, solid to liquid (Melting) or liquid to gas (Evaporation).

### Latent Heat

- It is the energy absorbed or released by a substance during a change in its physical state (Phase), that occurs without changing its temperature.
- The amount of heat is given by:

$$
Q = mL
$$ 

where $L$ is the latent heat and $L = L_f$ (fusion) or $L_v$ (evaporation)

- The SI unit of *L* is $J \backslash kg$.
- The word *latent* means hidden.
- The reason that is called *latent heat* is that it cannot be measured by a thermometer.
- The energy involved in the phase change is used to break bonds, and hence there is no increase in the KE of the particles (i.e. no temperature).
- At a given pressure, any phase change undertakes at a specific temperature.

#### Latent Heat of Fusion $(L_f)$

- Is defined as the amount of heat absorbed by 1kg of a substance to change its state from solid to a liquid, at a constant temperature.
- The point at which fusion takes place is called the melting point.
- Since fusion (solidification/freezing) and the melt are opposites of each other, they are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign.

#### Latent Heat of Vaporization $(L_v)$

- Is the amount of heat transferred by 1kg of a substance to change its state from liquid to a gas, at a constant temperature.
- The point at which vaporization is called the boiling point.

## Phase Diagram

- It is a graphical representation of the physical state of a substance under different conditions of temperature and pressure.
- There are three major curves for boiling, freezing, or melting, and sublimation in this phase diagram.
![[Pasted image 20240915073517.png|400]]


#### The Triple Point

- It is the point where the three lines on the pressure-temperature graph converge.
- It is the place where liquid, solid, and vapor coexists.
- Sublimation is the term for a phase change that proceeds directly from the solid phase to the vapor phase.

#### Critical Point (or Critical State)

- The critical point occurs where the critical temperature and critical pressure of a substance meet.
- Above this point, clear phase boundaries cease to exist.
- Beyond the temperature of critical points, the merged single phase is known as supercritical fluid.
- Critical point occurs only at a given specific temperature and pressure.

## Calorimetry

- It is the **measure** of the **quantity of heat exchanged** B/n substance.
- The approach of measuring **heat capacity** and **heat changes** during chemical and physical processes is called _calorimetry_.
- A **calorimeter** is a device used to measure _specific heat capacity_ and _specific latent heat_ of solids or liquids.

### Measuring Specific Heat Capacity

- There are several variables we can use to determine the **SHC** of a substance.
- But to calculate its value we will need to recall,
  - The mass of the substance.
  - The amount of heat energy supplied (required).
  - Its starting temperature.
  - And final temperature.

- All of the ways used to measure SHC involve **heat exchange**.
- This is done by heating one material and bringing it in thermal contact with a relatively colder body, so that they exchange heat.
- In an **isolated system**, the heat lost by the hotter body is always equal to the heat gained by the cooler body.
$$
Q_{lost} = Q_{gained}
$$
