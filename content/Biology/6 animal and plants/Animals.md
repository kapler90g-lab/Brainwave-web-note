## 1. **Characteristics of animals**

- Animals are *eukaryotic*, *multicellular*, **heterotrophic**, and sensitive to *stimuli*.
- They reproduce, **respire**, protect **themselves**, move, **excrete**, and have different **body symmetry**.
- Animals can be categorized into two groups based on the presence or absence of a **backbone**:
  - **Vertebrates** (with backbone)
  - **Invertebrates** (without backbone)

- Vertebrates are further classified into two: 
	- **homeothermic** (warm-blooded) and 
	- **poikilothermic** (*cold-blooded*).

- Invertebrates may also be classified into *Porifera*, *Platyhelminthes*, *Cnidaria*, *Arthropoda*, *Annelida*, *Echinodermata*, *Mollusca*.

## 2. **Invertebrates and Vertebrates**

### **Invertebrate animals**

- are *animals* that do not have the backbone or vertical column.
- They are the most diverse of all animals (about **99%**).
- Many invertebrates are **soft-bodied**, however, some of them have an *external* skeleton known as **exoskeleton**, 
	- usually made up of **chitin**, which protects their soft inner bodies.
- Invertebrates are *cold-blooded*, and hence, they don't regulate their body temp.

### **Vertebrate Animals**

- The most **advanced** group of all animal kingdom.
- They possess a well-defined internal **skeleton**, with cartilage and backbone Separated into an 
	- **axial skeleton** (skull, ribs, backbone, sternum), and
	- **appendicular skeleton** (girdles and appendages).
- The **skull** and **vertebral column (backbone)** protect the brain and **nerve cord** respectively.
- They also consist of systems like **circulatory system**, **respiratory system**, **nervous system**, and **excretory system**.

- **Circulatory System** is a closed circulatory system, with a ventral heart having 2 to 4 chambers, and a median dorsal aorta.
- The **respiratory system** consists of either gills or lungs.
  - **Nervous System** with a brain and sensory *organs*.
  - **Excretory System** consists of paired kidneys.
  - They have bilaterally symmetrical.

- All vertebrates are **Chordates** (animals in phylum Chordata having a backbone). But not all Chordates are vertebrates.
---

- ### **Why Are All Vertebrates Chordates, but Not All Chordates Vertebrates?**

The distinction lies in the defining characteristics of the phylum _Chordata_ and the subphylum _Vertebrata_ (vertebrates) within it.

---

### **Chordates: Overview and Defining Features**

All animals in the phylum _Chordata_ share four key characteristics at some point during their life cycle:

1. **Notochord**: A flexible, rod-like structure that provides support. In many chordates, it is replaced by a vertebral column (backbone) during development, but it remains throughout life in others.
2. **Dorsal Hollow Nerve Cord**: A tube of nerve tissue located dorsally (above the notochord). This nerve cord develops into the brain and spinal cord in vertebrates.
3. **Pharyngeal Slits**: Openings in the throat region used for filter-feeding, respiration (gills), or other functions, depending on the species.
4. **Post-Anal Tail**: A tail extending beyond the anus, present in all chordates at some developmental stage.

These features make an animal a chordate, but they do not necessarily imply the presence of a backbone.

---

### **Vertebrates: A Subgroup of Chordates**

Vertebrates are a more specialized group within the chordates and belong to the subphylum _Vertebrata_. They possess all the defining features of chordates but with additional adaptations:

- **Vertebral Column**: In vertebrates, the notochord is replaced by a series of interlocking bones or cartilage segments (vertebrae) that protect the spinal cord.
- **Cranium (Skull)**: A bony or cartilaginous structure that encloses the brain, providing protection.
- **Complex Organ Systems**: Vertebrates have highly developed organ systems, including a closed circulatory system, advanced nervous system, and specialized sensory organs.

Because vertebrates inherit all the defining characteristics of chordates and add these specialized features, they are considered a subgroup of chordates.

---

### **Non-Vertebrate Chordates**

Not all chordates develop a backbone. Two subphyla of _Chordata_ lack a vertebral column but still exhibit the four key chordate features:

1. **Urochordates (Tunicates)**:
    
    - Examples: Sea squirts.
    - The larval stage possesses the chordate features (e.g., notochord, dorsal nerve cord), but in adults, these features are greatly reduced or absent.
2. **Cephalochordates (Lancelets)**:
    
    - Examples: Amphioxus.
    - These retain the notochord throughout life but do not develop a vertebral column.

These groups are chordates because they possess the defining features at some stage of their development, but they lack the advanced structures like a backbone and skull that define vertebrates.

---

### **Summary**

- **All vertebrates are chordates** because they exhibit the four hallmark features of chordates during development.
- **Not all chordates are vertebrates** because some, like tunicates and lancelets, do not develop a backbone or other vertebrate-specific adaptations.

This hierarchical relationship highlights the evolutionary progression from simple chordates to more complex vertebrates.


## 2.3 **Reproduction In Animals**

**Reproduction** is the process by which *living things duplicate themselves*.

- There are two ways of reproduction in animals: **asexual** and **sexual reproduction**.
- Most animals go **sexual reproduction** and have similar forms of development.

### **Asexual reproduction in animals**

- You recall that **asexual reproduction** is a type of *reproduction* that requires only a single *parent* and doesn't require *fusion of gametes* from two parents.
- It is more common in *invertebrates* than *vertebrates*.
- **Budding and Fragmentation** are the two most common types of reproduction in *algae* and most commonly in *aquatic animals*.
- The other form of **Asexual reproduction in animals** is **parthenogenesis**. 
- It is a type of *reproduction* in which *unfertilized eggs develop into a new offspring*.
- - ### **Forms of Asexual Reproduction**

1. **Budding**
    
    - **Definition**: A form of asexual reproduction where a new organism develops from an outgrowth or bud on the parent organism.
    - **Process**: The bud forms due to localized cell division and detaches when mature, forming an independent organism.
    - **Examples**:
        - **Hydra**: Buds grow on the parent and eventually detach.
        - **Yeast**: Budding occurs in unicellular fungi.
2. **Fragmentation**
    
    - **Definition**: A form of asexual reproduction in which an organism splits into fragments, each capable of growing into a complete organism.
    - **Process**: The parent body breaks into pieces, and each fragment regenerates the missing parts.
    - **Examples**:
        - **Starfish**: Can regenerate a whole body from a single arm.
        - **Planaria**: A flatworm that regenerates a complete organism from body parts.
3. **Parthenogenesis**
    
    - **Definition**: A form of reproduction where an embryo develops from an unfertilized egg.
    - **Process**: No male gamete is involved, and the offspring are genetically identical to the mother.
    - **Examples**:
        - **Komodo Dragons**: Females can produce offspring without male fertilization.
        - **Bees**: Worker bees and drones are produced through parthenogenesis.

---

### Common Features in Budding, Fragmentation, and Parthenogenesis

- **No Need for Fertilization**: These processes do not involve the fusion of male and female gametes.
- **Clonal Offspring**: The offspring are genetically identical to the parent organism.
- **Rapid Reproduction**: Asexual reproduction enables faster reproduction compared to sexual reproduction.
- **Single Parent Involvement**: Only one parent organism is needed for reproduction.
- **Adaptation to Stable Environments**: These methods are efficient in environments with little change where variation is less critical.

### **Sexual Reproduction in animals**

- You recall that **sexual reproduction** is a type of *reproduction* that requires fusion of *gametes* from two parents, (male and female).
- The parents are **diploid** (meaning have 46 chromosome with a complete set of chromosomes (2n)).
- It involves **male** and **female reproductive structure** with *different functions*.
- One of the two most important functions is the production of **haploid cells called Gametes (n)** for the transmission of *genetic info from parents to offspring*.
- In **sexual reproduction males produce sperms and haploid cells (_n)_ in the testis known as sperm**.
- Cells are stored in the *epididymis* until *ejaculation*.
- On the other hand, *females produce an ovum or egg (haploid cell (n)) that matures in the ovary*.
- The *fusion* of **sperm cells with female gametes produces a zygote** through the process of ***fertilization***.
- There are two types of **fertilization**, **Internal and External**.
### **Types of Fertilization**

Fertilization, the union of male and female gametes to form a zygote, occurs in two primary forms: **external fertilization** and **internal fertilization**. Each has unique mechanisms, advantages, and challenges.

---

### **1. External Fertilization**

#### **Definition**

- Fertilization occurs **outside the body** of the female, typically in aquatic environments.

#### **Process**

1. Male and female gametes are released into the surrounding environment, usually water.
2. Sperm swims towards the eggs for fertilization.

#### **Examples**

- **Amphibians**: Frogs release eggs and sperm into the water.
- **Fish**: Salmon and other species use this method.

#### **Advantages**

- Large numbers of gametes can be produced, increasing the chances of successful fertilization.
- Offspring are widely dispersed, reducing competition.

#### **Disadvantages**

- High wastage of gametes as many are not fertilized.
- Vulnerability to predators and environmental conditions.

#### **Types of External Fertilization**

- **Broadcast Spawning**: Both gametes are released into the open water (e.g., coral).
- **Pair Spawning**: Male and female release gametes in proximity for higher success (e.g., some fish species).

---

### **2. Internal Fertilization**

#### **Definition**

- Fertilization occurs **inside the female body**.

#### **Process**

1. Male deposits sperm inside the female reproductive tract during mating.
2. Sperm travels to meet the egg for fertilization.

#### **Examples**

- **Mammals**: Humans, dogs, and whales.
- **Reptiles and Birds**: Internal fertilization followed by egg-laying or live birth.

#### **Advantages**

- Higher chances of fertilization due to a controlled environment.
- Protection of gametes and zygote from predators and environmental factors.

#### **Disadvantages**

- Requires more energy and complex reproductive structures.
- Fewer offspring are typically produced.

#### **Types of Internal Fertilization**

#### **Oviparity, Ovoviviparity, and Viviparity**

These terms refer to modes of reproduction in animals and describe how embryos develop and are delivered.

## **1. Oviparity**

### **Meaning**

- **Definition**: Oviparity is a mode of reproduction where animals lay eggs that develop and hatch outside the mother's body.
- **Etymology**:
    - _Ovi-_ (Latin: egg)
    - _-parity_ (Latin: to bring forth)  
        Together, it means "egg-laying."

### **Process**

1. **Fertilization**:
    - Can occur internally (e.g., in birds) or externally (e.g., in most fish and amphibians).
2. **Egg Development**:
    - Embryos develop inside the egg, relying on yolk as the sole source of nutrients.
3. **Hatching**:
    - The young emerge from the egg after completing development.

### **Examples**

- Birds (e.g., chickens), reptiles (e.g., turtles), amphibians (e.g., frogs), and many insects.

## **2. Ovoviviparity**

### **Meaning**

- **Definition**: Ovoviviparity is a reproductive mode where eggs develop inside the mother's body but hatch internally or immediately after being laid. The embryo relies on the yolk for nourishment.
- **Etymology**:
    - _Ovo-_ (Latin: egg)
    - _Vivi-_ (Latin: alive)
    - _-parity_ (Latin: to bring forth)  
        Together, it means "egg-live-bearing."

### **Process**

1. **Fertilization**:
    - Always internal.
2. **Egg Development**:
    - Embryos develop within eggs retained inside the mother.
    - Nutrients come solely from the yolk.
3. **Birth**:
    - Eggs may hatch inside the mother, with live young being "born," or may be laid shortly before hatching.

### **Examples**

- Some sharks (e.g., great white sharks), snakes (e.g., garter snakes), and certain fish (e.g., guppies).

## **3. Viviparity**

### **Meaning**

- **Definition**: Viviparity is a mode of reproduction where the embryo develops inside the mother's body and receives nourishment directly from her (e.g., via the placenta in mammals).
- **Etymology**:
    - _Vivi-_ (Latin: alive)
    - _-parity_ (Latin: to bring forth)  
        Together, it means "live-bearing."

### **Process**

1. **Fertilization**:
    - Always internal.
2. **Embryo Development**:
    - Embryos are nourished through a direct connection with the mother, such as a placenta.
3. **Birth**:
    - Young are born fully developed and live.

### **Examples**

- Most mammals (e.g., humans, dogs) and some reptiles (e.g., some vipers).

---

---

## **Summary**

- **Oviparity** involves laying eggs that hatch outside the mother.
- **Ovoviviparity** combines features of oviparity and viviparity, with eggs hatching inside or just before birth.
- **Viviparity** involves live births, with embryos nourished directly by the mother.

These reproductive strategies reflect adaptations to different environments and evolutionary pressures.


### **Similarities Between External and Internal Fertilization**

- Both involve the union of male and female gametes.
- Both result in the formation of a zygote that develops into an offspring.
- Genetic material is combined to ensure variation in offspring.

|**Feature**|**External Fertilization**|**Internal Fertilization**|
|---|---|---|
|**Location**|Outside the female body|Inside the female body|
|**Environment**|Requires water or moist environment|Can occur in land or aquatic environments|

### **Conclusion**

External and internal fertilization are adapted to different environmental and biological contexts. While external fertilization is common in aquatic animals and ensures widespread offspring dispersal, internal fertilization provides greater protection and higher survival rates for developing embryos. Both methods contribute to the diversity of reproductive strategies in nature.

### Stages of Embryonic Development

Embryonic development begins after fertilization and progresses through several well-defined stages. These stages lead to the formation of organs and tissues derived from germ layers. Below is a detailed explanation:
### **2. Cleavage**

- **Definition**: Rapid mitotic cell divisions without growth, transforming the zygote into a multicellular structure.
    
- **Process**:
    
    1. Zygote divides into 2, 4, 8, and 16 cells, forming a **morula** (solid ball of cells).
    2. Morula transitions into a **blastocyst** (hollow structure with a fluid-filled cavity).
- **Key Structures in Blastocyst**:
    
    - **Trophoblast**: Outer layer, later forming the placenta.
    - **Inner Cell Mass (ICM)**: Forms the embryo.
### **3. Gastrulation**

- **Definition**: A process where the blastocyst reorganizes into a structure with three germ layers.
    
- **Key Event**: Formation of the **gastrula**, which consists of:
    
    1. **Ectoderm** (outer layer).
    2. **Mesoderm** (middle layer).
    3. **Endoderm** (inner layer).
- **Importance**: Establishes the body’s basic layout and sets the stage for organ development.
### **4. Neurulation**

- **Definition**: Formation of the neural tube from the ectoderm.
    
- **Process**:
    
    1. The **notochord** (from the mesoderm) signals the ectoderm to fold.
    2. The ectoderm forms the **neural tube**, which becomes the brain and spinal cord.
- **Outcome**: Early formation of the central nervous system (CNS).
### **5. Organogenesis**

- **Definition**: Development of organs from the three germ layers.
- **Process**:
    1. Cells in the germ layers differentiate into specialized cells and tissues.
    2. Tissues form specific organs and systems.

### **Germ Layers and Their Derivatives**

The three germ layers each give rise to distinct organs and systems:

#### **1. Ectoderm** (Outer Layer)

- **Key Derivatives**:
    - **Nervous System**: Brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves.
    - **Skin**: Epidermis, hair, nails, sweat glands.
    - **Sensory Organs**: Eyes (lens and retina), inner ear.

#### **2. Mesoderm** (Middle Layer)

- **Key Derivatives**:
    - **Skeletal System**: Bones, cartilage.
    - **Muscular System**: Skeletal, smooth, and cardiac muscles.
    - **Circulatory System**: Heart, blood vessels, blood.
    - **Excretory System**: Kidneys, ureters.
    - **Reproductive System**: Gonads (testes and ovaries).

#### **3. Endoderm** (Inner Layer)

- **Key Derivatives**:
    - **Digestive System**: Stomach, intestines, liver, pancreas.
    - **Respiratory System**: Lungs, trachea.
    - **Urinary System**: Bladder.
    - **Endocrine Glands**: Thyroid, parathyroid.

---

### **Progression from Zygote to Organ Formation**

|**Stage**|**Description**|**Transition to Next Stage**|
|---|---|---|
|**Zygote**|Single-celled fertilized egg.|Cleavage begins, forming the morula.|
|**Morula**|Solid ball of cells.|Hollowing out to become the blastocyst.|
|**Blastocyst**|Hollow structure; implantation in uterus occurs.|Gastrulation establishes germ layers.|
|**Gastrula**|Three germ layers form.|Neurulation initiates neural tube formation.|
|**Neurula**|Neural tube and primitive structures develop.|Organogenesis begins with tissue differentiation.|

---

### **Summary of Development**

1. **Zygote** divides through cleavage to form a blastocyst.
2. **Gastrulation** establishes the germ layers.
3. **Neurulation** forms the neural tube.
4. **Organogenesis** differentiates the germ layers into organs and systems.

**Organogenesis** is the formation of organs during *embryonic development*.

# Reproduction in Invertebrates

## **Reproduction In Insects**

- Insects are the *largest* groups of **Phylum Arthropoda**.
- They have **jointed** legs, *segmented* bodies, and **exoskeletons**.
- They include **bees, wasps, beetles**.
- They follow **sexual reproduction**, and have their *own* life **cycle**.
- During **sexual reproduction**, eggs are *usually* fertilized **internally**.
- But some insects *undergo* **parthenogenesis**.
- In **sexual reproduction**, the male produces the **sperm** and the female produces the **eggs** during *mating*.
- After fertilization, the female *hatches* them after completing their *development*.
- After hatching, insects undergo **stages** of body changes. This changes are *known as* **[[metamorphosis]]**.
- There are two types of **metamorphosis**: **complete** and **incomplete** metamorphosis.
  - **Complete metamorphosis** has four stages while **incomplete metamorphosis** has three stages.
  - For example, the **complete metamorphosis** in honeybees is:
    - **egg** > *larva* > *pupa* > **adult**
  - In **grasshoppers**, the **incomplete metamorphosis** is:
    - **egg** > *nymph* > **adult**

### **Differences and Similarities Between Complete and Incomplete Metamorphosis**

Metamorphosis refers to the developmental changes that certain animals, particularly insects, undergo to transform from immature forms to adults. There are **two main types of metamorphosis: complete and incomplete.**

---

### **1. Complete Metamorphosis**

#### **Definition**

Complete metamorphosis involves **four distinct stages**:

1. **Egg**: Fertilized egg laid by the adult.
2. **Larva**: An immature, worm-like stage focused on feeding (e.g., caterpillar).
3. **Pupa**: A resting stage where major body restructuring occurs (e.g., cocoon or chrysalis).
4. **Adult**: The fully developed, sexually mature stage (e.g., butterfly).

#### **Examples**

- Butterflies, bees, beetles, and flies.

#### **Characteristics**

- Significant structural changes between stages.
- The larva has a completely different appearance and diet than the adult.
- The pupa is a dormant stage with internal transformation.

---

### **2. Incomplete Metamorphosis**

#### **Definition**

Incomplete metamorphosis involves **three distinct stages**:

1. **Egg**: Fertilized egg laid by the adult.
2. **Nymph**: An immature stage resembling a smaller version of the adult but lacking functional wings and reproductive structures.
3. **Adult**: Fully grown, sexually mature, and often winged stage.

#### **Examples**

- Grasshoppers, cockroaches, and dragonflies.

#### **Characteristics**

- Gradual changes with no dramatic transformation.
- Nymphs often share the adult’s habitat and diet.
- No pupal stage.

---

### **Key Differences**

|**Feature**|**Complete Metamorphosis**|**Incomplete Metamorphosis**|
|---|---|---|
|**Stages**|Four: Egg → Larva → Pupa → Adult|Three: Egg → Nymph → Adult|
|**Transformation**|Dramatic changes, including a pupal stage.|Gradual changes, no pupal stage.|
|**Appearance of Juveniles**|Larva looks entirely different from adult.|Nymph resembles a smaller adult.|
|**Habitat and Diet**|Larvae and adults often occupy different niches.|Nymphs and adults share similar habitats and diets.|
|**Energy Use**|Pupal stage consumes stored energy for transformation.|Energy is used consistently for growth.|

---

### **Key Similarities**

1. **Egg Stage**: Both types begin with an egg laid by the adult insect.
2. **Growth**: Both processes involve stages of development before reaching adulthood.
3. **Molting**: Insects in both types shed their exoskeletons as they grow.
4. **Purpose**: Both ensure survival by adapting the juvenile and adult stages for different roles, such as feeding, reproduction, or dispersal.

---

### **Summary**

- **Complete metamorphosis** involves a drastic transformation with distinct larval, pupal, and adult stages.
- **Incomplete metamorphosis** has a gradual development where the nymph grows directly into the adult without a pupal stage.  
    Despite these differences, both forms of metamorphosis help insects adapt to their environments and optimize survival.

### **Chemical Control of Metamorphosis**

Metamorphosis in insects is tightly regulated by **hormones**. The two key chemicals involved are **juvenile hormone (JH)** and **ecdysone**. These hormones coordinate the developmental transitions during an insect's life cycle, ensuring that each stage occurs at the appropriate time.

---

### **Key Hormones**

#### **1. Juvenile Hormone (JH)**

- **Produced by**: Corpora allata (glands near the insect brain).
- **Role**:
    - Maintains juvenile characteristics during development.
    - High levels of JH prevent the transition to the next stage of metamorphosis.
    - Declining levels of JH signal the organism to transition to a more mature stage (e.g., larva to pupa or nymph to adult).

#### **2. Ecdysone (Molting Hormone)**

- **Produced by**: Prothoracic gland.
- **Role**:
    - Triggers **molting** (the shedding of the exoskeleton) and promotes growth.
    - Works alongside JH to determine the nature of the molt.
        - **High JH + Ecdysone**: Molt into another larval or nymphal stage.
        - **Low JH + Ecdysone**: Molt into the next major stage (pupa or adult).

---

### **How These Hormones Control Metamorphosis**

1. **Larval/Nymphal Stages**:
    
    - High levels of JH ensure the insect remains in the immature stage.
    - Ecdysone induces molting to allow the insect to grow.
2. **Pupal Stage (Complete Metamorphosis)**:
    
    - As JH levels drop, ecdysone directs the insect to molt into the pupal stage.
    - The pupal stage is a critical transformation phase where adult structures form.
3. **Adult Stage**:
    
    - When JH levels are negligible, ecdysone promotes the final molt into the adult stage.
    - The insect becomes sexually mature and stops growing.

---

### **Interaction of Hormones**

|**Hormone Level**|**Effect**|**Stage**|
|---|---|---|
|High JH + Ecdysone|Maintains juvenile characteristics|Larval/Nymphal stages|
|Low JH + Ecdysone|Triggers transformation to pupa/adult|Pupal stage or Adult stage|
|No JH + Ecdysone|Final transition to adult|Adult stage (metamorphosis ends)|

---

### **Significance**

- Hormonal regulation ensures proper timing and coordination of growth and development.
- Disruption of these hormones (e.g., by environmental factors or insecticides) can halt development or lead to malformed adults.

## **Reproduction in Vertebrates**

### **Reproduction In Frog**
- ### **Frog Evolution and Position in the Animal Kingdom**

#### **Position in the Animal Kingdom**:

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata (presence of a notochord and dorsal nerve cord)
- **Class**: Amphibia (adapted to both aquatic and terrestrial environments)
- **Order**: Anura (frogs and toads, characterized by the absence of a tail in adults)
- **Family**: Various (e.g., Ranidae for "true frogs").

#### **Evolution**:

Frogs evolved from lobe-finned fish through transitional species that adapted to living on land. Over time, amphibians developed:

1. **lungs**: For breathing air in terrestrial environments.
2. **Limbs**: For locomotion on land.
3. **Moist skin**: For respiration in aquatic and damp environments.
4. **Metamorphosis**: A life cycle that bridges aquatic larval stages and terrestrial adults.
### **Reproductive Structures and Functions**

The image shows **male** and **female frog reproductive systems**:

#### **Male Reproductive Structures**:

- **Testis**: Produces sperm (male gametes).
- **Vasa efferentia**: Transports sperm from the testis to the cloaca.
- **Cloaca**: A common chamber for the release of gametes and excretory products.

#### **Female Reproductive Structures**:

- **Ovary**: Produces eggs (female gametes).
- **Oviduct**: Transports eggs to the cloaca.
- **Cloaca**: Releases eggs and excretory products.
- Frogs go *via* **sexual modes** of reproduction.
- Unlike birds, frogs do not produce **amniotic** (a hard cover) eggs.
- Rather, they are covered in a *jelly-like* substance.
- In which both the frogs *release* their **gametes** in the *water*.
- Usually, frogs lay a *lot* of **eggs** in one place at the same time, and by *metamorphosis*, they grow into the **larval stage** known as **tadpole**.

### **Fertilization and Egg Development**

#### **Fertilization**:

- **Type**: External fertilization.
- **Process**:
    - Males mount females (amplexus) and release sperm over the eggs as the female lays them in water.
    - Sperm fertilizes the eggs outside the female’s body.
    - In almost all frogs, egg fertilization happens outside the female's body instead of inside. The female releases her eggs and the male releases his sperm at the same time. In order to make sure that the sperm reach the eggs, the male and female get into a mating posture called **amplexus**. The male climbs onto the female's back and clasps his forelegs around her middle. Frogs can stay in amplexus for hours or even days as the female releases as few as one or as many as several hundred eggs.
### **Developmental Stages of Frogs**

1. **Zygote**:
    
    - The fertilized egg divides by mitosis to form a blastula.
2. **Embryo**:
    
    - Cells differentiate and gastrulation forms three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm).
3. **Tadpole (Larva)**:
    
    - Fully aquatic stage with a tail, gills, and no limbs.
    - Feeds on algae and uses gills for respiration.
4. **Metamorphosis**:
    
    - **Processes**:
        - Development of limbs.
        - Resorption of the tail.
        - Transition from gills to lungs for breathing air.
    - **Hormonal Control**: Thyroxine (produced by the thyroid gland) drives metamorphosis.

### 1. **Reproductive Structures and Fertilization**

- **Frogs**: Frogs exhibit **external fertilization**, where the male releases sperm over the female’s eggs as they are laid in water. ## What is Behavior?

### Definition

Behavior refers to the actions or reactions of an organism, usually in response to external or internal stimuli. It encompasses all observable activities of living beings, including their interactions with the environment and other organisms.

### Characteristics of Behavior

1. **Stimulus-Response Relationship**: Behavior is typically triggered by a specific stimulus, either external (e.g., light, temperature, sound) or internal (e.g., hunger, hormonal changes).
    
2. **Dynamic Nature**: Behavior can change over time due to learning, development, or environmental factors.
    
3. **Adaptation**: Many behaviors evolve as adaptations to improve survival and reproductive success.
    
4. **Conscious or Unconscious**: Behaviors can be deliberate (e.g., problem-solving) or automatic (e.g., reflex actions).
    

### Types of Behavior

- **Innate Behavior**: Inherited and instinctive actions, such as reflexes.
    
- **Learned Behavior**: Actions acquired through experience, such as solving a maze.

## **Learned or acquired behavior**

### **Classical Conditioning**
- Is a result of **associative learning**.
- In which a **response** associated with one **stimulus** is associated with the *second stimulus*, to which it had no *previous connection*.
- It was discovered by **Ivan Pavlov (1904 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine).**

- **Pavlov’s Dog Experiment**:
- **Setup**: Pavlov paired the sound of a bell (neutral stimulus) with the presentation of food (unconditioned stimulus).
- **Result**: Dogs began to salivate (conditioned response) upon hearing the bell, even in the absence of food.

- There are three stages of **classical conditioning**.

##### **Stage 1: Before Conditioning**
- This stage states that an **unconditional stimulus (UCS)** produces **unconditional response (UCR)** meaning the creation of a response or stimulus which is *unlearned* (i.e. *unconditional*).
- There is no *new behavior* is learned yet.

##### **Stage 2: During Conditioning**
- A **stimulus** that produces no response(NS) is associated with **UCS**. Due to this, it is known as **conditioned stimulus (CS)**.
- For *learning* (i.e., learning to respond) to take place, **UCS** must be associated with **NS** many times.

##### **Stage 3: After Conditioning**
- This happens once the *conditioned(natural) stimulus (CS)* associates with **UCS** to create a new acquired **conditioned response**.

### **stages of conditioning**

### Types of Stimuli

In classical conditioning, there are **three main types of stimuli**:

1. **Neutral Stimulus (NS)**
    
    - A stimulus that does not naturally trigger a response. It’s neutral before conditioning.
    - Example: A bell sound before it is paired with food.
2. **Unconditioned Stimulus (US)**
    
    - A stimulus that naturally and automatically triggers a response without prior learning.
    - Example: Food, which naturally causes salivation in dogs.
3. **Conditioned Stimulus (CS)**
    
    - A previously neutral stimulus that, after being paired with an unconditioned stimulus, begins to trigger a conditioned response.
    - Example: The bell after it has been paired with food and begins to trigger salivation.

### Types of Responses

There are also **two main types of responses** in classical conditioning:

1. **Unconditioned Response (UR)**
    
    - The automatic, natural reaction to an unconditioned stimulus. It happens without prior learning.
    - Example: Salivation in response to food.
2. **Conditioned Response (CR)**
    
    - The learned response to a previously neutral stimulus that has become a conditioned stimulus after being paired with an unconditioned stimulus.
    - Example: Salivation in response to the bell (after conditioning).


Classical conditioning, a concept developed by Ivan Pavlov, involves learning through associations between a stimulus and a response. The process is broken down into three main stages:

### 1. **Before Conditioning (Pre-conditioning)**

- **Neutral Stimulus (NS)**: At this stage, a neutral stimulus is introduced, which does not naturally provoke a response. For example, the sound of a bell.
- **Unconditioned Stimulus (US)**: A stimulus that naturally and automatically triggers a response without prior learning. For example, food that naturally causes salivation.
- **Unconditioned Response (UR)**: The automatic response to the unconditioned stimulus, such as salivation in response to food.

**Example:** In Pavlov's experiment, the food (US) causes salivation (UR), and the bell (NS) does not trigger salivation.

### 2. **During Conditioning (Acquisition)**

- **Association of NS and US**: During this stage, the neutral stimulus (NS) is repeatedly paired with the unconditioned stimulus (US). The goal is for the neutral stimulus to begin triggering the same response as the unconditioned stimulus.

**Example:** Pavlov would ring the bell (NS) just before presenting the food (US), which leads to salivation (UR).

### 3. **After Conditioning (Post-conditioning)**

- **Conditioned Stimulus (CS)**: After several pairings, the neutral stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS). It now triggers a response even without the unconditioned stimulus.
- **Conditioned Response (CR)**: The learned response to the conditioned stimulus. It is usually similar to the unconditioned response but occurs in reaction to the conditioned stimulus.

**Example:** After conditioning, the sound of the bell (CS) alone causes salivation (CR), even without food being presented.

### Summary:

- **Before Conditioning**: NS + US → UR
- **During Conditioning**: NS + US → UR
- **After Conditioning**: CS → CR

This process highlights how behaviors can be learned through the association of stimuli.
### Differences Between the Three Stages of Classical Conditioning

Here's a breakdown of the differences between the three stages of classical conditioning:

|**Stage**|**Stimuli**|**Response**|**Key Difference**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**1. Before Conditioning**|- Neutral Stimulus (NS) - Unconditioned Stimulus (US)|- Unconditioned Response (UR) (natural, unlearned response)|The NS does not trigger a response, only the US triggers the UR.|
|**2. During Conditioning**|- Neutral Stimulus (NS) + Unconditioned Stimulus (US)|- Unconditioned Response (UR)|Repeated pairing of NS and US leads to the association forming.|
|**3. After Conditioning**|- Conditioned Stimulus (CS)|- Conditioned Response (CR)|The CS now triggers a learned response (CR) instead of the original unconditioned response (UR).|

### Summary of Key Differences:

1. **Neutral Stimulus (NS) vs. Conditioned Stimulus (CS)**:
    
    - **NS** does not produce any response before conditioning.
    - **CS** produces a learned response after it has been associated with an unconditioned stimulus (US).
2. **Unconditioned Response (UR) vs. Conditioned Response (CR)**:
    
    - **UR** is a natural, automatic response to a US.
    - **CR** is a learned response to the CS after conditioning.

In short, the transition from **NS** to **CS** and from **UR** to **CR** illustrates how behavior is learned through the pairing of stimuli in classical conditioning.




### **Operant Conditioning**
- When an organism performs a **behavior**, the organism is given a **reward** or *punishment*.
- it is an associative learning 
- - It is different from **CC** because it doesn't rely on an *existing* **stimulus-response** pair.
- **Discoverer**: B.F. Skinner, building on Edward Thorndike’s work.
- **Experiments**:
    - **Skinner Box**:
        - **Setup**: Skinner placed rats or pigeons in a box with a lever. Pressing the lever would release food (positive reinforcement) or avoid a mild shock (negative reinforcement).
        - **Result**: Animals learned to perform actions that yielded positive outcomes and avoid those with negative outcomes.
    - **Thorndike’s Law of Effect**:
        - **Setup**: Cats placed in a puzzle box learned to press a lever to escape and access food.
        - **Result**: Behaviors leading to satisfying consequences were more likely to be repeated.
- Based on the theory, behavior will likely be repeated when the organism is *rewarded* and a behavior will be less frequent when it is punished.
- **Skinner** identified three types of responses or :operant behaviors
  1. **Neutral operant**
  2. **Reinforcers**
  3. **Punishers**
  
**neutral Operant**
- It is a *response* that neither **increases nor decreases** the probability of a behavior being repeated.

**Reinforcers**
- are *responses* that **increase** the probability of the behavior being repeated.
  ~ It can be **a positive** or **negative**.

**Punishers**
- It **decreases** the likely hood of being repeated.
- Punishment weakens **behavior**

- **Insight learning** is the sudden realization of how to solve a problem, often referred to as an "aha moment" or "eureka moment." This type of learning occurs when a person or animal makes a sudden connection between previously unconnected pieces of information, leading to a solution or understanding.

### **Latent Learning**

**Latent learning** refers to learning that occurs without any obvious reinforcement or immediate demonstration of the learned behavior. This type of learning becomes apparent only when there is a reason or motivation to display it. Essentially, it’s the learning that is hidden or not immediately observable.

#### Characteristics of Latent Learning:

- **Occurs without reinforcement**: The learner does not show behavior immediately after learning.
- **No immediate reward**: The learner might not be motivated to demonstrate the learned behavior right away.
- **Delayed demonstration**: The learned behavior appears when there is a motivation or reason to express it

- In which repeated administration of a stimulus results in the progressive amplification of response.

#### **Interspecific Competition**

- **Definition**: Interspecific competition occurs when individuals of **different species** compete for the same resources (food, shelter, mates, etc.) in an ecosystem.
- **Key Characteristics**:
    - Occurs between different species.
    - The species involved may have overlapping ecological niches or similar resource requirements.
    - Can lead to **competitive exclusion** (where one species outcompetes the other) or **resource partitioning** (where species adapt to share resources without direct competition).
- **Example**: Lions and hyenas in the African savanna both hunt similar prey, leading to competition for food. While they occupy different ecological niches, they still compete for similar resources, like large herbivores.

---

#### **Intraspecific Competition**

- **Definition**: Intraspecific competition happens when individuals of the **same species** compete for the same resources within a given environment.
- **Key Characteristics**:
    - Occurs within the same species.
    - Individuals compete for resources like food, territory, mates, and shelter.
    - Often leads to **natural selection** as individuals that are better adapted to survive the competition are more likely to reproduce.
- **Example**: Two male deer fighting for access to females during the breeding season. Both are of the same species and compete for mates, which could affect their reproductive success.

# **Homeostasis in Animals**

- *Despite the environmental changes that affect the physiological function of their bodies, animals maintain a stable and constant internal bodily condition through homeostasis*
- **Homeostasis is a self-regulatory process by which animals maintain stable internal body conditions regardless of external conditions**.
- *It helps animals to maintain equilibrium in the internal condition of their bodies e.g., temp*.
- *Animals' body systems adjust to internal and external changes in order to maintain this normal condition.
  - *A change in the internal or external environment (stimulus) is detected by receptors in animals' body, which sends info to control center (Brain) as a result they respond to the stimulus*.

  - Generally homeostasis involves four components: **Stimulus**, **Receptor**, **Control Center**, **Effector**
  
- **Stimulus** - is the change in the *environment* that forces the organisms to respond.
- **Receptor** - It detects the stimulus or body condition and sends signal to **Control Center** to counteract it, restoring the body's condition to a **normal condition**.
- **Control Center** - This receives signals or messages from the *receptors* and sends commands to **Effector**. The counteract the change.
- **Effector** - is the act on the stimulus, based on the commands sent by the **Control Center**. Counteracting and restoring the internal body condition to normal.

## **Thermoregulation**

- is the process of making the internal body temp constant.
- Based on temp regulations, animals can be divided into two:
  - **Poikilothermic animals (Cold-Blooded)**
  - **Homeothermic animals (Warm-Blooded)**

### 1. **Poikilothermic animals (Cold-Blooded)**
- Also known as **ectothermic animals** or cold-blooded animals, their body temperature varies with the environmental temperature. Examples include reptiles, amphibians, and fish.

### 2. **Homeothermic animals (Warm-Blooded)**

- also called **Endothermic animals**.
- are animals that can **generate** internal heat to maintain a constant *internal body temperature regardless of the environments condition*.

Endothermic animals regulate their body temperature through **vasoconstriction, vasodilation, shivering, and sweating.** These mechanisms help maintain a stable internal temperature despite changes in environmental conditions.

---

### **1. Vasoconstriction**

- **Definition:** Narrowing of blood vessels, particularly in the skin, to reduce blood flow near the surface of the body.
- **Environment Used:** Cold environments.
- **How It Works:**
    - Reduces heat loss by minimizing blood flow to the skin, retaining heat in the body core.
    - Maintains warmth in vital organs.
- **Control Center:** Hypothalamus.
- **Effector:**
    - Smooth muscles in the walls of blood vessels contract, reducing their diameter.

---

### **2. Vasodilation**

- **Definition:** Widening of blood vessels to increase blood flow to the skin.
- **Environment Used:** Hot environments.
- **How It Works:**
    - Allows heat to dissipate through the skin by increasing blood flow near the surface.
    - Enhances heat loss through radiation.
- **Control Center:** Hypothalamus.
- **Effector:**
    - Smooth muscles in the walls of blood vessels relax, increasing their diameter.

---

### **3. Shivering**

- **Definition:** Involuntary, rapid muscle contractions that generate heat.
- **Environment Used:** Extremely cold environments.
- **How It Works:**
    - Muscle activity increases metabolic heat production.
    - Shivering burns energy (glucose or fat) to generate warmth.
- **Control Center:** Hypothalamus.
- **Effector:**
    - Skeletal muscles contract rhythmically to produce heat.

---

### **4. Sweating**

- **Definition:** Release of sweat from sweat glands to cool the body.
- **Environment Used:** Extremely hot environments.
- **How It Works:**
    - Sweat evaporates from the skin surface, removing heat and cooling the body.
    - Evaporation is most effective in dry conditions.
- **Control Center:** Hypothalamus.
- **Effector:**
    - Sweat glands in the skin increase activity, producing sweat.
### **How Sweating Cools the Body**

Sweating cools the body through the **evaporation** of water, which is tied to the **properties of water**:

#### **Steps in Cooling:**

1. **Sweat Secretion:**
    - Sweat glands release water and electrolytes onto the skin.
2. **Evaporation:**
    - Water molecules absorb heat energy from the skin to transition from liquid to vapor.
    - This heat absorption lowers the temperature of the skin and, consequently, the body.
3. **Heat Transfer:**
    - Heat is transferred from the skin to the environment through evaporated water.

#### **Relation to the Properties of Water:**

- **High Specific Heat Capacity:**
    - Water absorbs a large amount of heat before it evaporates, making it efficient for cooling.
- **High Latent Heat of Vaporization:**
    - A significant amount of energy is required to convert liquid water into vapor, ensuring effective heat removal.
- **Surface Tension:**
    - Sweat forms droplets on the skin, maximizing the surface area for evaporation.

### **Role of the Hypothalamus**

The hypothalamus is the **control center** that detects changes in body temperature via thermoreceptors:

- **Central Thermoreceptors:** Monitor blood temperature in the brain.
- **Peripheral Thermoreceptors:** Detect temperature changes in the skin.

The hypothalamus sends signals to the respective **effectors** to restore homeostasis.

- The process of body *temperature* coordination occurs in the part of the brain called **Hypothalamus**.

- When the environment changes, signals are sent to the brain to alert the **Hypothalamus**.

- The **Hypothalamus** then responds by activating the process of **Vasodilation, Vasoconstriction,** *Shivering,* and *Sweating* to maintain body *temperature* constant.

### **Mechanisms of Heat Exchange Between Animals and Their Environment**

To maintain a stable body temperature, animals exchange heat with their environment through **radiation, evaporation, convection, and conduction.** Here's a detailed explanation of each mechanism:

---

### **1. Radiation**

- **Definition:** Transfer of heat through electromagnetic waves (infrared radiation) without direct contact.
- **How It Works:**
    - Heat is emitted from a warmer surface to a cooler one.
    - Example: An animal absorbs heat from sunlight or radiates heat to the cooler environment at night.
- **Application in Animals:**
    - **Gain Heat:** Animals bask in the sun to warm up.
    - **Lose Heat:** Radiate excess body heat to the cooler environment.
- **Environmental Factor:** Works in both warm and cold environments, depending on whether the animal is gaining or losing heat.

---

### **2. Evaporation**

- **Definition:** Loss of heat when water changes from liquid to vapor, carrying heat away from the surface.
- **How It Works:**
    - Water (sweat, saliva, or moisture) evaporates, cooling the skin or surface of the body.
    - Example: Sweating in mammals or panting in dogs.
- **Application in Animals:**
    - **Lose Heat:** Used primarily in hot environments to prevent overheating.
- **Environmental Factor:** Most effective in dry, hot climates; less effective in humid conditions where evaporation is slower.

---

### **3. Convection**

- **Definition:** Transfer of heat through the movement of air or water over the body surface.
- **How It Works:**
    - Heat is carried away when cool air or water passes over the animal’s body.
    - Example: Wind cooling an overheated animal or warm water heating a cold-blooded fish.
- **Application in Animals:**
    - **Gain Heat:** Animals may immerse themselves in warm water.
    - **Lose Heat:** Animals face into a breeze to enhance heat loss.
- **Environmental Factor:** Depends on air or water temperature and movement.

---

### **4. Conduction**

- **Definition:** Direct transfer of heat between two objects or surfaces in contact.
- **How It Works:**
    - Heat flows from a warmer object to a cooler one.
    - Example: A lizard warming itself on a hot rock or an animal losing heat to a cold surface.
- **Application in Animals:**
    - **Gain Heat:** Resting on warm surfaces.
    - **Lose Heat:** Contact with cool surfaces or water.
- **Environmental Factor:** Requires physical contact with a solid or liquid medium.

---

|Evaporation|Heat loss through water vaporization.|Sweating, panting, or licking fur.|Hot environments (dry preferred).|
|Convection|Heat transfer via air or water movement.|Breeze cooling or warm water immersion.|Depends on air/water temperature.|

### **Importance of These Mechanisms**

Animals use these heat exchange mechanisms to maintain **thermoregulation**, ensuring their survival in various environmental conditions. The balance between heat gain and heat loss allows homeothermic animals to stabilize their body temperature, while poikilothermic animals adapt more directly to external changes.

 The size of the animal also affects the regulation of body *temperature.*
  - As animals grow in size, their *volume* increases, and the surface area decreases.
  - This affects the *Surface-to-Volume ratio,* which affects heat loss.
  - The larger *Surface area* to *Volume ratio* an animal has, the less heat loss it will have, and vice versa.
  - Generally, smaller *Surface to Volume ratio* increases *diffusion* of heat and other materials, and vice versa.


## **Osmoregulation**

- **Osmoregulation** is a process that regulates the *osmotic pressure* of fluids and *electrolyte* balance in organisms to maintain *homeostasis.*
  - *electrolytic Balance* is the total body balance of charged molecules.
  - About 60% of the human body is fluids.
  - This water is divided into two main components:
    - **Intracellular Fluid (ICF):**
      - About 2/3 or *40%* of the total body water is found inside the cells.
      - It is useful for transporting nutrients and **waste** materials.
    - **Extracellular Fluid (ECF):**
      - The remaining 1/3 or *20%* is found outside the cells.
      - Outside the cell, it is further divided into *Interstitial Fluid* and *Plasma*.
  - So **Osmoregulation** is important to balance the pressure of fluids and *electrolytes*.
  - In humans and other animals, *osmotic pressures* (gradients) are controlled by the **Osmoreceptor**.
  - Humans and most animals have **Osmoreceptors** in their hypothalamus of their brain.
  - The two main types of **Osmoregulators** are:
    - **Osmoconformers**
    - **Osmoregulators**

- **Osmoconformers**
  - Animals that try to match the *osmolarity* of their surroundings with their body and cells.
  - **Osmoconformers** are animals that maintain *osmotic pressures* inside their body as outside.
  - *Examples* are *invertebrates.*

- **Osmoregulators**
  - Are animals that actively regulate their *osmotic pressures* irrespective of their surroundings.
  - *Examples* are most *vertebrates,* including *humans*.
  - The **kidney** is the main responsible organ for **Osmoregulation** in humans.
  - When the water level of the body is high, the **kidney** releases large amounts of *hypotonic urine.*
