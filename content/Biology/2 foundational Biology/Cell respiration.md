## **Introduction to Cellular Respiration and ATP**

>[!note] **Cellular Respiration** 
>**Cellular respiration** is the process in which organisms convert nutrients into micronutrients that will be used to form energy for various metabolic reactions.

- Respiration is the enzymatic release of energy from organic compounds in living cells. The energy stored in **ATP**, which is the primary energy carrier in all living organisms, is originally derived from the sun.
- Cells need energy to perform all their functions, such as muscle contraction, nerve impulses, and building new molecules. *Cellular respiration provides this energy by converting the chemical energy stored in glucose into a usable form (ATP).*

> [!note] **ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)**
> A molecule that serves as the **primary energy currency of the cell**, storing and releasing energy for cellular processes.

- The overall purpose of cellular respiration is to generate a **usable form of energy (ATP)** from the breakdown of fuel molecules, primarily **glucose**. This energy is then used to power all cellular activities.

- **There are two types of cellular respiration:**

	1. **Aerobic respiration** - Aerobic respiration requires oxygen and produces most of the energy. The [[Cell Biology#*Mitochondria*|Mitochondria]] is the organelle in which the aerobic stage of cellular respiration occurs.

	2. **Anaerobic respiration** - Anaerobic respiration does not require oxygen, and it produces a very small amount of energy. the [[Cell Biology#*Cytoplasm*|cytoplasm]] is where most of the anerobic reactions take place.


During the process of **cellular respiration**, *glucose* (a sugar produced as a result of digestion) reacts with *oxygen* to release energy that can be used by the cell. *Carbon dioxide and water* are produced as waste products.

The reaction can be summed up as follows:
$$C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy (ATP + Heat)$$
The first part of cellular respiration involves **breaking glucose down** into an even smaller molecule called **Pyruvate**. The process of turning glucose into pyruvate is called **Glycolysis**. One glucose molecule produces two pyruvate molecules and a small amount of ATP.

 > If **oxygen is not present** by the time the pyruvate is produced, *anaerobic respiration* continues into a step called **Fermentation** to create ATP and a byproduct . In animals the byproduct is **Lactic acid**, while in bacteria and yeast, the byproduct is **Ethanol(ethyl alcohol).**

 - Anaerobic respiration produces far less ATP than aerobic respiration. It also produces a different waste product called *lactic acid*. The body cannot get rid of lactic acid by breathing it out as it does carbon dioxide, so, lactic acid has to be broken down. This needs oxygen, and **the amount of oxygen needed to break down the lactic acid into water and carbon dioxide is known as the oxygen debt.**


> If **oxygen is present** after the creation of pyruvate, *aerobic respiration* occurs. During this process **pyruvate moves into the mitochondrial matrix** and enters into the **Krebs cycle**, where the molecule undergoes multiple changes in order to release its **high-energy electrons**.


>[!note] **Kerbs Cycle**
> **Krebs cycle** is a sequence of reactions that releases **high-energy electrons** from **pyruvate**.
> 
> > **Hans Krebs**: German biologist, physician, and biochemist who demonstrated the “*citric acid cycle*”—now known as the Krebs cycle—in 1937, explaining aerobic respiration.

 *These high-energy electrons can’t move on their own throughout the mitochondria*. As the pyruvate moves through the cycle and transforms, molecules known as **Electron carriers** are also generated. Carriers take the electrons from the pyruvate molecules to the last step of aerobic respiration : **The Electron transport chain**.


The electron carriers bring the high-energy electrons to the inner membrane of the mitochondria. Within the inner membrane of the mitochondria, *there is a series of proteins that exist to transfer electrons across the mitochondria, like a conveyor belt*. As electrons move through these proteins,the proteins absorb their energy. With that energy, the proteins pump hydrogen ions from the matrix into the intermembrane space.

>Electrons moving through the ETC create a difference in charge on both sides of the inner membrane called the **Proton gradient**. The mitochondrion in this state is like a battery.

**Having such a high concentration of ions in one area drives [[Cell Biology#*Diffusion*|diffusion]] in the mitochondria**. So, the hydrogen ions must find their way back into the matrix, which has a lower concentration of hydrogen atoms. They do this through the last complex protein in the ETC, **ATP synthase**.

ATP synthase is a gate for hydrogen ions to go through.As they pass through the gate, the ions power it, causing it to turn, just like a waterwheel. As ATP synthase turns, it creates ATP. This process is called **Chemiosmosis**.

> **CHEMIOSMOSIS** The *movement of ions* across a membrane from a high concentration to a low concentration through diffusion.

Lastly, oxygen, because it tends to attract electrons, accepts the low-energy electrons after they reach the last protein in the chain. Any remaining hydrogen atoms will bond with this oxygen to form water.

*The most important reason humans and other animals breathe in oxygen is to clean up hydrogen and low-energy electrons* The electron transport chain, the process of chemiosmosis, and the creation of water are called **Oxidative Phosphorylation**.
$$ \text{Oxidative Phosphorylation} = \text{ETC} + \text{Chemiosmosis} + \text{Water} $$
Without oxygen, the electrons would remain in the chain,clogging it and stopping any more electrons from moving. This would prevent all cells from creating energy.

## **ATP: The Energy Currency of the Cell**

Let's start looking at the structure of ATP. As the name **Adenosine Triphosphate** suggests, it has three main parts:

- **Adenine**: A nitrogenous base, also found in DNA and RNA.

- **Ribose Sugar**: A five-carbon sugar molecule.

- **Three Phosphate Groups**: A chain of three phosphate groups linked together. The bonds between these phosphate groups are where a significant amount of energy is stored.

![[Pasted image 20260308222824.png]]

These components are arranged to form the ATP molecule. The adenine is attached to the ribose sugar forming an *adenosine*, and the three phosphate groups are attached to the ribose sugar in a chain.


Without ATP, we couldn't form a thought or move a muscle. ATP keeps our nerves firing and our heart beating. It's our body's “energy currency."

It’s the main energy currency not only in our cells, but in all forms of life on the planet. All cells make it (it doesn't travel from cell to cell), and they use it to power nearly all of their processes.

- ATP is like a tiny battery. A rechargeable AA battery is basically a package of energy that can be used to power any number of electronic devices—a remote control, a flashlight, a game controller.

The cell doesn’t have to make ATP from scratch every time it needs some energy. Like a rechargeable battery, **ATP can also be recharged and reused**.

*ADP*, the “uncharged” version of the molecule, stands for adenosine diphosphate. The word diphosphate indicates that the molecule has 2 phosphate (PO3) groups. To “charge” ADP, the cell adds a third phosphate group, converting ADP to ATP. ATP stands for adenosine triphosphate.

![atp spring](https://learn-genetics.b-cdn.net/metabolism/atp/images/atpspring.svg)


- Your cells need energy from ATP to carry out the basic functions of life, known as **metabolism**. One of their main functions is to build up large molecules from smaller ones to make new cell material (**anabolism**). Much of the energy released in respiration is used for these ‘building’ activities. Cells also break large molecules down into smaller molecules. This is known as **catabolism** and it also requires energy.

- ATP powers the **“motor” proteins** that do the small-scale work of shuttling cargo around inside of cells, as well as the large-scale work of making our **muscles contract**. Motor proteins are one shape when bound to ATP. *Hydrolysis of ATP* to ADP causes a conformational change—the protein changes shape—that generates a mechanical force. To move our muscles, many thousands of motor proteins myosin work together, breaking many molecules of ATP at a time to generate a remarkable amount of force.

![ATP Mechanical](https://learn-genetics.b-cdn.net/metabolism/atp/images/atpmech.svg)

- We are ‘**warm-blooded**’. This means that our bodies are the same temperature inside almost regardless of the temperature around us. On cold days we use energy to keep our body warm, whilst on hot days we use energy to sweat and keep our body cool.

- ATP-powered “**pumps**” in brain cells are the human body’s biggest energy users, at least when we’re at rest. These molecular pumps set up the electrochemical gradients that enable neurons to communicate with one another. The sole job of pump proteins is to move molecules from one side of a cell’s membrane to another, **against their concentration gradients**. There are different kinds of pumps, each of which moves specific ions, such as sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), or protons (H+). A calcium (Ca++) pump is shown below. Some pumps move other types of small molecules.

 ![ATP Transport](https://learn-genetics.b-cdn.net/metabolism/atp/images/ATPTransport.svg)


### *Two Ways for Making ATP*

As our cells oxidize carbon-based molecules from our food, some of the energy held within their chemical bonds is released. Our whole complex metabolic system is arranged to capture some of this energy and put it to work. If ATP is like a battery, then cellular respiration is like a battery charger.

Our cells have two ways to make ATP: 

- **substrate-level phosphorylation**
- **oxidative phosphorylation** 

Plants have a third. During **photosynthesis**, they use energy from sunlight to make ATP.

##### Substrate-level Phosphorylation

This process involves an enzyme (a type of protein) which transfers a **phosphate group** from a *substrate* (in this case, a carbon-based molecule from food) to **ADP**.

The ATP generated during **glycolysis** and the **citric acid cycle** come about in this way, accounting for **4 ATPs per glucose molecule**.


![oxidative phosphorylation](https://learn-genetics.b-cdn.net/metabolism/atp/images/ATPSubPhos.svg)


##### Oxidative Phosphorylation

This process generates most of the ATP we use—up to 27 for each molecule of glucose.

As enzymes break apart the molecules from food, they transfer *hydrogen atoms (made of a proton and an electron) to the carrier molecules FAD and NAD.* The carriers deliver these protons and electrons to the mitochondrial membrane.

The movement of electrons along the electron transport chain powers pumps that move protons into the space between the two mitochondrial membranes. The protons then diffuse back across the membrane through **ATP synthase**, a remarkable molecular machine that uses the energy from proton diffusion to “charge” molecules of ATP.

![oxidative phosphorylation](https://learn-genetics.b-cdn.net/metabolism/atp/images/oxiphos.svg)


**ATP is broken down using a hydrolysis reaction and re-synthesised using a condensation reaction:**

1. **Hydrolysis** - The addition of water to break a chemical bond between two molecules.
2. **Condensation** - The removal of water to form a chemical bond between two molecules.

The breakdown and re-synthesis of ATP is an example of a reversible reaction.

- **Hydrolysis of ATP**: When water is added to ATP, it breaks down into adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate (Pi). This reaction is catalysed by the enzyme **ATP hydrolase** and releases energy for use in cells.

![[Pasted image 20260308225306.png]]

- **Condensation reaction**: When a phosphate group and ADP join, a water molecule is released. This reaction is catalysed by the enzyme ATP synthase. This process requires energy and traps chemical energy in the bond.

![[Pasted image 20260308225445.png]]

**The following features allow ATP to work well as an immediate energy source:**

1. The hydrolysis of ATP releases a small amount of energy, meaning little energy is lost as heat.
2. ATP is broken down in one step, meaning energy is released quickly.
3. ATP is rapidly re-synthesised so that ATP is always readily available.
4. The inorganic phosphate from ATP hydrolysis can phosphorylate other compounds, which makes them more reactive.
5. The bonds between the phosphate groups are unstable, have a low activation energy and are easily broken.
6. ATP is soluble, so it can easily be transported around cells.


## **Coupled Reactions and Energy Transfer**

First, let's clarify the difference between two types of reactions based on their energy change:

 **Exothermic vs. Endothermic Reactions**

| Feature                       | Exothermic (**Exergonic**) Reactions                    | Endothermic (**Endergonic**) Reactions            |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Energy Change                 | Release energy                                          | Require energy input                              |
| Gibbs Free Energy Change (ΔG) | Negative (ΔG < 0)                                       | Positive (ΔG > 0)                                 |
| Spontaneity                   | Spontaneous (can occur without continuous energy input) | Non-spontaneous (require continuous energy input) |
| Examples                      | Combustion, ATP hydrolysis                              | Photosynthesis, synthesis of proteins             |

In cells, many essential processes, like building complex molecules or moving substances *against a concentration gradient*, are **endergonic** – they require energy input to occur. However, cells can make these energetically unfavorable reactions happen by coupling them with **exergonic** reactions, particularly the **hydrolysis of ATP**.

> **Coupled reactions** occur when an energy-releasing reaction (exergonic) is paired with an energy-requiring reaction (endergonic). The energy released from the exergonic reaction is used to drive the endergonic reaction, making the overall coupled process *energetically favorable* (meaning it can proceed).

- A **coupled reaction** occurs when an **exergonic reaction** (energy-releasing, $\Delta G < 0$) drives an **endergonic reaction** (energy-requiring, $\Delta G > 0$) so that the **overall process becomes energetically favorable**.

In biological systems, the most common example is the hydrolysis of **ATP (adenosine triphosphate)**:
$$\text{ATP} + H_2O \rightarrow \text{ADP} + P_i$$
For this reaction:
$$\Delta G^\circ \approx -30.5 \text{ kJ/mol}$$
Because this value is **negative**, ATP hydrolysis is **exergonic**.

- **Exergonic reaction:**  Spontaneous (thermodynamically favorable) $\Delta G < 0$
- **Endergonic reaction:** Non-spontaneous unless energy is supplied $\Delta G > 0$
#### How Coupling Works

Suppose we have:

- **Reaction 1 (endergonic)**: $A\rightarrow B \quad \Delta G_1 = +20 \text{ kJ/mol}$
- **Reaction 2 (ATP hydrolysis, exergonic)**: $\Delta G_2 = -30.5 \text{ kJ/mol}$

If these reactions are **mechanistically linked** (often through a shared intermediate), their free energies **add**:
$$\Delta G_{\text{total}} = \Delta G_1 + \Delta G_2$$
$$\Delta G_{\text{total}} = (+20) + (-30.5) = -10.5 \text{ kJ/mol}$$
Now: $\Delta G_{\text{total}} < 0$ So the **combined reaction becomes spontaneous**. The **sum** of free energy changes determines spontaneity.

The coupling often happens through a **shared intermediate molecule**. For example, the phosphate group released from ATP hydrolysis can be temporarily attached to another molecule, making it more reactive and lowering the activation energy for the endergonic reaction.


## **Aerobic Respiration: Stages and Energy Harvest**

### *Site of Cellular Respiration*

the **mitochondrion** plays a central role in aerobic respiration. It's often called the "powerhouse" of the cell because this is where the majority of ATP is produced. To understand how the later stages of cellular respiration work, it's important to know the structure of the mitochondrion [[Cell Biology#*Mitochondria*|structure of mitochondrion]].

 let's consider **prokaryotic cells** (cells that lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles), such as bacteria. Prokaryotes also perform cellular respiration to generate ATP, but the location of the stages differs because they don't have mitochondria.

In prokaryotes, glycolysis still occurs in the **cytosol**. However, the processes analogous to pyruvate oxidation, the Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation occur in the **cytoplasm and on the inner surface of the plasma membrane**.

| Feature                   | Eukaryotic Cells             | Prokaryotic Cells                |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Glycolysis                | Cytosol                      | Cytosol                          |
| Pyruvate Oxidation        | Mitochondrial Matrix         | Cytoplasm                        |
| Krebs Cycle               | Mitochondrial Matrix         | Cytoplasm                        |
| Oxidative Phosphorylation | Inner Mitochondrial Membrane | Inner surface of Plasma Membrane |
### *Glycolysis*

> **Glycolysis** literally means "sugar splitting" ("glyco" for sugar, "lysis" for splitting).  It's a metabolic pathway that breaks down a six-carbon glucose molecule into two three-carbon molecules called **pyruvate**.

This process occurs in the **cytosol** of the cell and is **oxygen-independent**, meaning it can happen whether or not oxygen is present. Because it doesn't require oxygen, glycolysis is also the first step in anaerobic respiration (fermentation).

| Reactant         | Product                |
| ---------------- | ---------------------- |
| Glucose          | Pyruvate (2 molecules) |
| 2ATP (net input) | 4ATP (net output)      |
| NAD+             | NADH (2 molecules)     |
| 2ADP             | 4ADP                   |
| Phosphate groups | Water (2 molecules)    |
Glycolysis involves a series of ten enzymatic reactions. These reactions can be grouped into two main phases:

- **phase 1: energy investment phase: Steps 1–5**
- **phase 2: payoff(energy liberation) phase: Steps 6–10**


#### Phase I: Energy Investment Phase
---
**Step 1: Phosphorylation of Glucose**

- **Enzyme:** **Hexokinase / Glucokinase**

- **Reaction:**  $\text{Glucose} + \text{ATP} \rightarrow \text{Glucose-6-phosphate (G6P)} + \text{ADP}$

- ATP donates a phosphate group.
    
- Glucose becomes **trapped inside the cell** (charged molecule cannot exit).
    
- This is an **irreversible step**.

Phosphorylation activates glucose chemically, preparing it for structural rearrangement.

**Step 2: Isomerization**

- **Enzyme:** **Phosphoglucose isomerase**
    
- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{Glucose-6-phosphate} \leftrightarrow \text{Fructose-6-phosphate (F6P)}$

- Conversion from **aldose → ketose**.
    
- Structure rearranged for easier cleavage later.

The molecule is reshaped into a form that can be symmetrically split later.

---

**Step 3: Second Phosphorylation**

- **Enzyme:** **Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1)**
    
- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{F6P} + \text{ATP} \rightarrow \text{Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP)} + \text{ADP}$

- Another phosphate is added.
    
- This is the **committed step** of glycolysis.
    
- Irreversible.

Now the molecule is fully “activated” and ready to be split.

---
 **Step 4: Cleavage**

- **Enzyme:** **Aldolase**
    
- **Reaction:**  

$\text{F1,6BP} \rightarrow \text{Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P)} + \text{Dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP)}$

- 6-carbon molecule splits into two 3-carbon molecules.

Only **G3P continues**, so DHAP must be converted.

---

**Step 5: Interconversion**

- **Enzyme:** **Triose phosphate isomerase**
    
- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{DHAP} \leftrightarrow \text{G3P}$

- DHAP is converted into G3P.

Now we have **2 molecules of G3P**, and all further steps occur **twice per glucose**.

---

#### Phase II: Energy Payoff Phase

---

**Step 6: Oxidation and Phosphorylation**

- **Enzyme:** **Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase**
    
- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{G3P} + \text{NAD}^+ + P_i \rightarrow \text{1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (1,3-BPG)} + \text{NADH}$

- G3P is **oxidized**.
    
- NAD⁺ is reduced to **NADH** by adding an hydride($H^-$) to NAD⁺ giving one electron to on of the proton on NAD⁺ converting it into NADH.
    
- A **high-energy phosphate bond** is formed.

Energy from oxidation is captured in both **NADH and a high-energy intermediate**.

---

**Step 7: First ATP Generation**

- **Enzyme:** **Phosphoglycerate kinase**

- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{1,3-BPG} + \text{ADP} \rightarrow \text{3-phosphoglycerate (3PG)} + \text{ATP}$

- High-energy phosphate transferred to ADP.
    
- **ATP produced (substrate-level phosphorylation)**

Energy stored earlier is now converted into usable ATP.

---

**Step 8: Rearrangement**

- **Enzyme:** **Phosphoglycerate mutase**
    
- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{3PG} \leftrightarrow \text{2-phosphoglycerate (2PG)}$
    
- Phosphate group shifts position.

Prepares molecule for dehydration and formation of a higher-energy compound.

---

 **Step 9: Dehydration**

- **Enzyme:** **Enolase**
    
- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{2PG} \rightarrow \text{Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)} + H_2O$

- Water is removed.
    
- Produces **PEP**, a molecule with a very high-energy phosphate bond.
    

This step creates the most energy-rich intermediate in glycolysis.

---
**Step 10: Second ATP Generation**

- **Enzyme:** **Pyruvate kinase**
    
- **Reaction:**  
    $\text{PEP} + \text{ADP} \rightarrow \text{Pyruvate} + \text{ATP}$

- Phosphate transferred to ADP → ATP formed.
    
- Final product: **Pyruvate**.
    

The pathway ends with extraction of remaining energy as ATP.

![[Pasted image 20260323113242.png]]
$$ \boxed{\text{Glucose}+2ADP^{+}+2Pi \rightarrow 2\text{Pyruvate}+2ATP+H^{+}+2H_2O}$$
### *Pyruvate Oxidation(link reaction)*

> **Pyruvate oxidation** is the process that connects **glycolysis** to the **citric acid cycle**. It occurs in the **mitochondrial matrix** and converts **pyruvate (3C)** into **acetyl-CoA (2C)**, while releasing **CO₂** and generating **NADH**.

This process is carried out by a large multi-enzyme complex called the **pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC)**.

As we just learned, glycolysis breaks down one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate.  If oxygen is present in eukaryotic cells, these two pyruvate molecules are transported from the cytosol into the **mitochondrial matrix** to undergo pyruvate oxidation.

**Steps of Pyruvate Oxidation**:

- **Decarboxylation**: A carboxyl group is removed from pyruvate as a molecule of carbon dioxide (CO₂). This is the first time CO₂ is produced during aerobic respiration.

- **Oxidation**: The remaining two-carbon molecule is oxidized. Electrons are transferred to NAD⁺, reducing it to **NADH**.

- **Attachment to Coenzyme A**: The oxidized two-carbon molecule, now an acetyl group, is attached to a molecule of **coenzyme A (CoA)** to form **acetyl CoA**. CoA is a carrier molecule that will deliver the acetyl group to the next stage, the Krebs cycle.

Since two pyruvate molecules are produced from each glucose molecule during glycolysis, the pyruvate oxidation occurs twice per glucose molecule. Therefore, from one molecule of glucose, the pyruvate oxidation produces a total of **2 molecules of acetyl CoA, 2 molecules of NADH, and 2 molecules of CO₂**.


**Overall Reaction**: $\text{Pyruvate} + CoA + NAD^+ \rightarrow \text{Acetyl-CoA} + CO_2 + NADH$


```
Pyruvate (3C)
   │
   │  (Decarboxylation → CO₂ released)
   ↓
2-carbon fragment
   │
   │  (Oxidation → NADH formed)
   ↓
Acetyl group
   │
   │  (Attachment to CoA)
   ↓
Acetyl-CoA (2C)
```

---

**Step-by-Step Mechanism (Enzyme Complex)**

The process occurs through **3 tightly coupled enzymatic steps**, all within the **pyruvate dehydrogenase complex**:

**Step 1: Decarboxylation**

* **Enzyme:** **E1 – Pyruvate dehydrogenase**
* **Cofactor:** **Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP)**

 **Reaction:**
$$
\text{Pyruvate} \rightarrow \text{Hydroxyethyl-TPP} + CO_2
$$
* A **CO₂ molecule is removed** (loss of one carbon).
* Remaining 2-carbon fragment attaches to TPP.

This step removes the unstable carboxyl group, preparing the molecule for oxidation.

---

 **Step 2: Oxidation and Transfer**

* **Enzyme:** **E2 – Dihydrolipoyl transacetylase**
* **Cofactor:** **Lipoamide**

 **Reaction:**
$$
\text{Hydroxyethyl group} \rightarrow \text{Acetyl group}
$$
* The 2-carbon fragment is **oxidized**.
* Transferred to **lipoamide**, forming an **acetyl group**.


Energy from oxidation is conserved in a **high-energy thioester intermediate**.

---
**Step 3: Formation of Acetyl-CoA**

* **Enzyme:** **E2 (same enzyme continues)**
* **Cofactor:** **Coenzyme A (CoA)**

**Reaction:**
$$
\text{Acetyl group} + CoA \rightarrow \text{Acetyl-CoA}
$$
* Acetyl group is transferred to **CoA**.
* Forms **acetyl-CoA**, a high-energy molecule.

Now the carbon unit is activated for entry into the citric acid cycle.

---

**Step 4: Regeneration of Oxidized Cofactors**

* **Enzyme:** **E3 – Dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase**
* **Cofactors:** **FAD, NAD⁺**

**Reaction:**
$$
\text{Reduced lipoamide} \rightarrow \text{Oxidized lipoamide}
$$
* Electrons flow:

  * Lipoamide → FAD → NAD⁺
* Produces **NADH**

This restores the enzyme complex to its original state for another cycle.

### *Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle)*

> The **Krebs cycle** takes place in the *mitochondrial matrix* in eukaryotic cells. It's a series of **eight enzymatic reactions** that complete the oxidation of glucose derivatives, extracting **high-energy electrons** that will be used to generate a large amount of ATP in the final stage of aerobic respiration.

The citric acid cycle (CAC) is a cyclic metabolic pathway that occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. Its role is to **fully oxidize the acetyl group (2C) from acetyl-CoA** into CO₂, while capturing energy in the form of:

- NADH
- FADH₂
- ATP (or GTP)

Each turn of the cycle processes one acetyl-CoA, and since one glucose produces 2 acetyl-CoA, the cycle runs twice per glucose.

The cycle begins with the entry of **acetyl CoA**, the molecule produced during pyruvate oxidation. Acetyl CoA (a two-carbon molecule) combines with a four-carbon molecule called **oxaloacetate (OAA)** to form a six-carbon molecule called **citrate** (which is why it's also called the Citric Acid Cycle).

As the cycle progresses, citrate undergoes a series of reactions involving oxidation and decarboxylation. During these reactions, carbon atoms are released as carbon dioxide (CO₂), and high-energy electrons are transferred to electron carriers, specifically NAD⁺ and FAD, reducing them to NADH and FADH₂ respectively.

The cyclical nature of the Krebs cycle means that the starting molecule, oxaloacetate, is regenerated at the end of the cycle, ready to combine with another molecule of acetyl CoA and continue the process.


 **Overall Reaction**:  $\text{Acetyl-CoA} + 3 NAD^+ + FAD + ADP + P_i \rightarrow 2 CO_2 + 3 NADH + FADH_2 + ATP + CoA$


```id="5rsz1l"
Acetyl-CoA (2C)
   ↓
+ Oxaloacetate (4C)
   ↓
Citrate (6C)
   ↓
Isocitrate
   ↓
α-Ketoglutarate (5C) + CO₂
   ↓
Succinyl-CoA (4C) + CO₂
   ↓
Succinate
   ↓
Fumarate
   ↓
Malate
   ↓
Oxaloacetate (regenerated)
```


**Step 1: Formation of Citrate**

* **Enzyme:** **Citrate synthase**

**Reaction:**
$$
\text{Acetyl-CoA} + \text{Oxaloacetate} \rightarrow \text{Citrate}
$$
* 2C + 4C → 6C molecule
* CoA is released

This step **initiates the cycle** by combining incoming carbon with a carrier molecule.

---

**Step 2: Isomerization**

* **Enzyme:** **Aconitase**

**Reaction:**
$$
\text{Citrate} \leftrightarrow \text{Isocitrate}
$$
* Rearrangement of hydroxyl group


Prepares molecule for **oxidative decarboxylation**

---

**Step 3: First Oxidation + CO₂ Release**

* **Enzyme:** **Isocitrate dehydrogenase**

 **Reaction:**
$$
\text{Isocitrate} + NAD^+ \rightarrow \text{α-Ketoglutarate} + CO_2 + NADH
$$
* First **CO₂ released**
* First **NADH produced**

Energy begins to be extracted via **oxidation**

---

**Step 4: Second Oxidation + CO₂ Release**

* **Enzyme:** **α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase**

 **Reaction:**
$$
\text{α-Ketoglutarate} + NAD^+ + CoA \rightarrow \text{Succinyl-CoA} + CO_2 + NADH
$$
* Second **CO₂ released**
* Another **NADH produced**
* Formation of **high-energy thioester (succinyl-CoA)**

At this point, both carbons from acetyl-CoA are **fully oxidized to CO₂**.

---

**Step 5: Substrate-Level Phosphorylation**

* **Enzyme:** **Succinyl-CoA synthetase**

**Reaction:**
$$
\text{Succinyl-CoA} + ADP + P_i \rightarrow \text{Succinate} + ATP + CoA
$$
* Direct formation of **ATP**
* CoA released

Energy stored in thioester bond is converted into ATP.

---

**Step 6: Oxidation**

* **Enzyme:** **Succinate dehydrogenase**

$$
\text{Succinate} + FAD \rightarrow \text{Fumarate} + FADH_2
$$
* Produces **FADH₂**

Another electron carrier is reduced.

---

**Step 7: Hydration**

* **Enzyme:** **Fumarase**

**Reaction:**
$$
\text{Fumarate} + H_2O \rightarrow \text{Malate}
$$
* Water added across double bond

Prepares molecule for final oxidation.

### *Oxidative Phosphorylation (Electron Transport Chain and Chemiosmosis)*

> **Oxidative phosphorylation** occurs in the **inner mitochondrial membrane** of eukaryotic cells. This membrane is highly folded into **cristae**, which increases the surface area available for the many protein complexes involved in this process.

This is the **final stage of cellular respiration**, occurring in the **inner mitochondrial membrane**, where energy stored in:

* **NADH**
* **FADH₂**

is converted into a large amount of **ATP**.

The process has two tightly coupled parts:

1. **Electron Transport Chain (ETC)** → movement of electrons
2. **Oxidative Phosphorylation** → ATP synthesis using a proton gradient

The Electron Transport Chain (ETC) is a series of protein complexes and other molecules embedded in the *inner mitochondrial membrane*. The primary role of the ETC is to **accept high-energy electrons from NADH and FADH₂**, the electron carriers produced during glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, and the Krebs cycle.

As electrons are passed from one complex to another down the ETC, they move to increasingly electronegative carriers, releasing energy in a series of small steps.
 This released energy is used to pump **protons (H⁺ ions)** from the mitochondrial matrix across the inner mitochondrial membrane into the *intermembrane space*.

This pumping of protons creates a higher concentration of H⁺ ions in the intermembrane space compared to the mitochondrial matrix. This difference in **proton concentration**, along with the electrical charge difference across the membrane (positive in the intermembrane space, negative in the matrix), establishes an **electrochemical gradient or proton motive force** across the inner mitochondrial membrane

This brings us to the second part of oxidative phosphorylation: **Chemiosmosis**.
 The proton motive force represents stored potential energy, much like water behind a dam. Protons in the intermembrane space can only flow back into the mitochondrial matrix through a special enzyme complex embedded in the inner membrane called **ATP synthase**.
 
ATP synthase acts like a tiny turbine. As protons flow through ATP synthase down their **electrochemical gradient**, the energy released from this movement is used to catalyze the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi). This process is called chemiosmosis-driven ATP synthesis, and it is the major way ATP is produced during aerobic respiration.

At the end of the electron transport chain, the electrons, now at a lower energy level, are transferred to their final acceptor: oxygen (O₂). Oxygen then combines with protons (H⁺) from the mitochondrial matrix to form water (H₂O). Oxygen is essential for aerobic respiration because it acts as the terminal electron acceptor, allowing the electrons to flow through the ETC and the proton gradient to be maintained. Without oxygen, the ETC would become blocked, and oxidative phosphorylation would stop

$NADH + H^+ + \frac{1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow NAD^+ + H_2O + \text{ATP (via proton gradient)}$


```id="r4w5yo"
NADH / FADH₂
      ↓
Electron Transport Chain (Complex I–IV)
      ↓
Proton Gradient (H⁺ stored in intermembrane space)
      ↓
ATP Synthase
      ↓
ATP
```


 **Step 1: Electron Entry (NADH vs FADH₂)**

-  **Pathway A: NADH**

* **Enzyme:** **Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase)**
$$
\text{NADH} \rightarrow \text{NAD}^+ + e^-
$$
* Electrons enter at **higher energy level**
* Protons are pumped across membrane

 **Pathway B: FADH₂**

* **Enzyme:** **Complex II (Succinate dehydrogenase)**
$$
\text{FADH}_2 \rightarrow \text{FAD} + e^-
$$
* Electrons enter at **lower energy level**
* **No proton pumping here**

 **Step 2: Electron Flow Through Carriers**

Electrons move through a series of carriers:

* **Coenzyme Q (Ubiquinone)**
* **Complex III**
* **Cytochrome c**
* **Complex IV**

* Electrons move from **high energy → low energy**
* Energy released is used to **pump protons (H⁺)**

---

**Step 3: Proton Gradient Formation**

* Protons are pumped:

  * From **matrix → intermembrane space**
* Creates:

  * **Electrochemical gradient** Also called **proton motive force**

---

 **Step 4: Final Electron Acceptor**

* **Oxygen (O₂)** accepts electrons at **Complex IV**
$$
O_2 + e^- + H^+ \rightarrow H_2O
$$

* Oxygen is reduced to **water**
* This keeps electrons flowing

---
**Step 5: ATP Synthesis (Chemiosmosis)**

* **Enzyme:** **ATP synthase**
$$
ADP + P_i \rightarrow ATP
$$
* Protons flow back into matrix through ATP synthase
* This flow drives **ATP production**

## **Catabolism of Other Macromolecules**

> **catabolism** is the breaking down of complex macromolecules into simpler micro-molecules, releasing energy as a result.

### *Energy from Carbohydrates*

**Carbohydrates** are a primary source of energy for most organisms. They come in various forms, from simple sugars (*monosaccharides*) to complex chains of sugars (*polysaccharides*).

 Ultimately, for most carbohydrates to be used in cellular respiration, they must be broken down into their basic **monosaccharide units**, particularly glucose.


- **Monosaccharides**: Simple sugars like glucose, fructose, and galactose. Glucose can directly enter glycolysis. Fructose and galactose are converted into intermediates of glycolysis in a few enzymatic steps.

- **Disaccharides**: Carbohydrates made of two linked monosaccharides, such as *sucrose* (glucose + fructose), *lactose* (glucose + galactose), and *maltose* (glucose + glucose). These are broken down into their constituent monosaccharides by enzymes (like sucrase, lactase, and maltase) in the digestive system or within the cell. The resulting monosaccharides then enter the appropriate metabolic pathways.

- **Complex Carbohydrates (Polysaccharides):** Long chains of monosaccharides, like *starch* (the main storage carbohydrate in plants) and *glycogen* (the main storage carbohydrate in animals, found in liver and muscles). These must be broken down into glucose units through a process called **hydrolysis** (adding water to break bonds). Enzymes like amylase (for starch) and glycogen phosphorylase (for glycogen) facilitate this breakdown. The resulting glucose molecules then enter glycolysis.

Once these complex carbohydrates and disaccharides are broken down into monosaccharides, the monosaccharides can enter the cellular respiration pathway. Glucose directly enters glycolysis. Other monosaccharides like fructose and galactose are converted into intermediates of glycolysis, allowing them to be catabolized for energy.

### *Energy from Proteins*

While carbohydrates and fats are the primary energy sources, proteins can also be catabolized for energy, especially during times of **starvation or prolonged exercise when carbohydrate and fat stores are low**.

> **Proteins** are complex macromolecules made up of smaller units called **amino acids**.

Before proteins can be used for energy, they must be broken down into these individual amino acids.
This occurs through **digestion** in the gastrointestinal tract, or within cells through the breakdown of cellular proteins.

![[Pasted image 20260330162345.png]]

once broken down a **keto acid** is introduced which are major intermediates of Krebs cycle, you see proteins monomers(amino acids)  containing an **amino group($-CH_2$)**, a **carboxyl group**, a hydrogen atom, and a variable side chain(R), when a keto acid is introduced to an amino acid they start interchanging molecules such as the amino group and the double bonded oxygen until they have formed a new version of themselves 

$$\text{keto acid} + \text{amino acid} \rightarrow \text{new amino acid} = \text{new keto acid}$$

this process is known as **Transamination**. once this is produced the new amino acid is further broken down by removing the amino group and converting it into **ammonia**($NH_3$) which is toxic to the body so it is further broken down into **urea** via the **urea cycle**, and is excreted from the body in the form of urine and it converts the amino acid into a keto acid like $\alpha$-ketoglutrate and this process of removing the amino group from the amino acid and converting it into urea and making the amino acid usable is called **oxidative deamination**.

once this is done the new keto acid is introduced into the the cellular respiration pathway into the glycolysis or into the Krebs cycle all based on the type of amino acid used initially and this become essential for more energy production. 


Both proteins and carbohydrates provide approximately **4 kilocalories of energy per gram**.
 However, the net ATP yield per gram of protein is generally lower than that of carbohydrates

Proteins are **primarily used for building and repairing tissues, synthesizing enzymes and hormones**, and other vital cellular functions. Using them for energy is a secondary role. While proteins can serve as an energy source, they are not the body's preferred fuel. Carbohydrates and fats are more efficiently used for energy production, and the catabolism of protein for energy can lead to muscle breakdown if dietary intake is insufficient.

### *Energy from Fats (Lipids)*

> **Fats**, also known as **lipids**, are a diverse group of molecules that are generally insoluble in water. The most common type of fat used for energy is **triglycerides**, which are composed of a *glycerol* molecule linked to *three fatty acid chains*.


![[Pasted image 20260330162221.png]]

**Fats** are a highly efficient form of energy storage and yield a significant amount of ATP when broken down

Before triglycerides can be used for energy, they must be broken down into their component parts: **glycerol** and **fatty acids**. This process, called **lipolysis**, occurs through the action of enzymes called **lipases**.


Once broken down, glycerol and fatty acids enter cellular respiration pathways at different points:


- **Glycerol**: Glycerol which is a 3 carbon molecule is converted into *dihydroxyacetone phosphate* (DHAP) another 3 carbon molecule which is an intermediate of glycolysis. It can then proceed through the rest of glycolysis and into aerobic respiration.

- **Fatty Acids**: Fatty acids are catabolized through a process called *beta-oxidation*, which occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.

> **Beta-oxidation** is a cyclical process that breaks down *fatty acid* chains(typically a 16 carbon molecule) to **two carbons at a time**.

In each cycle of beta-oxidation, a two-carbon unit is cleaved from the fatty acid chain, producing:

- **1 molecule of Acetyl CoA(2c)**
- **1 molecule of NADH**
- **1 molecule of FADH2**

The cycles of beta-oxidation continue until the entire fatty acid chain is broken down into acetyl CoA molecules. For example, a 16-carbon fatty acid will undergo 7 cycles of beta-oxidation, producing 8 molecules of acetyl CoA.

Fats provide significantly more energy per gram: **approximately 9 kilocalories per gram compared to 4 kilocalories per gram for carbohydrates and proteins**. The reason for this higher energy yield is that fatty acids are more reduced than carbohydrates, meaning they have more carbon-hydrogen bonds. These bonds store more potential energy that can be released during oxidation in cellular respiration.

When the acetyl CoA molecules produced from beta-oxidation enter the Krebs cycle and the subsequent oxidative phosphorylation, they generate a large amount of ATP. The NADH and FADH₂ produced during beta-oxidation also contribute to ATP production in oxidative phosphorylation.


## **Anaerobic Respiration: Fermentation**

> **Anaerobic respiration** is a process that allows cells to produce a *small amount of ATP* in the absence of oxygen.

Unlike aerobic respiration, which uses oxygen as the *final electron acceptor* in the electron transport chain, anaerobic respiration utilizes different molecules as *terminal electron acceptors*.

However, the term "**fermentation**" is often used interchangeably with anaerobic respiration, and in many contexts, particularly in biology, **fermentation refers to the process that occurs after glycolysis to regenerate NAD⁺ when oxygen is not available**.

**Fermentation** has the following characteristics:

- The entire "purpose" of fermentation is not actually to make energy (Glycolysis already did that); it is to **recycle NAD+.**
- the result of recycling NAD+ requires NADH to dump all of its hydrides onto a waste molecule like pyruvate converting it into lactate.
- fermentation strictly adheres to glycolysis and doesn't involve the kerbs cycle or ETC.


Here is how it works in your cells:
1. Glycolysis needs NAD+ to keep running.
2. In the process of making ATP, NAD+ gets filled with electrons and becomes NADH.
3. If you run out of NAD+, Glycolysis stops, and the cell dies.
4. Fermentation takes those electrons off NADH and dumps them onto a waste molecule (like Pyruvate), turning it into Lactic Acid.
5. This "empties" the NADH back into NAD+, so Glycolysis can go again.


Unlike fermentation (which just tries to keep glycolysis moving), the purpose of **anaerobic respiration** is to generate as much ATP as possible using an **Electron Transport Chain** (ETC). It is essentially the "full version" of respiration, just using a different "plug" at the end of the wire. In anaerobic respiration, the cell uses a *non-oxygen inorganic molecule* to catch the electrons at the end of the ETC.


In biology, **Respiration** (whether aerobic or anaerobic) is a specific "machine" that requires three parts:

- The Krebs Cycle: To strip electrons from food.
- An Electron Transport Chain (ETC): A series of proteins in a membrane.
- A Terminal Electron Acceptor: A molecule at the end of the chain to catch the electrons.

**Anaerobic Respiration** has all three parts. It just uses something like Nitrate ($NO_3^-$) instead of Oxygen as the "catcher."

**Fermentation** has none of these. It stops after Glycolysis. There is no Krebs cycle, no ETC, and no membrane-bound proton pumping.


**Glycolysis** the first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration, breaking down glucose into pyruvate and producing a net of 2 ATP and 2 NADH molecules. Glycolysis doesn't require oxygen. However, for glycolysis to continue, the NAD⁺ that is reduced to NADH during the process must be regenerated back to NAD⁺.
 In aerobic respiration, this **regeneration happens when NADH donates its electrons to the electron transport chain**.

In the absence of oxygen, the electron transport chain cannot function efficiently or at all in most organisms. This is where fermentation comes in. **The primary role of fermentation is not to produce more ATP directly, but to regenerate NAD⁺ from NADH.**
 This regenerated NAD⁺ can then be used in glycolysis, allowing glycolysis to continue producing a small amount of ATP.


The specific pathway that pyruvate follows after glycolysis in anaerobic conditions depends on the **organism and the enzymes it possesses**. The two most common types of fermentation are:

- *alcoholic fermentation* 
- *lactic acid fermentation*

These pathways utilize pyruvate (or a molecule derived from pyruvate) as the electron acceptor to oxidize NADH back to NAD⁺


#### *Lactic Acid Fermentation*

This type of fermentation mostly happens in humans and non-human animals where NADH directly reduces pyruvate to produce lactic acid and NAD+ as a result.

 **Enzyme:** Lactate dehydrogenase

$$
\text{Pyruvate} + NADH \rightarrow \text{Lactate} + NAD^+
$$

- Pyruvate accepts electrons
- NADH is oxidized back to NAD⁺
