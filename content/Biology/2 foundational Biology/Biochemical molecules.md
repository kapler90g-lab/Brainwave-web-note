# **Biochemical molecules**

> **Biochemical molecules** are any chemical molecules that are used for the existence and survival of living organisms.

There are two **main** types of **biomolecules**. Those are:

  - **organic molecules**.
  - **inorganic molecules**.

> **Organic compounds** are generally defined as chemical compounds that contain *carbon-hydrogen bonds*. 

- While it's often said that organic compounds "must contain carbon," this is a simplification. The key is the presence of *carbon atoms bonded to hydrogen atoms, which forms the backbone of these molecules*. Organic compounds are typically associated with living organisms and their products, although they can also be synthesized in laboratories.

> **Inorganic compounds**, on the other hand, are generally defined as chemical compounds that do **not contain carbon-hydrogen bonds**.

 - Many inorganic compounds do contain carbon, such as water($H_2O$), carbon dioxide (CO2) and carbonates (like CaCO3), but **they lack the carbon-hydrogen framework characteristic of organic molecules**. Inorganic compounds are often found in non-living matter like rocks, minerals, and water.
 
organic chemistry is the study of carbon-based molecules, particularly those with C-H bonds. Inorganic chemistry covers everything else.

**Carbon has four valence electrons**, allowing it to form four stable *covalent bonds* with other atoms, including other carbon atoms. This ability to form multiple bonds and create chains, rings, and complex structures is fundamental to the diversity and complexity of organic molecules and is also the major reason why most life is carbon based.

The most abundant element in the human body by mass is *Oxygen*, accounting for roughly 65% of total body weight. This is primarily because the human body is about 60% water ($H_2O$), and oxygen atoms are significantly heavier than hydrogen atoms, the second most abundant by body weight is **carbon** then **hydrogen** then **nitrogen**. 

> **The "Big Four" (CHON):** Carbon, Hydrogen, Oxygen, and Nitrogen are the foundation of all biochemical structures.

## **Inorganic molecules**

In organic molecules as we said are molecules that do not contain a carbon-hydrogen bond. and water is a major example of inorganic molecule that is very critical for the existence of life.
  
### *Water*

**Water**, with the chemical formula **H₂O**, might seem simple, but its structure gives it unique properties that are fundamental to life. A water molecule consists of *one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms* hence why H₂O, and they react with 1:8 by mass and 1:2 by number to form water. 

like we said water is bonded by an intramolecular force called a *covalent bond* where the electrons are shared between them, hydrogen needs to fulfill the doublet rule to become stable while oxygen need to fulfill the octet rule to become stable, and because oxygen needs two electrons and hydrogen needs one, oxygen will bond and share its electrons with two hydrogen atoms fulfilling both of their rule, this results in a stable compound called **water**.

![[Pasted image 20260413193950.png]]

But the electrons shared between the hydrogen and the oxygen is not a shared equally, oxygen because it has a *stronger nuclear energy* pulling all the electrons in its vicinity towards the nucleus, so the electrons shared will be more in the vicinity of the oxygen than the hydrogen atoms. This means that oxygen has a higher **electronegativity** than hydrogen, creating a *weak negative charge* around the oxygen and as a result a *weak positive* around the hydrogen, we call them **partial positive($\delta ^+$) and partial negative($\delta ^-$) poles of the water molecule**, and as a result of the *asymmetrical geometrical shape* of a water molecule it creates a net dipole moment making a water molecule **polar**.

![[Pasted image 20260413195414.png|192]]

> Hence, *water is polar because it has polar covalent bonds and is asymmetrical*. 

The interactions between water molecule gives water one of the most important properties of it for life, Because water molecules are *polar*, *the partially positive hydrogen atom of one water molecule is attracted to the partially negative oxygen atom of a neighboring water molecule*. This attraction is called a **hydrogen bond**.
 Hydrogen bonds are weaker than covalent or ionic bonds, but they are crucial for many of water's special characteristics.
![[Pasted image 20260413201035.png]]

In liquid water, these hydrogen bonds are constantly forming, breaking, and reforming, creating a dynamic network of interconnected molecules. This network of hydrogen bonds is responsible for several of water's unique properties listed below.

#### *1. Water as a Solvent*

Water, as a **polar solvent**, readily dissolves ionic or polar solutes. How it dissolves is as follows: 
- the partially positive hydrogen atoms of water latch onto the negatively charged atoms of the solute, while the partially negative oxygen of water attracts the positively charged atoms of the solute. In doing so, water dissociates the covalent or ionic bonds of the solute, separating the solute’s atoms from each other.

![[Pasted image 20260413201417.png]]

However, **water can’t dissolve nonpolar solutes like oil or wax**, as they do not have the polar properties that water exploits to dissolve substances.

#### *2. High Specific Heat Capacity*

> **Specific heat capacity** is the amount of heat energy require to increase the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1k.

Water's ability to absorb a large amount of heat before its temperature rises significantly. A lot of the absorbed energy is used to break *hydrogen bonds*, rather than increasing the kinetic energy of the molecules. This helps moderate temperatures in living organisms and the environment.

#### *3. Heat Latent of Vaporization*

> **Latent heat of Vaporization** is the amount of heat energy require to change the phase of a substance from liquid into a gas.

The large amount of energy required to convert liquid water into water vapor. Breaking all the hydrogen bonds requires a lot of energy, which is why evaporation has a cooling effect. This is useful for organisms because they can use evaporation of water as a method of cooling without losing too much water. When water evaporates from the surface of the skin, it takes heat energy away from the surface, cooling the organism down, this is how  sweating works effectively.

#### *4. Density and Freezing Properties*

Unlike most substances, **water is less dense as a solid (ice) than as a liquid**. In ice, hydrogen bonds form a stable, crystalline lattice structure that spaces the molecules further apart than in liquid water. This is why ice floats, which is crucial for aquatic life in cold climates.

at low temperatures, water freezes to form ice. Water molecules are held further apart in ice, making it less dense than water.

This causes ice to float, forming an *insulating layer at the surface of ponds and lakes*. This means the water below this layer does not freeze, allowing organisms within the water to move and survive.

#### *5. High Surface Tension and Cohesion*

> The tendency of water molecules to stick together (via hydrogen bonds) is known as **cohesion**. Water also has a tendency to stick to other materials; this is known as **adhesion**. 

**Capillary Action**

This is the ability of water to flow upward against gravity in a narrow space, like a thin tube or the vascular tissue of plants (xylem). 

> It occurs because the *adhesive forces between water molecules and the walls of the tube are stronger than the cohesive forces between water molecules.* 

As water molecules adhere to the walls, they pull other water molecules up with them through cohesion. The narrower the tube, the higher the water will rise.

![[Pasted image 20260413203152.png]]

**Surface Tension**

when water molecules meet air they create a high surface tension. This forms a **skin-like structure at the surface of the water which is strong enough to support small organisms such as raft spiders**.

> At the surface of water, the water molecules are attracted to each other by **cohesion**. However, they are *only attracted to water molecules below and to the sides*, not to the air above. This creates a net inward force on the surface molecules, causing them to be pulled together and form a taut layer, like an invisible film. This surface tension allows small objects, like insects, to rest on the surface of water.

### *Inorganic Ions*

> **Inorganic ions** are a specific type of inorganic compound. *They are charged atoms or molecules that do not contain carbon-hydrogen bonds.* These ions are formed when an atom or molecule gains or loses electrons, resulting in a net electrical charge. 

 For example, *sodium* (Na) is a neutral atom, but when it loses an electron, it becomes a positively charged sodium ion (Na⁺). *Chloride* (Cl) is a neutral atom, but when it gains an electron, it becomes a negatively charged chloride ion (Cl⁻).

> **Minerals** refer to naturally occurring, *inorganic solid substances* with a defined chemical composition and crystal structure.

When we consume minerals, they are often broken down or dissolved, releasing the inorganic ions that can then be absorbed and utilized by our cells. Think of table salt, sodium chloride (NaCl), which is a mineral. When dissolved in water, it dissociates into sodium ions (Na⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻). so minerals are core source of inorganic ions.

> **Nutrients** are essential chemical substances found in food and drink that the body requires to function, grow, and maintain **good health**.

Nutritionists typically group all essential nutrients into six major categories:

- **Carbohydrates**: The primary energy source for your body and brain.

- **Proteins**: The "building blocks" used to repair tissues and create enzymes.

- **Fats (Lipids):** High-energy sources that also help you absorb certain vitamins.

- **Water**: Crucial for regulating temperature and transporting other nutrients through the blood.

- **Vitamins**: Organic compounds (like Vitamin C or D) that support metabolism and immune function.

- **Minerals**: Inorganic elements (like Calcium or Iron) essential for bone health and fluid balance.

While all of these are nutrients, they are divided by how much you need:

- **Macronutrients** Required in large amounts (grams). those are *carbohydrates, proteins, fats and water* . Their primary role is to provide energy (calories) and support growth.

- **Micronutrients** (Micros): Required in small amounts (milligrams or micrograms). They include *vitamins and minerals*. They act as catalysts to regulate metabolic processes, immunity, and overall health.

all minerals are *micronutrients* (needed in small amounts), but within that "small" category, there is still a hierarchy:

- **Macro-minerals** are actually measured in *milligrams* (mg). They are also called principal elements. These inorganic ions are required in the daily diet in an amount greater than 100 mg/day.
		e.g. calcium, sodium, chloride, magnesium, potassium, sulfur, phosphorus..etc 

- **Micro-minerals (Trace minerals)** are often measured in even smaller *micrograms* (mcg). These are also called trace elements. These inorganic ions are required in amounts less than 100 mg/day.
		e.g. Iron, zinc, iodine, Copper, manganese, selenium, etc

#### *Hydrogen Ions*

> These are the most important ions to maintain **balance and equilibrium in living systems**. They are released in the cells during various metabolic processes. They are found in the cytoplasm of cells as well as in the **intercellular fluid, blood, CSF and other body fluids.** 

- *The concentration of hydrogen* ions is measured in terms of pH which is a negative log of its concentration. pH is measured on a scale from 1 to 14. The value of pH is inverse relation with the hydrogen ions concentration i.e. the higher is the pH, the lower is the concentration of hydrogen ions. 

- *Enzyme action*: The fluids in the human body has a normal pH of around 7.4. The maintenance of normal pH is essential for all the metabolic processes taking place in a cell. **Any changes in the normal pH affects the enzyme structure and renders them ineffective**. The abnormal amount of hydrogen ions interact with the side chains of amino acids and change their secondary and tertiary structures, causing denaturation of enzymes. \

- *ATP synthesis*: Hydrogen ions are also used for ATP synthesis within the cells. They are moved against the concentration gradient, from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembranous space, using the energy of electrons. As they return to the mitochondrial matrix, they release energy that is used to phosphorylate ADP to ATP by ATP synthase. 

- *Oxygen delivery*: Hydrogen ions present in the blood play a role in oxygen delivery to the cells by the hemoglobin. When blood passes through tissues, the carbon dioxide produced by cells during metabolism enters the blood. It reacts with water to generate hydrogen ions. As the concentration of hydrogen ions increases, they bind to amino acid side chains, decreasing the oxygen affinity of hemoglobin. This causes hemoglobin to release oxygen in tissues. The reverse of this happens in the lungs. This specific effect of hydrogen on hemoglobin’s oxygen-carrying capacity is called **Bohr’s effect**. 


##### Clinical conditions

 **Acidosis**
- *It is a condition in which the pH of the blood falls below the normal i.e. hydrogen ions concentration becomes greater than normal*. It may be metabolic or respiratory. The signs and symptoms depend on the underlying reason. Death may ensue in a short time if pH falls way below the normal level as all the enzymes top working and proteins of the body are denatured. 

**Alkalosis**
- *It is a condition in which the pH of blood becomes greater than the normal pH* i.e. the concentration of hydrogen ions decreases below the normal value. It may also be due to metabolic or respiratory causes. Severe alkalosis is always fatal due to the denaturation of enzymes and proteins. 


#### *Sodium Ions*

> **It the second most important positively charged ion present in our body**. *The concentration of sodium ions is higher in extracellular fluids as compared to the cytoplasm of the cells*. It is a principal element that must be taken in diet for the normal functioning of the human body. 

##### Body Requirements and sources

Sodium requirement for an average human being is *around 5 to 10 grams per day*. It is usually taken in the form of common-salt present in our foods. The usual daily intake of sodium is greater than its requirements. Extra sodium is excreted by the body urines. In patients of hypertension, less intake of slats is required to maintain a normal blood pressure. 


The normal concentration of sodium ions in our blood is around 135 to 145 mEq/l. *It is an extracellular cation present abundantly in body fluids but being in less concentration within the cells*. The following are some of the important functions performed by sodium in the human body.



- *Fluid Balance*: Being an abundant extracellular cation., sodium plays an essential role in maintaining the fluid balance in our body. The volume of extracellular fluids like blood is determined by the concentration of sodium in these fluids. *As sodium ions are pumped out of the cell, water follows, and the fluid volume increases. If the concentration of sodium decreases, water moves into the cells and the fluid volume decreases.* 

- *Conduction of Nerve impulses*: Sodium ions along with the potassium ions are involved in the conduction of nerve impulses in our body. Nerve impulses are conducted in the form of a wave of depolarization that travels along the nerves. During this process, sodium ions move into the nerve and depolarize the cells. If the concentration of sodium on either side of the nerve cell membrane is disturbed, nerve impulses cannot be conducted appropriately. 

- *Absorption of Nutrients in Intestine*: Sodium ions are required for the absorption of glucose and amino acids in the small intestine. These molecules are absorbed into the intestinal cells via co-transport channels that are driven by sodium ions. For this purpose, sodium ions are first actively transported out of the intestinal cells into the lumen. The energy stored in the concentration gradient is then used to drive the sodium-glucose or sodium-amino acid cotransporters.  


- *Heart Contraction*: Sodium ions are involved in initiating and controlling the rhythmic contractions of the heart. The rhythmic nerve impulses are continuously generated in the SA node without any stimulus due to the slow influx of sodium ions. The SA node is the pacemaker of the heart controlling the heart rate. Any alterations in the normal concentration of sodium ions will result in abnormal heart rate. 

##### Clinical conditions

**Hyponatremia**
- *It is a condition in which the concentration of sodium ions falls below the normal levels in blood*. It may be due to loss of electrolytes from the body as happens in diarrhea or vomiting. It is characterized by decreased blood pressure leading to circulatory failure. 

**Hypernatremia**
- *In this condition, sodium ions are present in blood in excess concentrations*. It may be due to hormonal diseases like Cushing’s syndrome, dehydration caused by loss of water as in diabetes insipidus, or rapid IV administration of sodium salts. Hypernatremia is characterized by an increase in blood volume and blood pressure. 


#### *Potassium Ions*


> **These are also positively charged ions abundantly present in our body**. contrary to the sodium ions, potassium ions are the major *intracellular cations*, being in higher concentration within the cytoplasm of the cells.


##### Body requirements and Sources

Potassium ions also fall in the category of principal elements and must be taken in daily diet. **An average human being must intake 3 to 4 grams of potassium daily**. It is abundantly present in fruits like bananas, oranges, vegetables like potatoes, and in meat like that of chicken and liver. 


The intracellular concentration of potassium is around 136 mEq/l. However, its concentration in extracellular fluid is very low, the plasma concentration being as low as 3.5 to 5 mEq/l. Its concentration in plasma is much raised during hemolysis of RBCs. The following are some of the key functions performed by potassium ions in our body. 

- *Osmotic Pressure Maintenance*: Potassium ions serve the same function in maintaining intracellular fluid volume and osmotic pressure, as performed by sodium ions in the case of extracellular fluid. When the sodium ions enter the cells, water also moves in. Thus, water and fluid balance is maintained within the cells. 

- *Electrolytes and Acid-Base Balance*: Potassium ions are involved in maintaining the electrolyte and acid-base balance within the cells. As they are the major cations present within the cells, their positive charge neutralizes the negative charges of organic ions and proteins and thus, cell remains neutral as a whole. If the concentration of potassium ions falls within the cells, hydrogen ions will rush into the cells to maintain electrical neutrality. This will decrease the pH of the cell, disrupting the acid-base balance. 

- *Nerve impulse Transmission*: Potassium ions are also required for the proper conduction of nerve impulses. The outflux of potassium ions in the 4th phase of nerve impulse repolarizes the cell, restoring the negative charge inside the membrane. It is essential for preparing the cell to conduct additional impulses once the first impulse is over. 

- *Cardiac contractions*: Potassium ions are also involved in repolarizing cardiac muscles after they have undergone contraction. Any changes in extracellular potassium ions concentration severely affects the cardiac contractions and can result in cardiac arrest. 

##### Clinical conditions

**Hypokalemia**
- It is characterized by less than normal concentration of potassium ions in the blood. It may be due to hormonal imbalance or administration of potassium fluids in blood. It can result in irritability and tachycardia leading to cardiomegaly and cardiac arrest. 

**Hyperkalemia**
- It is the increased concentration of potassium ions in blood. It mostly occurs due to renal failure or hormonal imbalance. Hyperkalemia can cause mental confusion, depression of the CAN and bradycardia ultimately leading to cardiac arrest. 

#### *Calcium ions*

> **Calcium is the most abundant inorganic ion present in our body having great importance**. It is a positively charged ion majorly present in the cytoplasm of cells. Bones and teeth are the major organs having a lot of calcium.


##### Body requirements and Sources

Calcium requirements of a healthy human are around 800 mg/day. Milk and milk products are the ideal sources of calcium in our diet. Besides, leafy vegetables, egg yolk, fish, and beans, etc. also contain a lot of calcium in them. 

*Calcium makes around 1 to 1.5 kg of our body weight*. Although 99% of calcium is present in bones and teeth, around 1% calcium in the cytoplasm of other cells and extracellular fluid performs several important functions in our body. calcium concentration in plasma is around 9-11 mg/dl. Here are some of the major functions of calcium in the human body. 

- *Development*: Calcium is needed along with phosphate ions for the formation, growth, and development of bones and teeth. It provides strength to the skeletal tissues.

- *Muscle contraction*: Calcium ions are needed for normal muscle contraction. When the nerve impulse reaches a muscle fiber, calcium ions are released from its sarcoplasmic reticulum. These calcium ions bind to troponin C, removing the tropomyosin from myosin-binding sites on actin. As a result, actin-myosin cross-bridges are formed and contraction of muscle fiber takes place. 


##### Clinical conditions

**Hypercalcemia**
- It is an increase in calcium ion concentration and is usually associated with hyperparathyroidism. It is characterized by lethargy, muscle weakness, loss of appetite, tachycardia and increased risk of fractures. 

**Hypocalcemia** 
- It is a decrease in calcium ions within the body and is usually associated with hypoparathyroidism. It can cause tetany, muscle irritability, and convulsions. 

#### *Phosphate ions*

The plasma concentration of phosphate ions is around 3-4 mg/dl. It is more abundantly present in the cells. Here are some of its important functions in the human body.

- *ATP synthesis*: Phosphate ions are required for the synthesis of high energy compounds like ATP, GTP, etc. The bonds between the phosphate ions in ATP release a high amount of energy when they are broken. 

- *Nucleic acid synthesis*: Phosphate ions are one of the three components of nucleotides. Thus, they are involved in the synthesis of nucleic acids like DNA and RNA. 

- *Protein and Enzyme activation*: Phosphate ions are needed for the activation of various enzymes and proteins. They become activated upon phosphorylation by kinases. 


#### *Chloride ions*

Chloride ions perform their role in synergy with the sodium ions. they are involved in maintaining the osmotic pressure, fluid balance and acid-base balance in our body just like sodium.

#### *Disaccharides*

> **Disaccharides** are formed when two monosaccharides join together hence the name.

- This linkage occurs through a **glycosidic bond**, which is a type of covalent bond formed by a *condensation reaction* (also known as dehydration synthesis). In this reaction, a molecule of water is removed as the two monosaccharides are linked.
- When two monosaccharides join, a hydroxyl group (OH) on the first carbon of one monosaccharide reacts with a hydroxyl group (OH) on the forth caron of another monosaccharide. This forms a *1,4 glycosidic bond.*, and a water molecule (H2O) is released. 

![[Pasted image 20260608082006.png]]

Here is the *disaccharides* formed when the monosaccharides react through condensation reaction.

- **Glucose** + **Glucose** -> **Maltose**($C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}$) + water (H2O), maltose is a product of starch digestion.
- **Glucose** + **Galactose** -> **Lactose** + water, which is a primary suger in milk.
- **Glucose** + **Fructose** -> **Sucrose** + water, which is a common table sugar.

The glycosidic bond can also be differentiated based on weather it happen between an alpha and a beta glucose molecules, here are some of the polymer made by hundreds of glucose molecules.

##### Cellulose

> Cellulose is a polysaccharide formed from *beta-glucose*. Its primary use is to provide structural support for plant cell walls.

- Cellulose is made up of many beta-glucose monomers joined together via glycosidic bonds. However, if two beta-glucose monomers line up next to each other, the hydroxyl groups on carbon 1 and carbon 4 are too far from each other to react. 

![[Pasted image 20260608084728.png|412]]

- To fix this, every other beta-glucose molecule is inverted by 180° (flipped upside down). This brings the hydroxyl groups (OH) close enough together to react.

![[Pasted image 20260608084831.png]]
When many beta-glucose monomers join together they form long, straight, unbranched chains. The alternating inversion of the beta glucose molecules also allows for hydrogen bonds to form between individual chains. Although each hydrogen bond itself is relatively weak, the huge number of these bonds provides great strength to cellulose as a whole.
![[Pasted image 20260608084950.png]]

Multiple cellulose chains become tightly cross linked via hydrogen bonds to form bundles called microfibrils. These microfibrils join together to make macrofibrils which combine to make strong cellulose fibres in the plant cell wall.

![[Pasted image 20260608085052.png]]

Carbohydrates perform several crucial functions in living organisms:

- **Primary Energy Source**: Carbohydrates, particularly glucose, are the main fuel source for cells. Organisms break down carbohydrates through cellular respiration to release energy (ATP) for metabolic processes.

- **Energy Storage**: Organisms store excess carbohydrates for later use. In plants, this is often in the form of starch, and in animals, it's glycogen.

- **Structural Support**: Some carbohydrates provide structural support. For example, cellulose is a major component of plant cell walls, providing rigidity and strength. Chitin, found in the exoskeletons of insects and crustaceans, is another structural carbohydrate.

- **Allow cell to cell communications** through glycoproteins and glycolipids found on the surface of the cell membrane they allow cells to communicate and signal one another.

### *lipids*  

> Lipids are a diverse group of organic compounds that are characterized by their *insolubility in water*.

- Unlike carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids, *lipids are not typically polymers formed from repeating monomer subunits in the same way*. Instead, they are defined by their **hydrophobic** (water-fearing) nature, meaning they do not mix with water but are soluble in nonpolar organic solvents like oils, ether, chloroform, hexane and more.

The primary elements that make up lipids are also **Carbon (C), Hydrogen (H), and Oxygen (O**), similar to carbohydrates, but with a much lower proportion of oxygen and some lipids contain phosphorus and nitrogen.

Despite their insolubility in water, lipids perform a wide array of essential functions in living organisms. Their hydrophobic nature is actually key to many of these roles:

- **Energy Storage**: Lipids, particularly triglycerides (fats and oils), are a highly efficient form of long-term energy storage. They store more energy per gram than carbohydrates.
- **Cell Membrane Structure**: Phospholipids are the primary components of cell membranes, forming a bilayer that regulates the passage of substances into and out of the cell.
- **Insulation and Protection**: Fat layers in animals provide insulation against cold and protect organs from physical shock.
- **Hormone production:** Steroid lipids, like cholesterol, are precursors for important hormones such as sex hormones (estrogen, testosterone) and adrenal hormones.
- **Absorption of Fat-Soluble Vitamins**: Dietary lipids are necessary for the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K) in the digestive tract.

In humans and most animals, fat is primarily stored in specialized cells called **adipocytes**, which collectively form **adipose tissue**. Adipose tissue is found in various locations throughout the body, serving as an energy reserve and providing insulation and cushioning

- **Subcutaneous Tissue**: Fat stored beneath the skin, providing insulation and energy storage.

- **Visceral Fat**: Fat stored around internal organs in the abdominal cavity. Excess visceral fat is associated with increased health risks.

- **Muscles**: Muscles store small amounts of triglycerides that can be used as an energy source during activity.

#### *Types and Structures of fatty acids*

Many lipids are built upon the foundation of **fatty acids**. 

> **A fatty acid** is a long *hydrocarbon chain* with a *carboxyl group (-COOH)* at one end. The length of the hydrocarbon chain can vary, but it typically contains between 15 and 17 carbon atoms.

![[Pasted image 20260608103138.png]]
fatty acids based on how much of hydrogen they contain in their hydrocarbon chain can be classified as **saturated and Unsaturated fatty acid**.

##### Saturated fatty acids

- These have hydrocarbon chains that are 'saturated' with hydrogen, meaning all carbon atoms are bonded to the **maximum number of hydrogen atoms**.
- The hydrocarbon chain has no **carbon-carbon double bonds**. 
- Tend to be straight and can pack tightly together.
- Lipids that contain saturated fatty acids have higher melting points and so are usually solid at room temperature (fats).

![[Pasted image 20260608103345.png|471]]


##### Unsaturated fatty acids 

- These have hydrocarbon chains that do not contain the **maximum number of hydrogen atoms bonded to the carbon atoms** hence not completely saturated with hydrogen.
- The hydrocarbon chain has at least one carbon-carbon double bond, which causes the *chain to kink or bend*.
- Lipids that contain unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points and so are usually liquid at room temperature (oils).
- Unsaturated fatty acids may either be *monounsaturated or polyunsaturated*: **Monounsaturated meaning containing only one double bond**. and **Polyunsaturated containing two or more double bonds**.

![[Pasted image 20260608103557.png|512]]
Saturated fatty acids can pack closely together, allowing for stronger **van der Waals forces** between molecules, which requires more energy (higher temperature) to overcome, making them solid. The kinks in unsaturated fatty acids prevent close packing, reducing the strength of these forces, so they remain liquid at room temperature.
#### *Triglycerides*

> **triglyceride** is a type of lipid used as a store of energy in animals, plants, and some bacteria.

![[Pasted image 20260608103928.png|514]]
A triglyceride consists of a *glycerol backbone attached to three fatty acid tails*. Each fatty acid tail contains a hydrocarbon chain (R) which can vary in length and may be saturated or unsaturated. if saturated then we call them **fats**, if unsaturated we call them **oils**.

Triglycerides are synthesised via condensation reactions and broken down via hydrolysis reactions. These reactions involve the formation or the breakdown of covalent bonds known as **ester bonds**.

- **The hydroxyl groups (OH) on the glycerol and on the three fatty acids** react together to release three water molecules (H2O).
- This results in three *ester bonds* between the glycerol and the fatty acids.

![[Pasted image 20260608104124.png|600]]

#### *Phospholipids*

> ﻿A **phospholipid** is a type of lipid used as a structural component of the cell membrane. 

A phospholipid molecule has a glycerol backbone, similar to a triglyceride. However, instead of having three fatty acids attached, a phospholipid has:

- **Glycerol Backbone**: A three-carbon molecule that serves as the structural base.

- **Two Fatty Acid Tails**: These are long hydrocarbon chains that are hydrophobic. They can be saturated or unsaturated.

- **A Phosphate Group**: A negatively charged group attached to the third carbon of the glycerol. This group is hydrophilic (water-loving).

- **Often, a Choline or other Head Group**: Another molecule is often attached to the phosphate group, which is also typically hydrophilic.

![[Pasted image 20260608104638.png|315]]

This combination of a hydrophilic head (the phosphate and head group) and a hydrophobic tail (the fatty acids) makes phospholipids **amphipathic**. This means they have both a water-loving and a water-fearing part. This amphipathic nature is key to their function in forming cell membranes.

When phospholipids are placed in an aqueous (watery) environment, they spontaneously arrange themselves to minimize contact between their hydrophobic tails and the water, while allowing their hydrophilic heads to interact with the water. This results in the formation of a **phospholipid bilayer**.

![[Pasted image 20260608104755.png|545]]

The lipid family is diverse, including triglycerides, phospholipids, steroids, waxes, and more, each with unique structures and roles in biological systems.

### *Proteins*

> **Proteins** are large, complex biomolecules that perform a vast array of functions in living organisms. They are often called the "*workhorses*" of the cell because they are involved in **virtually every process, from catalyzing reactions to providing structural support and transporting molecules**.

Proteins are **polymers**, and their building blocks are smaller molecules called *amino acids*. There are **20 common types of amino acids** that are used to build proteins. The specific sequence and arrangement of these amino acids determine the unique structure and function of a protein.

#### *Amino acids*

> **Amino acids** are monomer unit of proteins, containing an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a unique side chain (R-group) attached to a central carbon atom.

All 20 common amino acids share a basic general structure.This structure includes a **central carbon atom**, called the *alpha-carbon (α-carbon),* to which four different groups are attached:

- **Amino Group (-NH₂)** - An amino group.

- **Carboxyl Group(-COOH)** - A carboxyl group (acidic).

- **Hydrogen Atom(-H)** - A single hydrogen atom.

- **R-Group (Side Chain)**: This is the variable part of the amino acid that is unique to each of the 20 types. The R-group determines the chemical properties of the amino acid.
![[Pasted image 20260608123718.png]]
As you can see in the diagram, the alpha-carbon is at the center. **The amino group and carboxyl group are the constant parts of all amino acids**. The R-group is what differentiates one amino acid from another. These R-groups can be *simple (like a single hydrogen atom in glycine) or complex, and they can be nonpolar, polar uncharged, acidic (negatively charged), or basic (positively charged)*. The nature of the R-groups is crucial for how the protein folds and functions.


Amino acids are joined together in a specific order to form a chain called a **polypeptide**. The bond that links two amino acids is called a covalent bond called a **peptide bond**. This bond is formed through a *condensation reaction*, similar to the formation of glycosidic bonds in carbohydrates, where a molecule of water is removed.

![[Pasted image 20260608124008.png|637]]


When two amino acids join, the hydroxyl (OH) in the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the hydrogen (H) in the amino group of another amino acid. This releases a water molecule (H2O) and forms a *peptide bond between the carbon of one amino acid and the nitrogen of another.* A chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds is a **polypeptide**. A protein can consist of one or more polypeptide chains folded into a specific three-dimensional structure.

Protein is an essential nutrient for humans, as we need to obtain certain essential amino acids from our diet because our bodies cannot synthesize them. 


According to their shape proteins can be classified into:

- **Globular proteins**: this are proteins whose molecules curl up into a ball like shape such as hemoglobin and myoglobin. Globular proteins are compact, spherical, and soluble proteins. they curl up as a result of the hydrophobic molecules being pushed into the center away from any watery environment while the weak and hydrophilic parts are pushed outside.

![[Pasted image 20260608124937.png|194]]
Globular proteins tend to have metabolic roles in the body, such as:
- **Enzymes** - These speed up chemical reactions in the body.
- **Hormones** - These are chemical messengers that travel in the bloodstream.
- **Antibodies** - These bind to and prevent the spread of pathogens.


- **Fibrous proteins**: are protein molecules that do not coil up into a ball but form a long stand. Fibrous proteins form long strands and are not usually soluble in water. 

![[Pasted image 20260608125249.png|393]]


Fibrous proteins tend to have structural roles in the body, such as: 

- **Keratin** - This is found in the skin, hair, and nails.
- **Collagen** - This is found in connective tissues such as bone and muscle.
- **Elastin** - This is found in elastic connective tissue.


#### *Major functions of proteins*

-  **Structural proteins**: are fibrous and tough, as well as insoluble in water. They are structural elements of connective tissues, bones,tendons, cartilages, nails, hair, and horns. Collagen, elastin, and keratin are examples of structural proteins.

- **Enzymes**: are globular proteins that serve as biological catalysts. They catalyse metabolic reactions by lowering the activation energy, which increases the reaction rate. Protein enzymes include DNA polymerase, lysozyme, nitrogenase, and lipase.

-  **Hormones**: are polypeptides that are made up of long chains of linked amino acids. They play critical roles in the regulation of the body's physiological processes, which include reproduction, growth and development, electrolyte balance, sleep, and so on. Growth hormone (GH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) are two examples of these hormones.

-  **Respiratory pigments**: are globular protein pigments that are typically water-soluble. Myoglobin, which provides oxygen to working muscles; and haemoglobin, which transports blood to all tissues and organs via the blood, is the two examples.

- **Transport proteins**: are cell membrane structural components They create channels in the plasma membrane to transport specific molecules within the cells.  channel proteins, and carrier proteins are examples of transport proteins.

-  **Motor proteins**: are involved in muscle contraction and relaxation (muscle movement). Actin, myosin, kinesin, and dynein are all components.

- **Storage proteins**: In cells, these proteins serve as a storage reserve for amino acids and metal ions. They can be found in eggs, seeds, and pulses. Ferritin, ovalbumin, and casein are examples of storage proteins.

-  **Toxins**: Bacteria are the most common producers of these proteins. Diphtheria toxin, Pseudomonas exotoxin, and ribosome- inactivating proteins are among them. By causing cytotoxicity, they aid bacteria in attacking and killing their host organism.


#### *Testing for Proteins*

Several chemical tests can be used to indicate the presence of proteins, often relying on reactions with specific parts of the amino acid or the peptide bond, resulting in a visible color change.

**Biuret Test**: The Biuret test is a common method used to detect the presence of peptide bonds, which are the linkages between amino acids in a protein or polypeptide chain. Therefore, this test is positive for proteins and polypeptides but generally negative for free amino acids or dipeptides.

- In an alkaline solution, copper(II) ions (Cu²⁺) in the Biuret reagent mixes with the peptide bonds, forming a **purple or violet color**. At least two peptide bonds are typically required for a positive test.


**Xanthoproteic Test**: The Xanthoproteic test is used to detect the presence of amino acids that contain an aromatic ring(**aromatic amino acids**), specifically *tyrosine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine*. While not a test for the peptide bond itself, these amino acids are common in many proteins, so a positive result often indicates the presence of protein.

- Concentrated **nitric acid** reacts with the aromatic rings in tyrosine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine, causing nitration and producing a **yellow color**. Adding an alkali like NaOH in excess intensifies the color to **Orange**.


| Test                   | Observation                                                             | Interpretation                                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Biuret test**        | changes from blue to purple/violet                                      | Protein (or polypeptide) is present. no color change indicates absence.                                                             |
| **Xanthoproteic Test** | Yellow color upon heating with HNO₃, turns orange with alkali like NaOH | Protein containing aromatic amino acids (tyrosine, tryptophan, phenylalanine) is likely present. no color change indicates absence. |
Keep in mind that the Xanthoproteic test is more specific to the presence of aromatic amino acids and may not be positive for all proteins if they lack these aromatic residues. The Biuret test is a more general test for the presence of peptide bonds, making it a good indicator for the presence of most proteins and larger polypeptides.


### *Nucleic acid*

> **Nucleic acids** are large biomolecules that play essential roles in storing and transmitting genetic information, as well as in protein synthesis. The two main types of nucleic acids are deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA).

Similar to how amino acids are the building blocks of proteins, **nucleotides** are the fundamental monomer units that make up nucleic acids. Nucleic acids are polymers formed by linking many nucleotides together in a chain.

#### *Nucleotides*

> **Nucleotides** are the building blocks of nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA. Nucleotides are monomers and can join together to form dimers (dinucleotides) and polymers (polynucleotides).

- **A nucleic acid is the functional molecule made of one or more polynucleotide chains.**

Nucleotides are made up of three components:

- **A pentose sugar** - Contains 5 carbon atoms.
- **A nitrogenous base** - Contains carbon and nitrogen.
- **A phosphate group** - Contains phosphate

![[Pasted image 20260608140948.png|485]]
Nucleotides are joined together via *condensation reactions* to form a **polynucleotide**. The phosphate group of one nucleotide forms a covalent bond with the sugar of another. This forms a **phosphodiester bond**.

![[Pasted image 20260608141042.png|442]]
- Many nucleotides can join in this way to create a chain of phosphates and sugars known as the **sugar-phosphate backbone**. Phosphodiester bonds can be broken via hydrolysis reactions, releasing the nucleotide monomers.

#### *DNA*

> **Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)** is a type of nucleic acid that contains the instructions needed to make proteins.

![[Pasted image 20260608141225.png|357]]
Each DNA nucleotide is made up of three components:

- Deoxyribose - A pentose sugar.
- A, T, G, or C base - Adenine, thymine, guanine, or cytosine.
- A phosphate group

 two scientists, James Watson and Francis Crick were credited with working out the structure of DNA. With the help of other scientists like Rosalind Franklin, they found that DNA is made up of two polynucleotide strands wound around each other to form a **double helix**.

The following features allow DNA to pass genetic information from one generation to another: 

- **Sugar-phosphate backbone** - This protects coding bases on the inside of the helix.
- **Double stranded** - This allows strands to act as templates in DNA replication.
- **Large molecule** - It stores lots of information.
- **Double helix** - This makes the molecule compact and stable.
- **Complementary base pairing** - This allows accurate DNA replication.
- **Weak hydrogen bonds** - This allows strands to separate in DNA replication.

#### *RNA*

> **Ribonucleic acid (RNA**) is a type of nucleic acid that uses information from DNA to synthesise proteins.

![[Pasted image 20260608141540.png|445]]

Each RNA nucleotide is made up of three components: 
- Ribose - A pentose sugar.
- A, U, G, or C base - Adenine, uracil, guanine, or cytosine. 
- A phosphate group


Unlike DNA, RNA nucleotides contain the sugar ribose rather than deoxyribose. They also contain the base uracil in place of thymine (uracil pairs with adenine).

![[Pasted image 20260608141643.png|521]]RNA is a single stranded molecule made up of just one polynucleotide strand. These strands are much shorter than DNA strands.
