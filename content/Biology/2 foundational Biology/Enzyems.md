## **Definition of Enzymes**

- **Enzymes** are specialized protein molecules that function as *biological catalysts*, also referred to as **biocatalysts**. Their primary role is to accelerate the rate of a biochemical reactions within living organisms by significantly reducing the **activation energy** required for these reactions to occur.

>[!important] Catalyst 
>A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without itself being consumed or permanently altered in the process. Catalysts achieve this by lowering the activation energy required for the reaction to proceed, thereby making it easier for the reactants to transform into products.

- **Activation energy** is defined as the minimum amount of energy that must be supplied to reactant molecules in order to transform them into products during a chemical reaction.

     **Energy barrier:** For a reaction to occur, reactants must overcome the activation energy to break existing bonds and form new ones. If the barrier is high, most molecules won't have enough energy to react, and the reaction proceeds slowly.

- By lowering this energy barrier, enzymes ensure that reactions proceed more efficiently and at a much faster rate than they would in the absence of these catalysts

- Enzymes lower the activation energy of a reaction by providing an **alternative pathway** that requires less energy for the reactants to convert into products

- **All enzymes are composed of proteins (except ribozymes)** , which are macromolecules built from long chains of amino acids. These **amino acid** chains are linked together through a covalent bond called **peptide bond**, forming a specific three-dimensional structure that is critical to the enzyme's function

an **amino acid** is the basic building block of proteins. Each amino acid consists of:

1. An amino group (-NH₂).
2. A carboxyl group (-COOH).
3. A central carbon atom (the α-carbon).
4. A unique side chain (R-group) that determines the chemical properties of the amino acid.

When amino acids link together, they form proteins.

![[Pasted image 20250112121917.png|450]]

- A **peptide bond** is a covalent bond formed between the carboxyl group (-COOH) of one amino acid and the amino group (-NH₂) of another. This bond forms through a *[[Human biology and health#^5faffc|condensation reaction]]*  (releasing water).

    - Peptide bonds are **not hydrogen bonds**. Hydrogen bonds, however, can form between amino acids in a protein's structure. 


- There are **thousands of different enzymes** in a single cell. These enzymes are classified into **six main categories** based on the type of reaction they catalyze.
- All cells contain different enzymes depending on the type of the living cell
     - In **muscle cells**, enzymes involved in energy production (e.g., oxidoreductases like lactate dehydrogenase) are dominant.
    - In **digestive cells**, hydrolases like amylase, protease, and lipase dominate.
    - In **all cells**, essential enzymes for basic metabolism, like ATP synthase (energy production) and ribosomes (protein synthesis), are universal.

- **Metabolism** refers to all the biochemical reactions that occur in a cell or organism to maintain life. It can be divided into two broad categories:

    1. **Catabolism:** Breakdown of complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy. Example: Breaking down glucose in glycolysis.
    2. **Anabolism:** Synthesis of complex molecules from simpler ones, requiring energy. Example: Synthesizing proteins from amino acids.

- The metabolic processes in the cells require enzymes to catalyze many biochemical reaction types at a rates fast enough to sustain life.
- Enzymes act upon molecules (*substrates*), convert them into products of different molecules, and remain unchanged

## **properties and functions of enzymes**

### **Physical Properties of Enzymes**

1. **Denaturation**
    - **Definition:** Denaturation refers to the structural change in an enzyme that causes it to lose its functional shape and activity.
    - is the process of breaking the intra and inter-molecular non-covalent bonds that distort the shape and active site of the enzymes
    
    - **Causes:**
        - **Heat:** Most enzymes function optimally at a specific temperature (e.g., 37°C for human enzymes). Excess heat disrupts hydrogen bonds and other interactions, unfolding the enzyme.
        - **pH:** Extreme acidic or alkaline conditions can disrupt ionic bonds and alter the enzyme's charge, leading to loss of structure.
        - **Chemicals:** Alcohol, heavy metals, and detergents can disrupt the enzyme's structure.
    - **Effect:** Denatured enzymes cannot bind to their substrate and thus lose their catalytic ability.

---

2. **Solubility**
    - Enzymes are generally **soluble in water** due to their hydrophilic amino acid residues on the surface, which interact with water molecules.
    - Solubility depends on:
        - **pH:** At the *isoelectric point*, enzymes tend to **aggregate and precipitate**, leading to **reduced solubility** enzymes are the most soluble below or above there pI.
        - **Salt Concentration:** High salt concentrations (salting out) can precipitate enzymes by disrupting their hydration shell.
    - **Examples:** Digestive enzymes like amylase and lipase dissolve in water to act on substrates.

---

3. **Colloids**
    - Enzymes behave like **colloids** in solution, forming a dispersed phase with a large surface area relative to their volume.
    - This colloidal nature enhances:
        - Their interaction with substrates.
        - Stability and ability to remain suspended in solution rather than settling.
    - Colloid behavior allows enzymes to function effectively in the aqueous environment of the cell.

---

4. **Biocatalysts**
    - Enzymes are **biocatalysts**, meaning they:
        - Accelerate chemical reactions by lowering activation energy.
        - Are highly specific, acting on one type of substrate or reaction.
        - Operate under mild biological conditions (temperature, pH).
        - very small quantities or a small amount of enzyme is enough to convert a large quantity of substrate and remain unchanged after the reaction.
        
    - Enzymes differ from inorganic catalysts because they:
        - Are more efficient.
        - Can be regulated (e.g., feedback inhibition).
        - Are biodegradable and do not leave harmful residues.

---

5. **Precipitation**
    - Enzymes can be precipitated (separated out of solution) by:
        - **Salting out:** Adding high concentrations of salts like ammonium sulfate.
        - **Organic Solvents:** Ethanol or acetone reduces enzyme solubility, causing precipitation.
        - **pH Adjustment:** At their isoelectric point (pI), enzymes have no net charge and are least soluble, leading to precipitation.
    - **Uses:** Precipitation is a common step in enzyme purification.

---

6. **Molecular Weight**
    - Enzymes are macromolecules with high molecular weights, typically ranging from **10,000 to over 1 million Daltons**.
    - The molecular weight depends on the number of amino acids in the enzyme and whether it has multiple subunits.

    - High molecular weight is related to their complexity and the specific 3D structure required for activity.

---

7. **Enzyme Activity**
    - **Definition:** Enzyme activity refers to the rate at which an enzyme converts substrate to product.
    - **Factors Influencing Activity:**
        - **Temperature:** Activity increases with temperature up to an optimum level (e.g., 37°C for human enzymes). Beyond this, activity decreases due to denaturation.
        - **pH:** Enzymes have an optimum pH range (e.g., pepsin works best in acidic conditions, pH ~2).
        - **Substrate Concentration:** Activity increases with substrate concentration until the enzyme is saturated (Vmax).
        - **Cofactors/Coenzymes:** Some enzymes require non-protein helpers (e.g., metal ions or vitamins) for activity.
    - **Measurement:** Enzyme activity is often measured in terms of the amount of product formed per unit time (e.g., micromoles per minute).



### **Chemical Properties of Enzymes**

1. **Sensitivity**
    - Enzymes are highly sensitive to environmental conditions that can affect their activity.
    - **Temperature Sensitivity:**
        - Enzymes have an **optimum temperature**, beyond which activity decreases due to denaturation.
        - Example: Human enzymes work best around **37°C**.
    - **PH sensitivity:**
        - Each enzyme has an **optimum pH**. Deviations can alter the charge on amino acid residues in the active site, affecting binding and catalysis.
        - Example: Pepsin is active at pH 2 (stomach), while trypsin functions at pH 8 (small intestine).
    - **Chemical Sensitivity:**
        - Heavy metals (e.g., mercury, lead) and detergents can inhibit enzyme activity by binding to or disrupting the active site.

---

2. **Regulations**
    - Enzyme activity can be regulated to ensure metabolic pathways operate efficiently by *activator and inhibitor molecules.*

---

3. **Specificity**
    - Enzymes are highly specific in their action, interacting with only one or a few substrates.
    - is a property of the enzyme that describes how restrictive the enzyme is in its choice of substrate. 
    - A completely specific enzyme would have only one substrate
    
    - **Types of Specificity:**
    
        - **substrate Specificity:** Acts on a single substrate.
            - Example: Urease catalyzes only urea hydrolysis.
        - **Group Specificity:** Acts on a specific functional group.
            - Example: Alcohol dehydrogenase acts on alcohols.
        - **bond Specificity:** Acts on a specific bond type.
            - Example: Proteases cleave peptide bonds.
        - **Optical specificity:** refers to an enzyme's ability to recognize and catalyze reactions involving specific **optical isomers** (also known as **enantiomers**) of a substrate. 
            - *Optical isomers* are molecules that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, much like how your left and right hands are mirror images but cannot be perfectly aligned
        - **Co-factor specificity:** refers to an enzyme's requirement for **cofactors** to carry out its catalytic activity and the specific interaction with particular types of cofactors.
            - **Cofactors** are non-protein molecules that bind to an enzyme to help it catalyze reactions.

---

4. **Catalysis**
    - is the process of the acceleration of a chemical reaction by a catalyst.
	- Enzymes act as **catalysts**, speeding up reactions by lowering the activation energy without being consumed in the process.
    - **Mechanism of Catalysis:**
        - Enzymes bind to the substrate at the **active site** to form an enzyme-substrate complex.
            - They can transform about 100-10,000 substrates per second
        - After the reaction, the enzyme is released unchanged and can catalyze more reactions.
    - **Example:** Carbonic anhydrase speeds up the conversion of carbon dioxide and water to bicarbonate and hydrogen ions $10^3-10^8$ times faster than the non-catalyzed reaction.

---

5. **Reversibility of Reactions**
    - Many enzyme-catalyzed reactions are **reversible**, meaning they can proceed in both directions depending on the conditions (e.g., concentration of substrates and products).
    
    - Factors affecting reversibility:
        - **Thermodynamics:** The reaction proceeds in the direction that minimizes free energy.
        - **Enzyme Availability:** Some enzymes are more active in one direction based on their role in the pathway.
    - enzymatic reactions catalyze biochemical reactions in both forward and reverse directions.

---
# **functions of enzymes** 

Enzymes are **biological catalysts** that speed up chemical reactions in living organisms without being consumed. They play a critical role in processes like:

- **Respiration**: Breaking down glucose for energy.
- **Digestion**: Breaking down food into nutrients for absorption.
- **Liver function**: Detoxifying harmful substances.
- **Muscle and nerve function**: Supporting essential body systems.

Each cell in your body contains thousands of enzymes, each specialized for specific chemical reactions.

<div class="ai-divider"></div>

The **turnover number** , often denoted as $k_{\text{cat}}$, is a measure of the enzyme's catalytic efficiency. It represents the **maximum number of substrate molecules that a single enzyme molecule can convert into product per unit of time**, under conditions where the enzyme is saturated with substrate.


The turnover number can be calculated using the equation:
$$k_{\text{cat}} = \frac{V_{\text{max}}}{[E_t]}$$
Where:
- $V_{\text{max}}$ = Maximum reaction velocity (rate at substrate saturation).
- $[E_t]$ = Total enzyme concentration.

**it tell us about it's:**

1. **Catalytic Efficiency**: $k_{\text{cat}}$ indicates how quickly an enzyme can process its substrate once the enzyme-substrate complex is formed.
2. **Comparison Between Enzymes**: It allows comparison of the efficiency of different enzymes. A high $k_{\text{cat}}$ suggests the enzyme is very efficient at catalyzing the reaction.
3. **Biological Role**: Turnover numbers can provide insights into the enzyme's role in the cell. Enzymes with high turnover numbers are typically involved in rapid processes, like breaking down toxins or metabolic regulation.

 **Example**:
 
For **catalase**, an enzyme that decomposes hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen:
- $k_{\text{cat}}$ = ~40 million per second.
This means one molecule of catalase can convert ~40 million molecules of hydrogen peroxide into product every second, showcasing its incredible efficiency.

1. **Active Site**:
    
    - A unique region on the enzyme where the substrate binds.
    - The shape of the active site is specific to the substrate (like a lock-and-key).
    - Once the substrate binds, the enzyme converts it into a product.
    ![[Pasted image 20250121192746.png]]

2. **Substrate Binding**:
    
    - The substrate (e.g., sucrose) binds to the enzyme's active site, forming an **enzyme-substrate complex**.
3. **Catalysis**:
    
    - The enzyme induces stress on the substrate bond (e.g., between glucose and fructose) to break it down.
4. **Reaction**:
    
    - The substrate is broken into its components (e.g., glucose and fructose), and water is involved in the process.
5. **Product Release**:
    
    - The enzyme releases the products and is ready to bind new substrates.

### **Examples of Enzymes and Their Functions**

| **Enzyme**               | **Function**                                                             |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Lipases**              | Break down fats into fatty acids in blood, gastric juices, and tissues.  |
| **Amylase**              | Converts starches (like those in saliva) into sugars.                    |
| **Maltase**              | Found in saliva; breaks maltose (from foods like potatoes) into glucose. |
| **Trypsin**              | Breaks proteins into amino acids in the small intestine.                 |
| **Lactase**              | Converts lactose (milk sugar) into glucose and galactose.                |
| **Helicase**             | Unwinds DNA strands.                                                     |
| **DNA Polymerase**       | Forms new copies of DNA during cell replication.                         |
| **Acetylcholinesterase** | Breaks down acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter in nerves and muscles.     |
