## **The Microscope**

> **The Microscope** is an optical or electronic instrument used by biologists and other scientists to study living and non-living things at the nanoscale.

>[!note]- **The history of the microscope**
>**Robert Hooke**, an English natural philosopher, published his groundbreaking book _Micrographia_ in 1665. This work contained detailed drawings of various specimens viewed under his compound microscope, including the famous illustration of **cork** in which he observed tiny pores he called "**cells**" – a term that would later become fundamental to biology. At the time, he did not realize that these cells were part of living organisms — the cork’s cells were long dead.
> ![[Pasted image 20250804161945.png|300]]
> Around the same time, **Antoine van Leeuwenhoek**, a Dutch scientist, began grinding his own lenses and creating simple, yet powerful, single-lens microscopes. With his exceptionally high-quality lenses, van Leeuwenhoek was the first to observe and describe a world of **microscopic life**, including bacteria, yeast, and single-celled organisms (which he called "**animalcules**"). His meticulous observations, communicated through letters to the Royal Society of London, earned him the title of the "**Father of Microbiology**."

- There are two main types of microscopes those are 
     - **light microscope**
     - **Electron microscope**
- **Light microscope** also known as **optical microscope** uses beams of light to magnify and form the image an object.
- while, **electron microscope** uses beams of **electrons** to magnify and form the image of an object,
- When designing a microscope there two main abilities it must have: being able to magnify and being able to create a detailed and quality image or simply **magnification** and **resolution**.

### *Magnification and resolving power*

- **Magnification** is the increase in the apparent size of an object, it process of enlarging the apparent size of something, not the physical size.
- The best Light microscope can magnify up to 2000x the size of the original object meaning a $1m^2$ square under a **Light Microscope** can be magnified to a $4000m^2$ square.
- an **electron microscope** can magnify 2,000,000x the original size of an object, Using electron microscopes makes it possible for us to learn a lot more about cells and the ways in which they become specialised for particular functions.
![[Pasted image 20250804165043.png|500]]
To calculate magnification, you need to know the image size and the object size:

- **Image size** - How large the object appears when you view it through a microscope.
- **Object size** (actual size) - The real size of the object.

**The equation for calculating magnification is:**

$$\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{Image size}}{\text{Object size}}$$
- **Resolution** is the ability of a Microscope to distinguish two separate points on a specimen and determines the quality and detail of the image that is being produced.  
- it is the ability of a microscope to distinguish between two closely situated objects as separate entities.
	- it is the intrinsic property of a microscopes.

![[Pasted image 20250804165309.png|500]]

- **The resolving power** of a microscope is often defined as the smallest distance between two separate points that can still be distinguished as separate points by the microscope.
	-  the resolving power of a **light microscope** about 200nm meaning light Microscope can distinguish two points separated by a distance 200nm or above.
	- the resolving power of an **electron microscope** is about 0.5nm meaning it has a higher resolving power than light microscope showing more finer details
### *Light Microscope*

> **light microscope** also known as optical microscope uses beams of light to magnify and form the image of an object

- Biologists use microscopes to magnify cells and investigate their structures. The first type of microscope to be developed was the **light (or optical) microscope**. They work by creating an image that is much larger than the real object.
- there are two main types of light Microscope those are:
	- **Simple light microscope**: is light microscope with a **single simple lenses** e.g. hand lenses 
	- **Compound light Microscope**: is a light microscope containing **two or more lenses** working together.

#### *Parts of Compound light Microscope*

 A **common compound light microscope** contains different essential parts that make the Microscope powerful those are:  

 **Eyepiece(ocular lenses)**: This is the part of the microscope that is used to magnify the image that has been sent from the **objective lenses** and make them viewable by your eye. Eyepieces typically have a magnification of 10x - 20x, and some microscopes have a **single eyepiece (monocular)** while others have **two (binocular)** to reduce eye strain.

 **Eyepiece Tube:** is used to hold the Ocular lenses in place 

 **body Tube**: This is the part of the Microscope that acts as a conduit between the **Eyepiece** and the **objective lenses**, providing an optimal distance and angle between them.

 **Arm**: The arm is the curved or angled part of the microscope that connects the body tube to the base. It is the part you should hold when carrying the microscope, as it provides support for the upper parts of the instrument, including the body tube, focusing knobs, and stage.

 **Nosepieces**: This is a rotating turret located at the lower end of the body tube. It holds the objective lenses and allows you to easily switch between different magnifications by rotating it to position the desired objective lens over the specimen. It "clicks" into place when an objective is correctly aligned.

 **Objective Lenses**: These are the main lenses responsible for collecting light from the specimen and creating the initial magnified image. comes with 3 or 4 different lenses mounted to the revolving nosepiece with a 4x, 10x, 40x and 100x magnification the first two are **Low power lenses** and the other two are **High power lenses**. 

 **The stage**: is the flat platform located below the objective lenses that are used to mount the slid holding specimen to the It provides support for the slide during viewing. depending on the microscope there can be a simple **stage clip** or a **Mechanical stage**.

**Microscope Adjustment Knobs**: they are used to adjust the stage to put the specimen in focus. there two kinds of adjustment knobs those are:
-  **course adjustment knobs**: typically located on the arm they are used to adjust the stage to move up and down in **large increments** during the use of a **lower power objective (4x or 10x)** lenses for rapid focusing of the specimen.

- **fine adjustment knobs**: This is the smaller knob, often located coaxially with the coarse adjustment knob. It moves the stage (or body tube) in very small increments, allowing for precise focusing to achieve a sharp and clear image, especially at higher magnifications (e.g., 40x and 100x). t's important to use the fine adjustment knob only at higher magnifications to avoid crashing the objective lens into the slide.
	
**Illuminator**: Located in the base of the microscope, the illuminator is the source of light that passes through the specimen. Older microscopes might use a mirror to reflect external light, but most modern light microscopes have a built-in light source, often an LED or halogen bulb, with adjustable intensity.
 
**Base**: The base is the bottom support of the microscope. It provides stability and houses the illuminator and sometimes the power supply and light intensity control. The arm connects the body tube and upper parts to the base.
 
**Condenser**: The condenser is a lens system located below the stage. Its function is to gather light from the illuminator and focus it onto the specimen. This ensures that the specimen is evenly and optimally illuminated, which is essential for obtaining a clear and bright image, especially at higher magnifications. Some condensers can be moved up and down to adjust the focus of the light cone.

**iris Diaphragm**: The diaphragm, often an iris diaphragm, is located within or below the condenser. It is an adjustable opening that controls the **amount of light passing through the specimen and the angle of the light cone**. Adjusting the diaphragm affects the brightness, contrast, and resolution of the image.

#### *Magnification and resolution of a Light microscope*

fascinating relationship between light wavelength, magnification, resolution, and resolving power in a light microscope. These concepts are fundamental to understanding what we can actually see with our microscope.

**Light microscopes have a lower resolution and magnification compared to other microscopes:**

- **Resolution** - Maximum of about 200nm.
- **Magnification** - Maximum of about x 2000.


- **Light wavelength**: Visible light, which light microscopes use, is part of the electromagnetic spectrum and has wavelengths ranging from approximately 400 nanometers (violet light) to 700 nanometers (red light). Different colors correspond to different wavelengths.

- **Magnification**: As we've discussed, magnification is the process of making an object appear larger than its actual size. In a compound microscope, it's the product of the eyepiece and objective lens magnifications.

  $$\text{Total magnification} = \text{magnification of 
  the eyepiece} \quad \times \quad \text{magnification of 
  the objective lense}$$


- **Resolution**: Resolution is the ability to distinguish between two separate points or objects that are very close together.
    - A microscope with high resolution can show fine details clearly, while one with low resolution will make closely spaced objects appear as a single blur.

- **Resolving Power**: Resolving power is a measure of a microscope's ability to resolve detail.
    - It is often defined as the smallest distance between two points that can still be distinguished as separate points. A higher resolving power means the microscope can distinguish finer details.

 The **wavelength of light** is a fundamental limiting factor for the **resolution** and **resolving power** of a light microscope.

> **The Abbe diffraction limit** states that the maximum resolution achievable by a light microscope is approximately half the wavelength of the light being used. This means that shorter wavelengths of light (like blue or violet) can resolve finer details than longer wavelengths (like red).

**Magnification**, while making the image larger, does not improve resolution beyond the limit set by the wavelength of light and the numerical aperture of the objective lens.  If you magnify an image beyond its resolving power, it simply becomes a larger, blurrier image. This is known as "empty magnification."


Let's trace the path of light from the illuminator through the microscope and how it leads to the magnified image we see in the eyepieces.

1. The process begins with the **illuminator**, the light source in the base of the microscope. It produces a beam of light that travels upwards towards the specimen.

2. The light from the illuminator passes through the **condenser**, located beneath the **stage**. The condenser focuses this light into a cone that illuminates the specific area of the specimen being viewed by the **objective lens**. The **diaphragm** within the condenser regulates the intensity and the angle of this light.

3. The focused light then passes through our thin, stained specimen on the stage. Different structures within the tissue absorb or transmit light differently, creating contrast.

4. The light that has passed through the specimen enters the **objective lens**, which is positioned just above the slide. The objective lens is the primary magnifying component. It collects the light and forms a magnified, real, and inverted intermediate image of the specimen inside the microscope **body tube**.

5. The light carrying the intermediate image travels up the body tube to the eyepiece (ocular lens). The eyepiece acts like a magnifying glass for this intermediate image, further enlarging it. The image you see in the eyepiece is a magnified, virtual, and inverted image of the specimen.

> Remember that the **total magnification** is the **product** of the magnification of the objective lens and the eyepiece. **For example**, if you are using a 10x eyepiece and a 40x objective lens, the total magnification is 400x

### *Electron Microscope*

> **Electron microscopes** use electrons rather than light to magnify and form an image. Since electrons have a shorter wavelength than light, electron microscopes have a better resolution than light microscopes.

  

However, electron microscopes are more expensive than light microscopes and only produce images in black and white (computers can add colour to these images). 

  

The two types of electron microscope you need to know are:

1. **Transmission electron microscope (TEM)**
2. **Scanning electron microscope (SEM)**


These microscopes require complex preparation of specimens, meaning they are more likely to create artefacts. Artefacts are visible details that aren't part of the specimen being observed, such as air bubbles or fingerprints.

#### *Transmission electron microscopes*

Transmission electron microscopes (TEMs) use electromagnets to transmit a beam of electrons through a specimen. The denser parts absorb more electrons, so appear darker in the image formed.

TEMs produce high-resolution images, meaning they can be used to observe the internal structures of organelles (e.g. chloroplasts). The diagram below shows a chloroplast as viewed under a TEM.

![[Pasted image 20250804210820.png|400]]

However, the specimen must be viewed in a vacuum, meaning only non-living or dead organisms can be observed. Also, the specimen must be thin to allow electrons to pass through.

**TEMs have a better resolution and magnification compared to light microscopes:**

- **Resolution** - Maximum of around 0.5 nm.
- **Magnification** - Maximum of around x 2,000,000.

#### *Scanning electron microscopes* 

Scanning electron microscopes (SEMs) scan a beam of electrons across the surface of a specimen. Reflected electrons are then used to form an image. 
  

SEMs produce 3D images of the surface of the specimen. The diagram below shows algae as viewed under a SEM.

![[Pasted image 20250804210953.png|400]]

Like TEMs, SEMs can only view non-living or dead specimens. However, SEMs can be used on thicker specimens.

**SEMs have a worse resolution and similar magnification compared to TEMs:**

- **Resolution** - Maximum of around 5 nm.
- **Magnification** - Maximum of around x 2,000,000.

## **The Cell** 
### *Cell Theory*

- Cells were first seen over 300 years ago. In 1665, the English scientist *Robert Hooke* designed and put together one of the first working **optical microscopes**. He examined many different things including thin sections of cork. Hooke saw that these sections were made up of many tiny, regular compartments, which he called **cells**.
- It took many years of further work for the importance of cells to be recognised. In 1839 **Matthias Schleiden and Theodore Schwann** introduced an idea known as the **cell theory**. The cell theory states that cells are the basic units of life and by the 1840s this idea was accepted by most biologists.


  >[!Important]- **The Three Core Principles of Cell Theory**
  >1. **ALL living organisms are made of one or more cells**
  > - This principle emphasizes that the cell is the universal building block of life. Whether it's a single-celled bacterium or a complex multicellular organism like a human, cells are the fundamental components.
  > 2. **Cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life**
  > - Cells are the smallest entities that can carry out all the processes necessary for life. They are the functional units that perform specific tasks within an organism.
  > 3. **All cells arise from pre-existing cells by cell division**
  > - This principle, famously stated by Rudolf Virchow as "Omnis cellula e cellula" (every cell from a cell), disproved the idea of **spontaneous generation** which was the idea that living things arise from non-living things and established that cells reproduce to create new cells.

**There are two main types of cell membrane:** 

1. **cell (plasma) membranes** - These surround cells to act as a barrier between the cell and its environment, controlling which substances enter and leave the cell. 
2. **Membranes around organelles** - These surround organelles (e.g. mitochondria) to act as a barrier between the organelle and the cytoplasm, dividing the cell into different compartments (this is sometimes called compartmentalisation). 

  Both types of membrane are **partially permeable**, meaning they let some molecules pass through, but not others.
  
  
  
- **Key components of cell membranes:**
    - **Phospholipid bilayer** - This is made up of phospholipids with hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails.
    - **Cholesterol** - This adds stability during different temperature .
    - **Proteins** - These may be intrinsic(integrated) and extrinsic(peripheral) proteins.
    - **Glycoproteins** - These are proteins attached to a carbohydrate.
    - **Glycolipids** - These are lipids attached to a carbohydrate.

- The cell membrane is primarily composed of a **phospholipid bilayer**.  
  
  > **phospholipid bilayer** are lipid molecules arranged in two layers creating the fundamental structure of the cell membrane.
  
- **Phospholipids** are special lipid molecules with a *hydrophilic ("water-loving") head* and *two hydrophobic ("water-fearing") tails*. In the cell membrane, these phospholipids arrange themselves into **two layers** with the hydrophilic heads facing outwards towards the watery environment inside and outside the cell, and the hydrophobic tails facing inwards, away from the water.
     - This arrangement creates a hydrophobic centre in the bilayer so that **water-soluble substances cannot pass through**. 
    - However, **lipid-soluble substances** can dissolve in the bilayer and pass directly through the cell membrane.
  
  ![[Pasted image 20250818114019.png|400]]
- This lipid bilayer is not a rigid structure; it's *fluid*, allowing the phospholipids and other components to move laterally. This fluidity is important for the membrane's function and is often described by the **fluid mosaic model**.
  
  >  **In 1972, the fluid-mosaic model was proposed to describe the structure of cell membranes:**
  >     **Fluid** - Phospholipids form a bilayer in which the phospholipid molecules are constantly moving and are flexible.
  >    **Mosaic** - There are proteins of different sizes and shapes embedded in the phospholipid bilayer (so it looks a bit like a mosaic).

- Proteins in cell membranes can be grouped into two categories: **intrinsic(integrated)** and **extrinsic(peripheral) proteins**.
  ![[Pasted image 20250818115206.png]]
  
  **Intrinsic proteins**:
    - Intrinsic (or integral) proteins are embedded through both sides of the phospholipid bilayer.
    - These proteins include channel and carrier proteins which transport large molecules and ions across the membrane.

**Extrinsic proteins:**
    - Extrinsic (or peripheral) proteins are present on only one side of the phospholipid bilayer.
    - These proteins provide support to the membrane or may be involved in cell signalling.

- **Glycoproteins** consist of intrinsic proteins attached to carbohydrates, whereas **glycolipids** consist of lipids attached to carbohydrates.
     **Integral proteins** + *carbohydrates* = Glycoproteins
     **lipids** + *carbohydrates* = Glycolipids 
  ![[Pasted image 20250818115903.png]]
  
- **Glycoproteins and glycolipids are involved in the following:** 
    1. **Cell adhesion** - This is the attachment of cells to one another. 
    2. **Cell recognition** - This allows cells to recognise one another.
    3. **Cell signalling** - This is communication between cells.
       
- **Cholesterol** molecules provide stability to cell membranes during different temperatures.
  - Cholesterol molecules consist of a **hydrophilic and hydrophobic region** just like the phospholipids. The hydrophobic regions bind to phospholipid fatty acid tails, causing them to pack more closely together. This reduces the fluidity of the cell membrane.
    
#### *Cytoplasm*

> **the cytoplasm** is the everything bounded by the cell membrane except the nucleus. The cytoplasm is the entire contents within the cell membrane of a eukaryotic cell, excluding the nucleus

- The part of the cell referred to as **cytoplasm** is slightly different in eukaryotes and prokaryotes. In eukaryotic cells, which have a nucleus, *the cytoplasm is everything between the plasma membrane and the nuclear envelope*. In prokaryotes, which lack a nucleus, cytoplasm simply means everything found inside the plasma membrane.

- One major component of the cytoplasm in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes is the gel-like **cytosol**, a water-based solution that contains ions, small molecules, and macromolecules. In eukaryotes, the cytoplasm also includes membrane-bound organelles, which are suspended in the cytosol. The [cytoskeleton](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-wmopen-biology1/chapter/the-cytoskeleton/) , a network of fibers that supports the cell and gives it shape, is also part of the cytoplasm and helps to organize cellular components.

- Even though the cytosol is mostly water (70-80%), it has a semi-solid, Jello-like consistency because of the many proteins suspended in it. The cytosol contains a rich broth of macromolecules and smaller organic molecules, including glucose and other simple sugars, polysaccharides, amino acids, nucleic acids, and fatty acids. Ions of sodium, potassium, calcium, and other elements are also found in the cytosol. Many metabolic reactions, including protein synthesis, take place in this part of the cell.


#### *Nucleus*

> The **nucleus** is a prominent, membrane-bound organelle found in eukaryotic cells, including animal cells. It's typically the largest organelle and houses the cell's genetic material in the form of **DNA**

- The **nucleus** (plural = _nuclei_) houses the cell’s DNA and directs the synthesis of ribosomes and proteins.

- the nucleus consists of **three** critical parts that, discussed bellow:
![[Pasted image 20250922151119.png]]
###### *The Nuclear Envelope*

The **nuclear envelope** is a double-membrane structure that constitutes the outermost portion of the nucleus. Both the inner and outer membranes of the nuclear envelope are phospholipid bilayers.

  - The nuclear envelope is punctuated with **nuclear pores** that control the passage of ions, molecules, and RNA between the *nucleoplasm* and cytoplasm.


##### *nucleoplasm*

The **nucleoplasm** is the semi-solid fluid inside the nucleus, where we find the chromatin and the nucleolus.

- It is the jelly-like substance filling the nucleus, similar to the cytoplasm of the cell. It contains enzymes, nucleotides, and other molecules necessary for nuclear functions

- *Chromatin/Chromosomes*: Within the nucleoplasm, DNA is organized with proteins (primarily histones) to form **chromatin**. During cell division, chromatin condenses to form visible **chromosomes**. Chromatin's structure allows for packaging of DNA and regulation of gene expression.

###### *The Nucleolus*

Located inside the nucleus, the **nucleolus** is involved in the synthesis of **ribosomal RNA** (rRNA) and the assembly of ribosomes.



**Functions of the nucleus:** 

- **Controlling the cell's activities** - It contains DNA with instructions to produce proteins. 
- **Synthesis of ribosomes** - The nucleolus makes ribosomal RNA.
- **Exchange between nucleus and cytoplasm** - Substances can enter or leave the nucleus via the nuclear pores.



#### *Mitochondria*

> **Mitochondria** (often called the "powerhouses" of the cell) are essential organelles found in nearly all eukaryotic cells. Their primary role is to generate the majority of the cell's supply of **adenosine triphosphate (ATP)**, which is the main energy currency of the cell.

- and the process in which they produce ATP is called **cellular respiration** or **oxidative phosphorylation**.
- Cells that require a lot of energy, such as muscle cells and nerve cells, have a higher number of mitochondria. This highlights the direct link between **mitochondrial abundance and a cell's energy demands.**

Mitochondria have a unique structure that is key to their function:
![[Pasted image 20250922181404.png]]

##### *double Membrane*

- The *outer membrane* is smooth and permeable to small molecules and ions, thanks to channel proteins called **porins**.
- The *inner membrane* is folded into numerous structures called **cristae**, which significantly increase the surface area. This membrane is less permeable and contains proteins involved in the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis with in it.
	- The term "*cristae*" refers to the folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane. This folding **increases the surface area available for biochemical reactions**, particularly those involved in ATP production. The expanded surface area due to cristae allows for more efficient processes to occur simultaneously, as there is more space for enzymes and proteins to operate.
- The *Intermembrane Space*: The space between the outer and inner membranes.

  
##### *Mitochondrial Matrix*

- The **mitochondrial matrix** is the innermost compartment of the mitochondrion, enclosed by the *inner mitochondrial membrane*. It's a dense, gel-like substance that contains a complex mixture of enzymes, ribosomes, tRNAs, and several copies of the mitochondrial DNA (mt-DNA).

- This is where several key metabolic processes take place, most notably the **Krebs cycle (also known as the citric acid cycle or TCA cycle)** and the **beta-oxidation of fatty acids**. These pathways break down fuel molecules, generating electron carriers (like NADH and FADH₂) that are then used in the electron transport chain on the inner membrane to produce ATP.



#### *Endoplasmic reticulum*

> **Endoplasmic reticulum** is a series of interconnected membranous sacs and tubules that collectively modifies proteins and synthesizes lipids

- It's often the largest organelle in the cell by membrane area and plays a crucial role in synthesizing, modifying, and transporting proteins and lipids.

The ER exists in two distinct forms, which are structurally and functionally different, although they are continuous with each other:

##### *Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum*

- The rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) is so named because the ribosomes attached to its cytoplasmic surface give it a studded appearance when viewed through an electron microscope.
![[Pasted image 20250929120705.png]]
- its primary function is **Protein synthesis** (for proteins destined for *secretion, insertion into membranes, or delivery to other organelles*), protein folding, modification, and quality control.

- **Protein Synthesis**- Ribosomes attached to the RER synthesize proteins that are destined for secretion from the cell, incorporation into cellular membranes, or delivery to organelles like lysosomes.


- **Protein Folding and Modification**- The RER lumen provides an environment for proteins to fold correctly with the help of chaperone proteins. Modifications like the addition of carbohydrates (glycosylation) also occur here.

##### *Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum*

- The smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER) is continuous with the RER but has few or no ribosomes on its cytoplasmic surface. Functions of the SER include *synthesis of carbohydrates, lipids, and steroid hormones; detoxification of medications and poisons; and storage of calcium ions*.

![[Pasted image 20250929121056.png]]

- **Lipid Synthesis**- The SER is the primary site for the synthesis of various lipids, including phospholipids (components of cell membranes) and steroids (like hormones).

- **Detoxification**- In liver cells, the SER contains enzymes that detoxify harmful substances, such as drugs and metabolic waste products.

- **Calcium Storage**- The SER is a major storage site for calcium ions within the cell. The release and uptake of calcium from the SER play crucial roles in various cellular processes, including muscle contraction and signaling.

#### *Ribosome*

> **Ribosomes** are the cellular structures responsible for protein synthesis.

- Ribosomes are complex molecular machines responsible for **protein synthesis, a process called translation**

-  Found freely in the cytoplasm of **all cells** or as part of the **rough endoplasmic reticulum** in eukaryotic cells

-   Ribosomes are composed of *ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins*.  In eukaryotic cells, ribosomes are assembled in the **nucleolus** and exported to the cytoplasm. They consist of **two subunits**, a large subunit and a small subunit, that come together during protein synthesis.

- The process of *translation* involves the ribosome moving along the mRNA molecule, reading the **codons** (sequences of three nucleotides). For each codon, a **transfer RNA** (tRNA) molecule carrying the corresponding amino acid binds to the ribosome. The ribosome then catalyzes the formation of a peptide bond between the incoming amino acid and the growing polypeptide chain.
- The size of ribosomes are measured in S units. Eukaryotic cells contain 80S ribosomes.

---
**Composition**: Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal proteins.

**Structure**: Consists of a large subunit and a small subunit that join to form a functional ribosome.

**Location**: Can be found free in the cytoplasm or attached to the membranes of the rough endoplasmic reticulum.

**Function**: Catalyzes the formation of peptide bonds between amino acids to build protein chains (translation).

#### *The Golgi Apparatus*

We have already mentioned that **vesicles** can bud from the ER and transport their contents elsewhere, but where do the vesicles go? Before reaching their final destination, the lipids or proteins within the transport vesicles still need to be **sorted, packaged, and tagged so that they wind up in the right place**. Sorting, tagging, packaging, and distribution of lipids and proteins takes place in the *Golgi apparatus (also called the Golgi body)*

> **The Golgi apparatus**, also known as the Golgi complex or Golgi body, is a stack of flattened, membrane-bound sacs called **cisternae**. It acts like a cellular post office or processing plant, receiving proteins and lipids from the endoplasmic reticulum, **modifying them, sorting them, and packaging them into vesicles** for transport to various destinations within or outside the cell.

The receiving side of the Golgi apparatus is called the _cis_ face. The opposite side is called the _trans_ face. The **transport vesicles** that formed from the ER travel to the _cis_ face, fuse with it, and empty their contents into the lumen of the Golgi apparatus. As the proteins and lipids travel through the Golgi, they undergo further modifications that allow them to be sorted. The most frequent modification is the **addition of short chains of sugar molecules**. These newly modified proteins and lipids are then tagged with phosphate groups or other small molecules so that they can be routed to their proper destinations.
![[Pasted image 20251006142631.png]]

Finally, the modified and tagged proteins are packaged into **secretory vesicles** that bud from the _trans_ face of the Golgi. While some of these vesicles deposit their contents into other parts of the cell where they will be used, other secretory vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell.

>[!note]- Vesicles
> **Vesicles** are **small, membrane-bound sacs** found inside cells.  
> ![[Pasted image 20251006143624.png]]
They act as **tiny transport containers**, moving materials such as **proteins, lipids, or waste** between different parts of the cell — for example, from the **endoplasmic reticulum** to the **Golgi apparatus**(the transport vesicles), and then to the **cell membrane** for secretion(secretory vesicles) .


#### *lysosomes*

> **Lysosomes** are spherical, membrane-bound organelles found in animal cells that act as the *cell's recycling centers or garbage disposal units*. They contain powerful enzymes that break down waste materials, worn-out organelles, and engulfed substances.

- They contain a variety of powerful **hydrolytic enzymes** that can break down a wide range of biological molecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids. These enzymes work best in an acidic environment, and the lysosomal membrane maintains this low pH (around 4.5-5.0) by pumping in hydrogen ions into the cell.

>[!note]- Hydrolytic Enzymes
Enzymes that catalyze hydrolysis reactions, which involve the breaking of a chemical bond by adding water. Lysosomal enzymes are acid hydrolases, meaning they function optimally in an acidic environment.

Lysosomes are involved in several essential cellular processes:
![[Pasted image 20251006163620.png]]

**Digestion of Engulfed Material**

Lysosomes fuse with vesicles containing materials taken up from outside the cell through processes like **endocytosis** (bringing substances into the cell) and **phagocytosis** (engulfing large particles like bacteria). The lysosomal enzymes then digest these materials.

**Autophagy**

Lysosomes are involved in the degradation of the cell's own worn-out or damaged organelles and macromolecules. This process, called **autophagy**, is crucial for cellular homeostasis and recycling.


**Waste Degradation and Recycling**

By breaking down various substances, lysosomes help to eliminate cellular waste products and recycle their components for reuse by the cell.

**Programmed Cell Death (Apoptosis)**

In some cases, lysosomes can trigger programmed cell death (apoptosis) by releasing their enzymes into the cytoplasm, leading to the dismantling of the cell.

**Defense Against Pathogens**

Lysosomes can fuse with vesicles containing invading bacteria or viruses, digesting and destroying them.

####  *Peroxisomes*

> **Peroxisomes** are small, round organelles enclosed by single membranes. They carry out **oxidation reactions** that break down fatty acids and amino acids.

- They also detoxify many poisons that may enter the body. 
- Many of these oxidation reactions release *hydrogen peroxide*, H2O2, which would be damaging to cells; however, when these reactions are confined to **peroxisomes**, enzymes safely break down the H2O2 into *oxygen and water*. For example, alcohol is detoxified by peroxisomes in liver cells. *Glyoxysomes*, which are specialized peroxisomes in plants, are responsible for converting stored fats into sugars.


#### *vacuoles*

>**Vacuoles** are essentially membrane-enclosed sacs within the cytoplasm. While they are present in both animal and plant cells, they are particularly prominent and play a crucial role in plant cells, often occupying a large portion of the cell's volume

- **Vacuoles** are membrane-bound sacs that function in **storage and transport**. unlike vesicles, The membrane of a vacuole does not fuse with the membranes of other cellular components. Additionally, some agents such as enzymes within plant vacuoles break down macromolecules. 
- Its formation involves the fusion of smaller vacuoles derived from the ER and Golgi apparatus. Its importance in plant life cannot be overstated, contributing to everything from structural support and growth to storage and defense.
![[Pasted image 20251006192010.png]]
- plant cells typically have one large central vacuole that can occupy a significant portion of the cell's volume, sometimes up to 90%. This is a defining characteristic of plant cells and contributes significantly to their unique properties
- The large central vacuole is surrounded by a single membrane called the **tonoplast**. This membrane is crucial for regulating the movement of substances into and out of the vacuole, maintaining the vacuole's internal environment, and establishing the proton gradient that drives transport across the tonoplast.
- The fluid inside the central vacuole is called **vacuolar sap**. It's an aqueous solution containing a diverse mix of substances, including water, ions (such as K+, Cl-, and Ca2+), sugars, amino acids, waste products, pigments, and sometimes toxic compounds.

Key Features and Functions of the Large Central Vacuole:

**Turgor Pressure Maintenance**

This is perhaps the most critical function. As water enters the vacuole by osmosis, it swells and pushes against the cytoplasm and cell wall, generating turgor pressure. This pressure is essential for maintaining the plant's rigidity and supporting its upright structure. When the vacuole loses water, turgor pressure drops, leading to wilting.


**Storage Reservoir**

The large size of the central vacuole makes it an ideal storage space for a wide variety of molecules, including essential nutrients, ions, and metabolic byproducts. This storage can help buffer against environmental changes.


**Waste Disposal and Detoxification**

The vacuole serves as a compartment for isolating and storing cellular waste products and potentially toxic substances, preventing them from interfering with metabolic processes in the cytoplasm.


**Degradation and Recycling**

Similar to lysosomes, the vacuole contains hydrolytic enzymes that break down macromolecules, damaged organelles, and pathogens, facilitating the recycling of their components.


**Cell Growth**

The expansion of the central vacuole is a primary driver of cell elongation during plant growth. By taking up water, the vacuole increases the cell's volume with minimal synthesis of new cytoplasm, which is energetically efficient.


**Defense**

Storage of defensive compounds in the vacuole can protect the plant from herbivores and pathogens. When the cell is damaged, these compounds can be released and activated.


#### *Centrosome*

> The centrosome is a major **microtubule-organizing center (MTOC)** in animal cells. It's typically located near the nucleus and plays a critical role in organizing the cytoskeleton and forming the spindle fibers during cell division. Unlike many other organelles we've discussed, the centrosome is **not** found in plant cells

![[Pasted image 20251006174733.png]]
- The core of the centrosome consists of two barrel-shaped structures called **centrioles**, arranged perpendicular to each other. Each centriole is a cylinder of nine triplets of microtubules.
- The centrosome (the organelle where all microtubules originate) replicates itself before a cell divides, and the centrioles appear to have some role in pulling the duplicated chromosomes to opposite ends of the dividing cell.

#### *Cilia and flagella*

> **Cilia and flagella** are hair-like structures that extend from the surface of many eukaryotic cells. They are involved in movement, either moving the cell itself or moving substances across the cell's surface. They have a characteristic internal structure made of *microtubules*.

- **Cilia** and **flagella** are structurally similar but differ in length, number, and beating pattern.They are composed of microtubules arranged in a characteristic **9+2 pattern**, meaning nine doublets of microtubules surround a central pair of single microtubules. This arrangement, along with associated proteins like **dynein**, forms the core structure called the **axoneme**
- The movement of cilia and flagella is powered by the motor protein **dynein**, which uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to slide the microtubule doublets past each other. This sliding movement, constrained by other proteins in the axoneme, results in the bending of the cilium or flagellum.
![[Pasted image 20251006190819.png]]

>[!note] Axoneme
 The core structure of cilia and flagella, composed of microtubules arranged in a characteristic 9+2 or 9+0 pattern and associated proteins.

#### *Chloroplasts*

> **Chloroplasts** are the sites of **photosynthesis**, the process by which light energy is converted into chemical energy in the form of glucose.They are essential for life as we know it, forming the base of most food chains on Earth.

- Like the mitochondria, chloroplasts have their own DNA and ribosomes, but chloroplasts have an entirely different function. Chloroplasts are plant cell organelles that carry out photosynthesis. 

> Photosynthesis is the series of reactions that use carbon dioxide, water, and light energy to make glucose and oxygen. 

- This is a major difference between plants and animals; **plants (autotrophs)** are able to make their own food, like sugars, while **animals (heterotrophs)** must ingest their food.
![[Pasted image 20251006192036.png]]

- Like mitochondria, chloroplasts have **outer and inner membranes**, but within the space enclosed by a chloroplast’s inner membrane is a set of interconnected and stacked fluid-filled membrane sacs called **thylakoids** . E*ach stack of thylakoids is called a granum (plural = grana)*. The fluid enclosed by the inner membrane that surrounds the grana is called the **stroma**.

- The chloroplasts contain a green pigment called *chlorophyll*, which captures the light energy that drives the reactions of photosynthesis. Like plant cells, photosynthetic protists also have chloroplasts. Some bacteria perform photosynthesis.

### *Prokaryote vs Eukaryote cells*

Cells fall into one of two broad categories: **prokaryotic and eukaryotic**. The single-celled organisms of the domains Bacteria and Archaea are classified as *prokaryotes* (_pro_ = before; _karyon_– = nucleus). Animal cells, plant cells, fungi, and protists are *eukaryotes* (_eu_ = true).

- The most significant difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells lies in the presence or absence of a **membrane-bound nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.**

#### *Components of Prokaryotic Cells*

> A **prokaryotic cell** is a simple, single-celled (unicellular) organism that lacks a nucleus, or any other membrane-bound organelle.

![[Pasted image 20251006211953.png]]

- Prokaryotic DNA is found in the central part of the cell: a darkened region called the **nucleoid.**
- The cytoplasm of prokaryotic cells is **not divided** into **compartments**, it **lacks membrane-bound organelles**
- Prokaryotes do not have a nucleus, but they **do have genetic material**. This is generally in the form of a **single circular DNA molecule** (**not associated with** **proteins**) located in the **nucleoid**
- Prokaryotes have a **cell wall** containing **murein/peptidoglycan** (a glycoprotein)
    - The cell wall acts as **protection**, maintains the **shape** of the cell and prevents the cell from **bursting**
- At 0.1–5.0 µm in diameter, prokaryotic cells are significantly smaller than eukaryotic cells, which have diameters ranging from 10–100 µm

#### *Components of Eukaryotic Cells*

> A **eukaryotic cell** is a cell that has a membrane-bound nucleus and other membrane-bound compartments or sacs, called **organelles**, which have specialized functions.

- Eukaryotic cells have a more **complex ultrastructure** than prokaryotic cells
- The cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells is divided up into **membrane-bound** compartments called **organelles**. These compartments are either bound by a **single** or **double membrane**
- The **compartmentalization** of the cell is **advantageous** as it allows:
    
    - Enzymes and substrates to be localised and therefore available at higher concentrations
    - Damaging substances to be kept separated, e.g. digestive enzymes are stored in lysosomes so they do not digest the cell
    - Optimal conditions to be maintained for certain processes e.g. optimal pH for digestive enzymes
    - The numbers and location of organelles to be altered depending on requirements of the cell
    
- **Eukaryotic** cells have a key compartment called the **nucleus**


 Think of a prokaryotic cell as a single room where everything happens in one open space. In contrast, a eukaryotic cell is like a house with multiple rooms (organelles) that allow different activities to occur in specialized compartments

| Prokaryotes                                     | Eukaryotes                                            |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Smaller, less complex                           | Larger, more complex                                  |
| No **compartmentalization**                     | Membrane bound organelles                             |
| Evolved first                                   | Evolved from prokaryotes                              |
| Most are single celled                          | Can be single celled, complex multicellular organisms |
| DNA in a single loop free floating in cytoplasm | DNA wrapped around proteins contained in nucleus      |
| 70s ribosomes free floating in cytoplasm        | 80s ribosomes both free and bound to RER              |

## **The Cell and Its Environment**

Your cells need to take in substances, such as oxygen and glucose,and to get rid of waste products and chemicals that are needed elsewhere in your body. In human beings, just like all other living organisms, dissolved substances can move into and out of your cells across the cell membrane in two different ways: **active and passive transport** 

### *passive transport*

- *passive transport* is the movement of substances from an area of high concentration to an area of lower concentration down the **concentration gradient** with no input of energy. 
- there are two main types of passive transport:
	- **Diffusion**
	- **Osmosis**

#### *Diffusion*

> **Diffusion** is the net movement of substances(ions,molecules and atoms) from an area of **higher concentration** to an area of **lower concentration** down along the *concentration gradient* until **equilibrium** is established.

- **Concentration** is a way of measuring how much (how many particles) of a substance is in one place.
- This movement is driven by the random **kinetic energy of molecules**. They are constantly in motion, colliding with each other and spreading out. Imagine dropping a drop of food coloring into a glass of water. Initially, the food coloring is concentrated in one spot. Over time, the food coloring molecules will spread out and mix with the water molecules until they are evenly distributed throughout the glass. This is diffusion in action
- The key concept here is the **concentration gradient**. This is the *difference in the concentration* of a substance between two areas. Diffusion occurs _down_ the concentration gradient, meaning from where there's more of a substance to where there's less.


![[Pasted image 20251016192616.png]]

- Diffusion is a **passive process**, meaning it does not require the cell to expend energy. The movement is solely driven by the inherent kinetic energy of the molecules.

Here are the key factors that affect the rate of diffusion:

- **Concentration Gradient**: A larger difference in concentration between two areas leads to a faster rate of diffusion. Molecules will move from high to low concentration more rapidly when the gradient is steeper.


- **Temperature**: Higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of molecules, causing them to move faster and collide more frequently. This results in a faster rate of diffusion. Conversely, lower temperatures slow down diffusion.


- **Surface Area**: A larger surface area for diffusion allows more molecules to move across at the same time, increasing the overall rate of diffusion.

- **Distance**: The shorter the distance molecules need to travel, the faster the rate of diffusion. Diffusion is very efficient over short distances but becomes much slower over longer distances.


- **Properties of the Substance**: The size and type of molecule diffusing also matters. Smaller molecules generally diffuse faster than larger ones. The properties of the medium through which diffusion is occurring (like its viscosity) also play a role.

These factors work together to determine the overall rate of diffusion in a given situation.  For instance, in the human body, the structure of the lungs is designed with a massive surface area and very thin membranes to maximize the rate of oxygen and carbon dioxide diffusion during breathing.


#### *Osmosis*

>**osmosis**, which is a special type of diffusion. Osmosis is specifically the diffusion of **water** across a **selectively permeable membrane** from an area of **higher water concentration** to an area of **lower water concentration** until distributed evenly.

- Think about a situation where you have a high concentration of salt on one side of a selectively permeable membrane and a lower concentration of salt on the other side. The membrane allows water to pass through but not salt. In this case, water will move from the side with lower salt concentration (higher water concentration) to the side with higher salt concentration (lower water concentration) to try and equalize the concentrations. This is osmosis.

![[Pasted image 20251020173048.png]]
- The driving force for osmosis is the **water potential gradient($\Phi$)**. Water potential is a measure of the amount of water molecules in an area. Water moves from an area of higher water potential to an area of lower water potential. Adding solutes to water lowers its water potential.

##### *Tonicity*

 **Tonicity** compares the concentration of solutes in the solution outside the cell to the concentration of solutes inside the cell.

 There are three main types of solutions we consider in terms of tonicity:
 ![[Pasted image 20251020174750.png]]

1. **Isotonic Solution**

In an isotonic solution, **the concentration of solutes outside the cell is the same as the concentration of solutes inside the cell**. This means there is *no net movement of water across the cell membrane*. Water molecules move in and out at equal rates, so the cell maintains its normal shape and volume.

2. **Hypotonic Solution**

In a hypotonic solution, **the concentration of solutes outside the cell is lower than the concentration of solutes inside the cell**. This results in a higher concentration of water outside the cell compared to inside. Water moves _into_ the cell by osmosis. This causes the cell to swell. In animal cells, which lack a rigid cell wall, this can lead to the cell *bursting (lysis).* Plant cells, with their cell walls, become firm and *turgid*.

3. **Hypertonic Solution**

In a hypertonic solution, **the concentration of solutes outside the cell is higher than the concentration of solutes inside the cell.** This means there is a lower concentration of water outside the cell compared to inside. Water moves _out of_ the cell by osmosis. This causes the cell to *shrink or shrivel*. In plant cells, the cell membrane pulls away from the cell wall, a process called *plasmolysis*.

##### *Osmosis in Plant cells*

Plant cells have a key structural component that animal cells lack: a rigid **cell wall**. This cell wall significantly influences how plant cells respond to different tonicity conditions

Here's a more detailed look at the effects of osmosis on plant cells:

- **In a Hypotonic Solution:** When a plant cell is placed in a hypotonic solution (lower solute concentration outside the cell), water enters the cell by osmosis. The large central vacuole in plant cells swells as it fills with water, pushing the cytoplasm and cell membrane against the rigid cell wall. The cell wall prevents the cell from bursting. The pressure exerted by the cell contents against the cell wall is called **turgor pressure**. A plant cell in this state is described as **turgid**. Turgidity is essential for maintaining the plant's rigidity and support. and is the best normal condition plants should be This turgor pressure is vital for the structural integrity of plants, allowing them to stand upright and for leaves to be held out to capture sunlight
![[Pasted image 20251020174657.png|300]]

- **In an Isotonic Solution:** In an isotonic solution (equal solute concentration inside and outside the cell), there is no net movement of water. Water enters and leaves the cell at equal rates. The plant cell is neither turgid nor plasmolyzed and is described as **flaccid**. The plant may appear slightly droopy or limp in this state.
![[Pasted image 20251020174712.png|300]]

- **In a Hypertonic Solution:** When a plant cell is placed in a hypertonic solution (higher solute concentration outside the cell), water leaves the cell by osmosis. The central vacuole shrinks, and the cytoplasm and cell membrane pull away from the cell wall. This process is called **plasmolysis**. The cell wall remains intact, but the cell membrane detaches. A plant cell in this state is described as **plasmolyzed**. Plants with plasmolyzed cells will appear severely wilted.

![[Pasted image 20251020174725.png|300]]

##### *Osmosis in animal cells*

Unlike plant cells, animal cells do _not_ have a rigid cell wall. This absence of a cell wall makes animal cells more vulnerable to changes in the tonicity of their surrounding environment

Here's how osmosis affects animal cells in different solutions:

- **In an Isotonic Solution**: When an animal cell is in an isotonic solution, the concentration of solutes outside the cell is equal to the concentration of solutes inside the cell. Water moves across the cell membrane in both directions at equal rates, resulting in no net movement of water. The animal cell maintains its normal shape and volume. This is the ideal environment for most animal cells.
![[Pasted image 20251020175154.png|300]]

- **In a Hypotonic Solution:** When an animal cell is placed in a hypotonic solution (lower solute concentration outside), water enters the cell by osmosis. Since there is no cell wall to resist the influx of water, the cell swells. If too much water enters, the cell membrane can stretch and eventually burst, a process called **lysis** or **hemolysis** (specifically for red blood cells).
![[Pasted image 20251020175143.png|300]]

- **In a Hypertonic Solution**: When an animal cell is placed in a hypertonic solution (higher solute concentration outside), water leaves the cell by osmosis. This loss of water causes the cytoplasm to shrink and the cell membrane to shrivel or become notched. This process is called **crenation**. The cell becomes dehydrated and its functions can be impaired.
![[Pasted image 20251020175206.png|300]]

### *active transport*

> **Active transport** is the movement of molecules or ions across a cell membrane **against** their concentration gradient – meaning from an area of **lower concentration** to an area of **higher concentration**.

- Active transport is the movement of particles from an area of lower concentration to an area of higher concentration. This process requires energy from respiration in the form of ATP.

![[Pasted image 20251020180542.png]]


**Carrier proteins**: Like facilitated diffusion, active transport involves the use of carrier proteins to transport molecules or ions across membranes. The process works in the following way:

1. The molecule or ion binds to the carrier protein.

![[Pasted image 20251020180749.png|300]]
2. ATP binds to the carrier protein.
![[Pasted image 20251020180828.png|300]]
3. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and phosphate (Pi) causes the carrier protein to change shape(conformational change). This releases the molecule or ion on the opposite side of the membrane.

![Diagram showing a carrier protein facilitating active transport across a cell membrane with molecules and ions.|300](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/c9b7130b-ecbc-4f49-977d-d21fa147bdd1/carrier-protein-active-transport.png)

4. The phosphate (Pi) is released from the carrier protein, causing the carrier protein to return to its original shape, ready to be used again.

![[Pasted image 20251020180954.png|300]]
A classic example of active transport is the **sodium-potassium pump**, found in many animal cells. This pump uses ATP to move sodium ions out of the cell and potassium ions into the cell, both against their concentration gradients. This is vital for maintaining cell volume, nerve impulse transmission, and many other cellular functions.
