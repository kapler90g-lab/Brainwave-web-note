## **Cell Cycle**

- **cell cycle** is a _sequence_ of _events_ that takes _place_ in the **parent cell** as a _means_ of _distributing_ _genetic material_ thereby forming a **daughter cell**.

- The cell cycle is an ordered series of events that involve cell growth and cell division to produce new daughter cells

- Organisms need new cells to grow and repair tissues in the body. Not all cells in the body are capable of dividing, but those that are follow a sequence of steps known as the **cell cycle.**

- **The cell cycle consists of three main phases:** 
     1. **Interphase** - This occupies the majority of the cycle as cells prepare for division.
     2. **Mitotic phase(karyokinesis)** - This is when the nucleus of the cell divides in two or more daughter cells. 
     3. **Cytokinesis** - This is when the whole cell and cytoplasm divides in two to produce two identical cells.

![[Pasted image 20250217184037.png]]
### *Interphase(preparatory phase)*

- During **interphase**, cells carry out their usual functions whilst also **preparing for cell division**. This involves replicating the DNA and organelles.
- it is the period of preparation for a cell to divide and start the cell cycle
-  It is the longest part of the cycle and can be thought of as the parent cell preparing for division.


- **Interphase consists of three phases:** 

1. **G1 phase** - This is when the cell grows and makes new proteins to replicate DNA.
2. **S phase** - This is when DNA is replicated. 
3. **G2 phase** - This is when the cell continues to grow and replicated DNA is checked for errors and replicates organelles.
![[Pasted image 20250217185517.png]]
#### *G1 phase(first Gap phase)*

- *G1 phase (first gap)* is the first stage of interphase in which the cell is quite active at the biochemical level and The cell is **preparing** for DNA replication by **gathering resources** and **growing** in size.

- They accumulate the _building block_ of **chromosomal DNA or free nucleotide**, The cell **collects nucleotides** (A, T, C, G) to use later for making a copy of its DNA in the next phase (S phase)
- they associate **proteins** These are **histone proteins** that help **package DNA** into a compact structure called **chromatin**, meaning they **collects histone proteins**, which will later be used to wrap and organize DNA properly when it is replicated.
- they _store significant energy_ _reserves_ The cell needs **a lot of energy** for DNA replication and growth. This energy is mainly stored in **ATP molecules to complete task of replicating DNA**.
- All these preparations (nucleotide collection, histone proteins, and energy storage) help the cell **successfully copy its DNA** in the next phase.

- It is also the **Checkpoint** for the cell to determine whether it is ready to proceed to **S phase**
- If the cell is not ready, it can exit the cell cycle and enter a phase called **G0**.
- *The G0 phase (resting phase)*, is a phase in which the cell is neither dividing nor preparing to divide but performs regulatory and basic cellular functions.

#### *S phase (synthesis of DNA phase)*

- The S phase is a stage in which [[Genetics#*DNA replication*|DNA replication]] proceeds to form identical pairs of DNA molecules (*sister chromatids*) that are firmly attached to the *centromeric region*
- After DNA replication is complete, the chromosomes are made of identical **sister chromatids**. Each chromatid contains a double helix of DNA bundled up tightly. The two chromatids are held together by a centromere in the middle.

#### *G2 phase(second gap phase)*

- The **G2 phase** is a stage in which, the cell **replenishes its energy** stores that exhausted during DNA replication at S-phase.
- The cell **checks and finalizes preparations** for mitosis by producing necessary proteins, duplicating organelles, and reorganizing its internal structure.

- **synthesizes proteins** necessary for chromosome manipulation These are **special proteins** (such as *tubulin(spindle fibers)*) that help move chromosomes during mitosis. 
- In this phase, some cell organelles duplicate, The cell **copies some of its organelles** so that each of the two new daughter cells will have the necessary structures after mitosis.
- and the cytoskeleton(A network of **protein fibers** that gives the cell **shape and support** and helps in **cell movement**) disintegrates, The **cytoskeleton breaks down** so that its proteins (like tubulin) can be **used to form spindle fibers**, which help **move chromosomes** during mitosis.
- Cells might continue growing in this phase.

- After completing the interphase, the cell undergoes either mitotic or meiotic cell divisions.


### *Cell Division*

- cells do not know what reproduction is, The processes of mitosis and meiosis are **biochemical and physical** reactions controlled by molecular signals, not by thought or will. **Cell division arises from physical and biological constraints.**

- cells generally divide to fix two major **physical and biological constraints**:

	1. Cells have an optimal size range. If a cell becomes too large, it becomes less efficient at transporting nutrients and waste across its membrane and communicating with its environment. **Repeated cell division prevents cells from becoming excessively large**. When a cell divides, it effectively reduces its size, restoring a favorable *surface area to volume ratio*.
	
	2. when the cell becomes to extremely large the nucleus struggles to control a large volume of cytoplasm. 

- Cell division is a fundamental biological process that is essential for life as we know it. In multicellular organisms, it serves several critical functions:

	- **Growth**: Starting from a single fertilized egg, multicellular organisms grow by increasing the number of their cells through repeated cell division. This process is essential for development from embryo to adult.
	
	- **Repair**: Cell division is vital for replacing damaged or worn-out cells and repairing tissues after injury.
	
	- **Reproduction**:  While not the primary mode of reproduction in all multicellular organisms, cell division (specifically meiosis) is essential for producing the gametes (sperm and egg cells) required for sexual reproduction. and they are the direct means of reproduction in most unicellular organisms.

- **Cell division** consists of two main **sub-divisions**:

	1. **Nuclear division(karyokinesis)**: results in the separation of **genetic** material of mother cell into _daughter cells_ by mitosis or meiosis. 
	
	2. **Cytoplasmic division(cytokinesis)**: it is the separation of the **cytoplasmic** components of the mother cell into the _daughter cells_.

>[!important]- Cancer 
>- When cell cycle control mechanisms fail, cells can divide uncontrollably, leading to the formation of a mass of abnormal cells. This is the hallmark of **cancer**. Mutations in genes that regulate the cell cycle, particularly those that control *checkpoints or produce cyclins and CDKs*, can disrupt the delicate balance and allow cells to divide without restraint.
>
> - Uncontrolled cell division can lead to tumors, which can damage tissues and organs. If these cells spread to other parts of the body (**metastasis**), the disease becomes even more challenging to treat. Understanding cell cycle regulation is therefore crucial for developing therapies to combat cancer and other diseases associated with abnormal cell proliferation.


#### *Mitosis*

- Mitosis is a type of nuclear cell division in which a parent cell divides to produce two **genetically identical daughter cells**. Each of these daughter cell contains the same DNA as the original parent cell.
- **Mitosis** is the division of *somatic cells*. Somatic cells make up most of your body's tissues and organs, including skin, muscles, lungs, gut, and hair cells

	- **Tissue Repair and Replacement**: Mitosis replaces damaged, worn-out, or lost cells. This is crucial for healing wounds, replacing skin cells, and renewing the lining of your digestive tract.

	- **Asexual Reproduction**: In some organisms, mitosis is the basis of asexual reproduction, where a single parent organism produces genetically identical offspring.

- Mitosis undergoes multistep processes during which a cell duplicates all of its contents, including its chromosomes and organelles, which the duplicated chromosomes are aligned, separated, and moved into respective poles then, two new identical daughter cells produced.


- Mitosis is one continuous process, but is often described as a **series of four stages**.

![[Pasted image 20250217193753.png|600]]

##### *Prophase*

- **Prophase is the first step in mitotic cell division** in which the nuclear envelope or nuclear membrane starts to dissociate into small vesicles

- The chromosomes condense (become shorter and thicker) and are now visible under a microscope.

- *centrosomes* migrate to opposite poles (sides) of the cell. Each **centrosome** starts forming **spindle fibres** from there **Centroles**.  

- The *nucleolus disappears* and the nuclear membrane starts to break down, leaving the chromosomes free in the cytoplasm, and the whole process takes 1 hour.

![[Pasted image 20250217195838.png]]
##### *Metaphase*

- **Metaphase** is the second step in the mitosis process. All the chromosomes are aligned at the **metaphase plate or the equatorial plane**, which is at the middle of the cell between the two poles of the cell.

- The chromosomes line up at the equator (middle) of the cell.
- Each chromosome attaches to the **spindle fibers** by their **Kinetochore** on the centromere. 

- At the metaphase checkpoint, each chromosome is checked to ensure it is attached to the spindle.
- this phase takes about 10min.
![[Pasted image 20250217195935.png]]
##### *Anaphase*

- During this phase, **cohesion proteins** linking similar **chromatids** together called the **centromere** breakdown.

- The **centromeres** divide to separate each pair of sister chromatids. 

- The *spindle fibres* contract and shorten(depolymerize) to pull the chromatids to opposite poles of the cell. 

- Each chromatid is pulled by its centromere, causing them to take on a 'V' shape when viewed under a microscope.
- this takes abouts 20min

![[Pasted image 20250217200456.png]]
##### *Telophase*

- The chromatids reach the opposite poles of the cell where they uncoil to become long and thin chromosomes again, **chromatids decondense back into chromatin**. 

- A nuclear envelope forms around each set of chromosomes to form two nuclei and the nucleolus starts to reform.

- The **mitotic spindles are depolymerized**, the nuclear envelopes form around the chromosomes and centrosomes appear within the nuclear area
- this takes about 30min
![[Pasted image 20250217200936.png]]
#### *Cytokinesis*

- After the steps of *mitosis*, the cytoplasm divides in a process known as **cytokinesis**. This produces two daughter cells that are genetically identical to one another.
- the **Completion** in the **animal** **cells** is different from the **plant** **cell**.

    - as **animal** **cells** are surrounded only by **cell** **membranes**, **cytokinesis** enables the **cytoplasm** of the **mother** **cell** to pinch or contact In the **middle**, as a result, two **daughter** **cells** completely separate.

    - as **plant** **cells** are surrounded by a **cell** **wall**, in addition to the **cell** **membrane**, the **cellulose** cannot easily pinch off and fully separate. Instead, a new **wall** will be laid down Between the two **daughter** **cells** called **middle** **lamella**.




#### *Meiosis*

- **Meiosis** is a type of cell division in which a parent cell divides to **form four haploid cells**, each *genetically distinct* from one another.
- it is a type of **karyokinesis** in which the germ cells divide into four genetically distinct daughter cells.
- Meiosis involves a **reduction division** in which the **chromosome number is halved to form haploid cells**. These haploid cells form the *gametes (egg and sperm cells)* in animals and plants and creates genetic variation through **crossing over and independent assortment.**

- **The cell then undergoes 2 divisions:** 

    1. **Meiosis I** -  a cell duplicates all of its contents and divides into two daughter cells
    2. **Meiosis II** -  it divides into four different daughter cells with haploid number of chromosomes


- it has **eight stages**, four for each meiosis, **The two divisions of meiosis contain the same stages as mitosis:** 

    1. **Meiosis I** - Prophase I, metaphase I, anaphase I, and telophase I. 
    2. **Meiosis II** - Prophase II, metaphase II, anaphase II, and telophase II. 

- Remember that cells have already replicated their DNA during interphase and each chromosome is made up of two identical sister chromatids.

    - A **germ cells** (the cell that will undergo meiosis) is **diploid (2n = 46 chromatin)**.
    - It contains **23 pairs of homologous chromosome** (one half from the mother, one half from the father). 

    - **After S-Phase (DNA Replication) in a germ cells** Each **chromatin duplicates**, forming **two identical sister chromatids** connected at the centromere.
    - Now, we have **46 sister chromatids** (so there are **92 chromatids total)**.

    - **Meiosis I** Homologous **chromosomes** form and separate, reducing the number of chromosomes by half.
    - Each new cell now has **23 homologous chromosomes** (each made of sister chromatids with genetic variations).
    - **Meiosis II** Sister chromatids separate.
    - Each gamete (sperm or egg) now has **23 chromatin**.

##### *Meiosis I*

- In diploid organisms, chromosome exists in pairs each members of the pair are called *homologous chromosomes*.

    - **Homologous chromosomes** are **pairs of chromatins** that have the **same size, shape, and gene arrangement** but may carry **different versions (alleles) of genes**. they might be matching but not identical as genes have alleles(different versions).
    - One chromatin in each pair comes from the **mother (maternal chromatin)** and the other from the **father (paternal chromatin).**
    - **Humans have 46 chromatin (23 pairs), meaning 23 homologous pairs.**

###### *prophase I*

- Similar to prophase in mitosis, the chromatin *condenses and thicken* making the chromosomes visible.
- In this stage **centrioles migrate** to opposite poles and the spindle is formed
- The **nuclear envelope breaks down** and the **nucleolus disintegrates**

![[Pasted image 20251117222058.png|260]]
- *Homologous chromosomes* (the two sister chromatids, one from each parent) come together and pair up along their entire length. This precise alignment is called **synapsis**. The paired homologous chromosomes are referred to as a **tetrad or bivalent**, as they consist of **four chromatids**.

- **Crossing Over (Genetic Recombination):** This is a critical event unique to Prophase I. Non-sister chromatids (chromatids from homologous chromosomes) exchange segments of genetic material. This physical exchange of DNA is called **crossing over or genetic recombination**. It creates new combinations of alleles on the chromosomes, contributing significantly to *genetic variation*. The point at which the crossing over occurs is called the **chiasma** (chiasmata; plural)

The pairing of **homologous chromosomes (synapsis)** and the exchange of genetic material between them (**crossing over**) are the defining events of Prophase I. These processes are essential for the accurate segregation of homologous chromosomes in Meiosis I and for generating genetic diversity among the resulting gametes.


###### *Metaphase I*

- The key event of Metaphase I is the alignment of the **homologous chromosome pairs** (the tetrads) at the *metaphase plate*, which is the imaginary plane in the center of the cell.
![[Pasted image 20251117222921.png|260]]

- Unlike metaphase in mitosis, where individual sister chromatids line up, in Metaphase I of meiosis, the **homologous chromosomes** remain paired up and align side-by-side at the metaphase plate. Each tetrad is positioned with one homologous chromosome facing one pole of the cell and the other homologous chromosome facing the opposite pole.

- The orientation of each homologous pair at the metaphase plate is **random**. This means that for each pair of homologous chromosomes, the maternal chromosome is just as likely to face one pole as the paternal chromosome. This random orientation of homologous pairs is known as **independent assortment.**

- *Independent assortment is a second major source of genetic variation* in meiosis (the first being crossing over in Prophase I). The number of possible combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes in the resulting gametes is vast. For a human cell with 23 pairs of chromosomes, there are $2^{23}$ (over 8 million) possible combinations of chromosomes that can end up in a gamete, just from independent assortment alone.  This highlights how meiosis contributes to the genetic diversity of offspring.


###### *Anaphase I*

-  The homologous chromosomes, which were paired up at the metaphase plate, are now pulled apart and move towards opposite poles of the cell. Crucially, the **sister chromatids remain attached at their centromeres** and move together as a unit towards the poles.
![[Pasted image 20251117223633.png|260]]
- in Metaphase I, we had pairs of homologous chromosomes lined up. In Anaphase I, one chromosome from each pair moves to one pole, and the other homologous chromosome moves to the opposite pole. This is different from anaphase in mitosis, where sister chromatids separate.

###### *Telophase I*

- The chromosomes reach the opposite poles of the cell where they uncoil. 
- A nuclear envelope forms around each set of chromosomes and the nucleolus starts to reform.

![[Pasted image 20251117223903.png|260]]

- The cytoplasm divides to form two cells (**cytokinesis**).


The key outcome of Meiosis I is the formation of **two haploid daughter cells**.  Each of these cells contains half the number of chromosomes as the original diploid parent cell. However, each chromosome still consists of sister chromatids. These two haploid cells will now proceed to Meiosis II.

##### *Meiosis II*

Meiosis II is often described as being similar to mitosis, but it occurs in the two haploid cells produced during Meiosis I.  The key difference is that the cells entering Meiosis II are already **haploid**, but their chromosomes are still duplicated (each is a sister chromatid). Meiosis II is an **equational division** because the chromosome number remains the same in the daughter cells as it was in the cells entering Meiosis II.

- **Prophase II**: The chromosomes, which may have decondensed slightly in Telophase I, condense again. The nuclear envelope (if it reformed) breaks down, and the spindle fibers forms.

- **Metaphase II**: The chromosomes, each still composed of two sister chromatids, line up individually along the metaphase plate, similar to metaphase in mitosis.

- **Anaphase II**: The centromeres of the sister chromatids finally separate, and the sister chromatids (now considered individual chromatins) are pulled by the spindle fibers towards opposite poles of the cell.

- **Telophase II**: The chromatins arrive at opposite poles and begin to decondense. Nuclear envelopes reform around each set of chromatin, and cytokinesis occurs, resulting in the formation of four genetically distinct haploid daughter cells.

Comparing Meiosis II to mitosis in terms of sister chromatid separation is straightforward: The key difference lies in the starting cells: *mitosis begins with a diploid cell, while Meiosis II begins with haploid cells that have replicated chromosomes.*
