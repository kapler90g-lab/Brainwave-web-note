#atomic_structure_and_periodic_properties_of_elements #grade9 #unit2 

- Grouping elements with similar properties together. This process is known as **Classification of Elements**

## **1. Early Attempts to Classifying Elements**

- Early attempts were based on **their atomic mass**.
- Scientists began to seek relationships between the **atomic masses and properties of elements**.

### i) **Dobereiner's Triads**
The first attempts to group similar elements was attempted by **J. Dobereiner**. In **1817**, he put together similar elements in a group of three or **Triads**. According to Dobereiner, when elements are arranged in triads in order of increasing atomic masses, the middle element has an average atomic mass of the other two elements.

**Examples of Triads**:

| Element   | Atomic Mass |
|-----------|-------------|
| Chlorine  | 35.5        |
| Bromine   | 80          |
| Iodine    | 127         |

Average atomic mass of I and Br = $\frac{35.5 + 127}{2} = 81.25$

### ii) **Newlands' Law of Octaves**
In **1864**, **J. Newland** reported the law of **Octaves**, which is also known as **The Law of Eights**. According to Newland, when elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic mass, every eighth element had similarity in properties to the first Element

**Example:**

Li       Be         B        C        N        O            F  

Na     Mg       Al       Si        P          S           Cl 

K        Ca

However, the law of Octaves could not be applied beyond Calcium.

## 2 **Modern Periodic Table**
#### **A. Mendeleev's Periodic Law**
- The chemical and physical properties of elements occur at regular intervals. This is called the **Periodic Law**.
- According to **Mendeleev's Periodic Law**, the elements are a periodic function of their atomic mass.
- Only 63 elements were known when he constructed his table in **1871**.
- Mendeleev left two blank spaces for these two elements. Just under aluminum and silicon, he called the unknown elements "eka-aluminum" and "eka-silicon" (eka means first). When found, "Eka-aluminum" is currently known as Gallium, following aluminum.

So, Gallium, named by Mendeleev's system, is **Eka-aluminum**.

##### **Defects in Mendeleev's periodic table**

1. **Position of Isotopes** - The isotopes were not given a separated place in the M.P's periodic table.
   - Since elements are arranged in increasing atomic number, the isotopes belong to different groups *(because isotopes have different masses)*.

2. **Wrong order of atomic masses of some elements**
   - For example, Argon with atomic mass of 39.95 precedes Potassium with atomic mass of 39.1.

### B. Modern Periodic Table

In **1913**, the English physicist **Henry Moseley** determined the atomic number of each element by analyzing their X-ray spectra. *No two elements can have the same atomic number.* For example, the atomic number of $O_2$ is 8, so no other element can have an atomic number of 8.

Modern periodic law states that:

>**"elements are organized by a function of their atomic number."**

So, when elements are arranged according to their increasing atomic number, elements with the same chemical and physical properties fall in the same group.

## Characteristics of Groups and Periods

Based on the modern periodic law, in the modern periodic table, elements are arranged in periods and groups. 
### periods

**Periods** are horizontal rows of elements in the periodic table (series). 

- Elements in a period are arranged in increasing order of their atomic masses from right to left.
- There are **7 periods** in the modern periodic table, and each period is represented by an Arabic numeral 1, 2, … and 7.
- Elements in the same period have the same shell.
- **Period 1, 2, and 3** are called **Short periods** 
- while period **4, 5, 6** are known as **Long periods**.
- **Period 1** contains only **2 elements**, H₂ and He.
- **Period 2** and **Period 3** contain **8 elements each**.
- **Period 4** and **5** contain **18 elements each, period 6** the longest period has **32 elements**.
- **Period 7** which is an **Incomplete period**. Contains more than **28 elements**, **Period 7** elements are **radio-active and artificial elements**.

**Except for the first period**, all periods start with an **alkali metal** and end with **noble gas**.

| Period No | Orbitals Occupied | No. Elements         |
|-----------|--------------------|----------------------|
| 1         | 1S                 | 2                    |
| 2         | 2S, 2P             | 8                    |
| 3         | 3S, 3P, 3D         | 18 and so on...      |

**No shell = No period**

_Example:_

$$ ^{23}_{11}Na = 1S^2 2S^2 2P^6 3S^1 \quad (2, 8, 1) $$

So, Na has three main shells so Na is found on the period 3.

### group

- **Group (family):** are the vertical columns of element in the periodic table.
- There are **18 Groups** in the modern periodic table.
- Groups $\text{IA}$ to $\text{VIIA}$ are designated with Roman numbers I to VIII each by **A and B.**

  There are:
  
 IA --------- VIIIA **Main Groups (A Groups)**
 IB ---------- VIIIB **Sub Groups (B Groups)**
  
- Some groups have the same number of **outermost electrons**.
- Elements in the same group have the same **chemical properties**.
- For main group elements, the group number equals to the number of **valance electrons**.

  Example: E.g., Cl $Z=17$;
  
  $Cl = [1s^2, 2s^2, 2p^6, 3s^2, 3p^5]$
  
  The valence electron of Cl is 7. Hence, it is found in Group VIIA.

- There are **18 vertical columns** but **16 groups.**
- The **16 groups** are classified into two: these are **8 main groups and 8 sub-groups.**

  - For 'A' Group Elements:
    - Group number = valance electron

  - For all elements:
    - Period No = number of *shells* or number of *energy levels*.

  - For 'B' Group Elements:
    - Use the formula: $$ns(n-1)d$$

## Classification of Elements

Elements in the periodic table can be classified into **three** distinct categories based on their electron configuration and type of sublevel being filled. These are:

- **Representative elements**
- **Transition elements**
- **Rare-earth elements**

## 1. Representative Elements: S and P-Block Elements

These are elements in which valence electrons are filled in the *s* and *p* orbitals. Representative elements are also known as **main group elements (IA-VIIIA)**.

### **S-block elements**
  - Are elements in which the last electron enters the *s*-orbital of the outermost shell.
  - General electron valence shell electron configuration is $ns^1$ and $ns^2$, where $n$ represents the outermost shell.
  - Found on the **left side of the periodic table** and contain the first two groups: **Group IA** and **Group IIA**. These two groups contain very reactive metals.
  
### **P-block elements**
- **One element in which the last electron enters _p_-orbital** of the outermost shell.
- **They have General Valence Electron Configuration of:**  
  $$ns^2np^1 - ns^2np^6$$ 
- **p-block elements are located at the right-hand side.**
- **They include 6 Groups (IIIA - VIIIA) most are nonmetals**

  | **Group No** | **Group name**      | **General Valence Shell electron** | **No valence $e^-$** |
  |--------------|---------------------|------------------------------------|----------------------|
  | IA           | Alkali metals       | $ns^1$                             | 1                    |
  | IIA          | Alkali earth metals | $ns^2$                             | 2                    |
  | IIIA         | Boron family        | $ns^2np^1$                         | 3                    |
  | IVA          | Carbon family       | $ns^2np^2$                         | 4                    |
  | VA           | Nitrogen family     | $ns^2np^3$                         | 5                    |
  | VIA          | Chalcogen (oxygen)  | $ns^2np^4$                         | 6                    |
  | VIIA         | Halogens            | $ns^2np^5$                         | 7                    |
  | VIIIA        | Noble Gas (inert gas)| $ns^2np^6$                        | 8                    |

1. **Except H: S-block**  
2. **Except He: p-block**

## **Transition elements (metals) d-block**

* **They are found between s-block and p-block**
* In these elements the valence electron is added to the d-orbital of outermost shells. They are known as d-block elements.
- They are '**B** Group (Sub-group)' elements ($\mathrm{IB-VIIIB}$);
- They are found between IIIA and IIIB;
- Transition metals are found in the period *4-7*. 
    - This includes Iron, Gold, and Copper.
- **D-Block Elements** are sometimes called transition metals.

## **Rare-Earth Elements**: _f-block elements_
   - Is also known as **Inner transition metals**
   - Are elements in which the valence electron can be being added to the **f-Orbital** of the outermost shell. 
   - Are found in the 6<sup>th</sup> and 7<sup>th</sup> period. *(Lantanids and actinides)*
   - The 6<sup>th</sup> period’s inner transition metals series fill the $4f$ orbitals, known as **Lanthanide Series**.
   - The 7<sup>th</sup> period’s inner transition elements series fill the $5f$ orbitals, known as **Actinide Series**.


- The properties of elements such as **atomic size**, **Ionization Energy**, **Electron affinity**, and **Electronegativity** 
  - show a regular difference with in a group or series across a period.
# Periodic properties within a Group

- The no of valence **electrons** determine the group no of an element.
- The periodic properties of elements can be explained on the basis of **Nuclear Charge** and **Effective Nuclear Charge**.

### 1. **Nuclear Charge (Z)**

- Is the total positive charge in the nucleus of an atom.
- It increases down a group and across a period.

### 2. **Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_eff$)**

- The net positive nuclear charge attracting the valence electron is called $E.\ N.\ C.$ $Z_{eff}$.
- The $Z_{eff}$ is the difference between **Nuclear Charge (Z)** and the inner electrons (S) that shield the valence electron.

$$Z_{eff} = Z - S$$
- The fact that the inner electron shield or screen the outer electrons from full charge of the nucleus is known as **Shielding or Screening effect**.
- $Z_{eff}$ increases across a period and decreases down a group.

## Vary In regular periodic manner 

#### **Atomic Size / Atomic Radius**

- **The size of an atom is defined in terms of atomic radius.**
- It is defined as **half the distance ($d$)** between two chemically bonded atoms in a molecule.
- **Atomic radius** equals to the **one half the diameter** (the distance $d$: the nuclei of an atom in the molecules).

  $$
  \text{Atomic radius} (r) = \frac{d}{2}
  $$
  **Example:**
  
  $$
  Br_2 = \frac{2 \times 1.14 \, \text{Å}}{2} = 1.14 \, \text{Å}
  $$

- _Atomic radii_ is very short as a result. It is measured with _angstrom_ (Å).

  $$
  1 \, \text{Å} = 10^{-10} \, \text{m} = 10^{-8} \, \text{cm}
  $$

- **Atomic size increases** down a group because No shell increases$\uparrow$ but **decreases across a period** because Zeff $\uparrow$

>[!Note] 
>Atomic size of positive ion is less than the neutral form. Example: $K^+ < K$
  Atomic size of negative ion is greater than the neutral. Example: $K^- > K$.



#### **Ionization Energy (IE)**

- It is the amount of **energy** required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom.
- Ionization is represented by the following equation (where M denotes any metal):

$$
M(g) + \text{energy} \rightarrow M^+(g) + e^-
$$

- *First* ionization energy is the energy needed to remove the first valence electron.
- *Second* ionization energy is the energy needed to remove the second valence electron and so on.

$$
M(g) + \text{1st energy} \rightarrow M^+(g) + e^- \quad \text{ *First Ionization* }
$$

$$
M^{+}(g) + \text{2nd energy} \rightarrow M^{2+}(g) + 2e^- \quad \text{ *Second Ionization* }
$$

- For any element the _**second**_ ionization energy is greater than the first one.
- Ionization energy is always a **positive value** **B/C** energy is required to remove an electron from an atom. Ionization energy is measured in **Electron Volts (eV)** or **Kilo Joules per mole (kJ/mol)**.
- **Ionization energy is a measure of the tendency of an atom to lose an electron.
    - Metals easily lose electrons thus they have **low** ionization energy.
    - Non-metals have high ionization energy **B/C** they do not easily lose electrons.
- **Ionization energy** *decreases* down a group and *increases* across a period.

##### **Factors affecting Ionization**

1. **Atomic Size**
    - As atomic size *increases*, **Ionization Energy** *decreases*, and vice versa.

2. **Effective nuclear charge**
    - The smaller the $Z_{eff}$, the lower the energy needed to remove the electron from the atom.

3. **Types of Electron**
    - The closer the electron to the nucleus, the more difficult it is to remove the electron.
    - *S*-electron is closer to the nucleus than *P*-electron.
    - So on, $$s > p > d > f$$

4. **Screening (shielding) effect of Inner Electron**
    - As the number of inner electrons increases, **Ionization energy** decreases.

5. **Electron Configuration (Stability)**
    - Full-filled and half-filled are stable and require more energy to remove electrons from them.
    - Metals have low **Ionization energy**.
    - Non-metals have higher **Ionization energy**.
    - Ionization energy of nitrogen is greater than oxygen due to half filled orbital in nitrogen

### **Electro Affinity (Ea)**

- The tendency of an atom to form a negative ion is expressed in terms of **electron affinity** (*Ea*).

- It is a measure of the *attraction* or *affinity* of the atom for the extra added electron.

$$X(g) + e^- \rightarrow X^-(g) + \text{energy}$$

- It is defined as the energy released in the **KiloJoule/mol**.

**Example:** When an electron is added to a fluorine (F) atom 
328 $\text{KJ/mol}$ of energy is released to produce a fluorine ion (F<sup>-</sup>) and $E_A$ is -328 $\text{KJ/mol}$
$F(g) + e^-  \rightarrow F^-(g) + EA = -328 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$

- **Electron affinity** is a measure of the strength of an atom to attract an additional electron.

>[!Note]
> $EA$ depends on atomic size and $Z_{eff}$  
_The smaller the atomic size, the stronger to form a negative ion._

### Electronegativity

- Is the _ability_ of an atom in a molecule to attract the shared electrons in a chemical bond.
- **Pauling Scale** ranges from 0.7 to 4.0.
- **Fluorine**, the most **electronegative** element, is assigned a value of 4.0.

And the least ___electronegative___ element, Cesium, has an ___electronegativity value___ of 0.7.

_For all the rest elements_, the values lie between these extremes.

>[!Note]
Electronegativity depends on ionization energy $IE$ and $EA$. An atom with high $Z_{eff}$ and high $EA$ also tends to have high electronegativity value, because it have a strong attraction for a chemical bond.

### **Metallic Characteristics**

- Metals have a tendency to lose **electrons** and form __*positive ion*__. as a result that are called **electropositive element**
- Metallic characteristics increase down a group.
- Metals and non-metals are separated by a border and elements near to this border are called **metalloids**.
- Metals are found on the left side, while non-metals
  are found on the right side.

## **Periodic properties within a Period**

1. **Variance electron**
  - __Group (down)__: the same
  - __Period (left to right across)__: increases

2. **Nuclear Charge**
  - __Group__: Increases
  - __Period__: Increases

3. **Atomic Size**
  - __Group__: Increases
  - __Period__: decreases

4. **Ionization energy**
  - __Group__: decreases
  - __Period__: Increases

5. **Electronegativity**
  - __Group__: decreases
  - __Period__: Increases

6. **Electron Affinity**
    - Group
        - decreases
    - Period
        - Increases

7. **Metallic Characteristics**
    - Group
        - *Increases*
    - Period
        - decreases
