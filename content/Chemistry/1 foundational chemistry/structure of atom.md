## **Atomic Theory**
### *Historical Perspective on Atoms*

- Around the middle of the 5th century BCE, **Empedocles** proposed that all matter was made of four fundamental substances: earth, air, water, and fire. This was a significant idea that attempted to explain the diversity of materials in the world through combinations of these basic elements.

- Later, **Plato**, a student of Socrates, adopted **Empedocles' theory** and is credited with coining the term "**element**" to describe these four substances. His most famous student, **Aristotle**, also embraced the concept of the four elements. Aristotle further developed this idea by introducing properties like hot/cold and wet/dry to differentiate the elements and explain transformations of matter.

- Alongside these ideas, **Leucippus** is credited with originating the concept of atomism around 440 BCE. However, much of what we know about this early atomic theory comes from his student, **Democritus** (460-370 BCE). Democritus, born in Abdera, significantly elaborated on the atomic theory, developing extensive works on the atomic structure of the physical world, the universe, and the concept of *void space*. Although a philosopher, his ideas on the structure of matter were remarkably consistent with later scientific discoveries, earning him recognition as a pioneer in the history of physics and chemistry.

Democritus's theory of atomism held that the universe is composed of indivisible and indestructible particles called **atoms**, moving in a void (empty space).

The early concept of atoms was simply a result of thinking and reasoning on the part of the philosophers, instead of experimental observations. In 1803, however, **John Dalton** proposed a completely different theory of matter. His theory was based on *scientific experimental observations and logical laws*. These scientific assumptions were very closely related to what is presently known about the atom. Due to this, John Dalton is often referred to as the **father of modern atomic theory**.


### *The laws of chemical combinations*

>  They describe the fundamental principles that govern how elements combine with each other during chemical reactions to produce compounds

- These laws, established through careful experimentation in the 18th and early 19th centuries, provided the empirical evidence needed to move beyond philosophical speculation and towards a more scientific understanding of matter.


- There are [five main laws of chemical combination](https://images.topperlearning.com/topper/revisionnotes/3707_Topper_21_101_3_2_27_32_Laws_of_Chemical_Combination_up201605111103_1462944788_0099.pdf?v=0.0.1).  We'll focus on three key ones: 
 - **The law of conservation of mass.**  
  - **The law of definite proportion.**  
  - **The law of multiple proportion.**

#### *The law of conservation of mass*

>[!Note]- The Law of conservation of mass
> This law states that **mass is neither created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction**. In simpler terms, the total mass of all the substances involved before a chemical reaction (the reactants) is equal to the total mass of all the substances present after the reaction (the products).

- *Antoine Lavoisier*, a French chemist often considered the "**father of modern chemistry**," is credited with formalizing this law in the late 18th century. Through meticulous experiments, he showed that in a closed system, the mass of the reactants before a reaction always equaled the mass of the products after the reaction.

#### *The law of definite proportions*

>[!note]- The law of definite proportions
> a given **chemical compound** always contains the *same elements in the exact same proportions by mass*, regardless of the source of the compound or how it was prepared.

- This law, attributed to the French chemist *Joseph Proust* in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, states that **a given chemical compound always contains the same elements in the exact same proportions by mass, regardless of the source of the compound or how it was prepared**

    - For example, *pure water (H₂O)* will always consist of hydrogen and oxygen in a fixed mass ratio of **approximately 1:8**. Whether the water comes from a river, a lab, or rain, this mass ratio remains constant. This law implies that a specific compound has a unique and unchanging composition.

#### *The law of multiple proportions*

>[!Note]- The law of multiple proportions
> When two elements combine to form **more than one compound**, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in ratios of small whole numbers. 

- This law, proposed by John Dalton, states that **when two elements can combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in ratios of small whole numbers**.

	- Consider the elements carbon and oxygen. They can combine to form two different compounds: **carbon monoxide (CO)** and **carbon dioxide (CO₂).** If we fix the mass of carbon at 1 gram, we see that the masses of oxygen that combine with it are 1.33 grams (in CO) and 2.66 grams (in CO₂). The ratio of these masses of oxygen (2.66 g / 1.33 g) is approximately 2:1, a simple whole number ratio. This observation supports the Law of Multiple Proportions. 
	
	- Suppose you want to compare **how much oxygen** combines with **the same fixed amount of carbon**.
	    - In CO: 12 g of carbon combines with 16 g oxygen.
        - In CO₂: 12 g of carbon combines with 32 g oxygen.
     - Now, to compare fairly, you **fix the carbon** (keep it the same). Then you look at the oxygen.
        - Oxygen in CO : Oxygen in CO₂
        - **16 g : 32 g = 1 : 2**
    That 1:2 ratio tells you oxygen comes in **whole atoms**: either one O or two O’s per C.

- *atoms* exist in a whole form and no atom can exist in a fraction form meaning for example we can't have half an oxygen atom.
- This law is about how *different elements with different proportions form different compounds* like in the case of carbon here when using 2 oxygen we get CO₂ and when using 1 oxygen we get CO.

### *Dalton's Atomic Theory*

**John Dalton**, an English chemist, proposed his atomic theory in the early 19th century.  This theory was a significant step in the development of chemistry and provided a framework for understanding the nature of matter. It was based on **experimental observations and chemical laws** known at the time, such as the **Law of Conservation of Mass and the Law of Definite Proportions.**

Dalton, being a meticulous experimenter and thinker, studied these laws and conducted his own experiments, particularly with gases. He observed that gases with different densities would mix uniformly rather than separating into layers, which suggested they were composed of discrete particles in motion.

His most compelling evidence came from his work on the composition of compounds and his formulation of the **Law of Multiple Proportions**. This law, which Dalton himself proposed, states that if two elements can combine to form more than one compound, then the ratios of the masses of the second element that combine with a fixed mass of the first element are in ratios of small whole numbers. This could be explained if atoms combined in simple whole-number ratios.

**Basic Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory:**

1. **All Elements are Composed of Atoms**

Dalton proposed that all matter, whether an element, compound, or mixture, is made up of extremely small, discrete particles called *atoms*. He envisioned these atoms as solid, **indivisible spheres, much like billiard balls**. This was a significant departure from the idea of continuous matter.

> [!note] **Atoms**
>  Are *smallest fundamental particles* of an element

2. **Atoms Cannot Be Created or Destroyed**

Dalton stated that atoms are indivisible and cannot be created or destroyed during a chemical reaction. Chemical reactions are simply rearrangements of these existing atoms. This postulate directly supported the *Law of Conservation of Mass*, as the total number and type of atoms remain constant, thus conserving the total mass.

3. **Atoms of the Same Element are Identical and atoms of different elements are not**

According to Dalton, all atoms of a specific element are exactly alike. They have the same mass, size, and chemical properties. Conversely, atoms of different elements are different; they have different masses, sizes, and properties. This explained why elements have unique characteristics.

4. **Atoms Combine in Whole Number Ratios**

Dalton's theory proposed that atoms of different elements combine in fixed, simple, whole-number ratios to form compounds. This explained **the Law of Definite Proportions**, where a given compound always has the same elemental composition by mass. It also led to the **Law of Multiple Proportions**, which further supported the idea of atoms combining in discrete units.


**Limitations of Dalton's Atomic Theory:**

while revolutionary for its time and successful in explaining the laws of chemical combination known then, later scientific discoveries would reveal that some of these postulates needed modification.

**Atoms are not Indivisible**: Dalton believed atoms were the ultimate, indivisible particles of matter. However, we now know that atoms are composed of subatomic particles: protons, neutrons, and electrons.

**Atoms of the Same Element Can Have Different Masses (Isotopes)**: Dalton stated that all atoms of a given element are identical in mass. This was disproven by the discovery of isotopes. Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) but with different numbers of neutrons, and therefore, different masses. For example, Carbon-12 and Carbon-14 are both carbon, but they have different masses.

**Atoms of Different Elements Can Have the Same Mass (Isobars)**: Dalton's theory implied that atoms of different elements would always have different masses. However, the existence of isobars shows this isn't always true. Isobars are atoms of different elements (different numbers of protons) that have the same mass number (sum of protons and neutrons). For instance, Argon-40 and Calcium-40 have approximately the same atomic mass but are different elements.



Dalton's theory was based on the knowledge available in the early 19th century. The discovery of subatomic particles and isotopes didn't make his theory entirely wrong, but rather showed that the atom is more complex than he initially envisioned. His fundamental contributions, like the concept of atoms and their combination in fixed ratios, are still central to chemistry.


### *Modern Atomic Theory*

Building upon the foundation laid by Dalton, and incorporating the discoveries of subatomic particles and the atomic nucleus, scientists developed the modern atomic theory. This theory provides a more accurate and detailed picture of the atom.

It's a more complex model that acknowledges the **subatomic nature of atoms and the probabilistic nature of electron location.**

**Key Principles of the Modern Atomic Theory:**

1. **Atoms** are the smallest particles that make up the elements and participate in chemical reactions.
2. atoms are neither created nor destroyed during any ordinary chemical reactions.
3.  atoms of the same elements have the same **atomic number** but different **mass number** this is because of the existence of Isotopes.
4. atoms of different elements are different.
5. atoms combine in a simple whole number ratio to form compounds.


## **Discoveries of the Fundamental Subatomic Particles**

In late 1880's Jhon Dalton thought atoms were indivisible but a series of experiments demonstrated that atoms are made of even more smaller particles, called *subatomic particles*. There are three fundamental subatomic particles in which every atom in the universe is made of those are: **Electrons, protons and neutrons**

### *Discovery of Electrons*

In 1879, a man called **William crook** studied electrical discharges from in a partially evacuated tubes called **discharge tubes or crook's tubes.**

This tubes consisted of **two electrodes** connected to a high voltage source where air in the glass tube has been evacuated. the negative electrode is the **cathode** and the positive electrode is the **anode**. this tube also consists of an ammeter to measure the flow of current and the glass tube consisted of a zinc sulphide fluorescent screen detector. when the voltage was turned on the glass tube emitted a greenish light beam completing the circuit upon further investigation it was discovered that the greenish lights were moving in straight line from the cathode to the anode  thus they called it **The cathode rays**. 

Scientists were intrigued by these cathode rays and conducted various experiments to understand their nature. **J.J. Thomson's experiments** were particularly insightful. He designed a cathode ray tube with a **fluorescent screen** at the end to visualize the path of the rays and incorporated **electric and magnetic fields**:

- **Applying Electric Field**: When Thomson applied an electric field across the cathode rays, he observed that the rays were deflected towards the positive plate. This indicated that the particles making up the cathode rays carried a *negative charge*.

- **Applying Magnetic Field**: When he applied a magnetic field, the cathode rays were also deflected, but in a direction consistent with negatively charged particles moving through a magnetic field. By carefully controlling and measuring the strengths of both **electric and magnetic fields**, Thomson could determine the *[[charge-to-mass ratio]] (e/m)* of the particles.

- **Varying Electrode Material and Gas**: Crucially, Thomson found that the charge-to-mass ratio of the cathode ray particles was the same regardless of the material used for the electrodes or the type of gas in the tube. This suggested that these negatively charged particles were a fundamental component of all matter.

**Properties of Cathode Rays:**

1. when an object was paced between the two electrodes a shadow was formed at the opposite end of the tube, this showed that cathode rays are moving in **straight line** in the absence of electric or magnetic field.

2. when a rotating paddle was placed in the path of the cathode rays, the paddle starts to rotate this showed that the rays are not waves but actual particles together.

3. The cathodes rays deflect in the the direction of the positive plate when exposed to electric field and deflected in the direction in which negatively charged material would deflect when exposed to a magnetic field, this showed that the **cathode rays are negatively charged**. this in turn allowed Thomson to calculate the charge-to-mass ratio of the particles of the cathode rays. 

$$ 
\frac{e}{m} = 1.76 \times 10^{8} \, \text{C/kg} 
$$

4. the properties of the cathode rays like the charge to mass ratio is independent of the material used  This suggested that these negatively charged particles were a fundamental component of all matter.

From his experiments, Thomson concluded that cathode rays were streams of negatively charged particles.He initially called these particles "**corpuscles**," but they were later named *electrons*. 

#### *Robert Millikan's Experiment to determine the charge of an electron*
Following J.J. Thomson's discovery of the electron and his determination of its charge-to-mass ratio, the next crucial step was to determine the actual charge of a single electron. This was achieved by Robert Millikan through his ingenious [[oil drop experiment]], conducted between 1909 and 1917.

Here's a breakdown of the experiment's setup and how it worked:


- **The materials**: The core of the experiment was a chamber with two parallel metal plates, one above the other, connected to a voltage source to create a uniform electric field between them. A fine mist of oil droplets was sprayed into the chamber above the top plate.

- **Observing the Droplets**: Some of the oil droplets would fall through a small hole in the top plate into the region between the plates. These droplets would acquire a negative charge, often through friction during the atomization process or by being exposed to ionizing radiation like X-rays. Millikan observed the motion of individual droplets using a microscope.

- **Balancing Forces**: Without an electric field applied, the oil droplets would fall due to gravity, reaching a terminal velocity determined by their mass and the air resistance (drag force). When the electric field was turned on, the negatively charged droplets would experience an upward electric force. Millikan adjusted the voltage across the plates to make the electric force exactly balance the gravitational force, causing some droplets to remain suspended in mid-air.

By measuring the voltage required to suspend a droplet and knowing the distance between the plates (to calculate the electric field strength), Millikan could determine the electric force on the droplet. By also calculating the mass of the droplet (based on its terminal velocity when no electric field was applied), he could then determine the charge on the droplet using the relationship: 

$$ Electric Force = Charge × Electric Field$$

Millikan repeatedly measured the charges on many different oil droplets. He made a crucial observation: **the measured charges were always found to be integer multiples of a smallest, fundamental value** and that value is approximately equal to 160 zeptocoloumbs so if two electrons it is equal to 320 zeptocoloumbs and so on. This strongly suggested that electric charge is quantized – it exists in discrete packets, and the smallest packet is the charge of a single electron which is equal to:
$$ 
e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C} 
$$

This experiment provided compelling evidence for the existence of the electron as a fundamental particle carrying a specific, quantized charge. Combined with Thomson's charge-to-mass ratio, Millikan's value for the electron's charge allowed for the calculation of the electron's mass, which was found to be extremely small compared to atoms.

$$
\text{Mass of } e^{-} = \frac{1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}}{1.76 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C/kg}}
= 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}
$$

So an electron is a fundamental particle of an atom carrying a negative charge and having a very small mass.


### *Discovery of Atomic Nucleus*

#### *Radioactivity*

>[!note] Radioactivity
>Radioactivity is the **spontaneous emission of radiation** from the nucleus of an **unstable atom**. This process occurs when an atomic nucleus has an unstable balance of protons and neutrons and releases energy and/or particles to become more stable.

The discovery of this phenomenon showed that atoms were not immutable and unchanging, as previously thought, but could undergo transformation.

Let's look at the key figures and discoveries in the history of radioactivity:

- **Wilhelm Röntgen**: In 1895, German physicist *Wilhelm Röntgen* discovered **X-rays**, a form of electromagnetic radiation. While not radioactivity itself, this discovery sparked interest in invisible rays emitted by materials.

- **Henri Becquerel**: Inspired by Röntgen's work, French physicist *Henri Becquerel* investigated whether phosphorescent materials emitted similar rays. In 1896, he discovered that uranium salts emitted a new type of invisible radiation that could expose *photographic plates*, even in the dark. This was the discovery of **radioactivity**.

- **Marie Curie and Pierre Curie**: *Marie Curie*, a Polish-French physicist and chemist, and her husband *Pierre Curie*, a French physicist, were pivotal in the early study of radioactivity. They investigated various substances and, through painstaking chemical separation, discovered two new radioactive elements: polonium and radium in 1898. Marie Curie also coined the term "**radioactivity**."


Further research into radioactivity revealed that the emissions were not all of the same type. **Ernest Rutherford**, a student of J.J. Thomson, played a key role in identifying and characterizing the different types of radiation emitted by radioactive substances. He named them alpha, beta, and gamma rays based on their penetrating power and how they were deflected by electric and magnetic fields here are the three types of Radioactive Emissions:

1. **Alpha (α) Rays**: These consist of *alpha particles*, which are positively charged and relatively heavy. They were found to be helium nuclei (two protons and two neutrons). Alpha particles have low penetrating power and can be stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimeters of air.
2. **Beta (β) Rays**: These consist of *beta particles*, which are high-energy electrons. They are negatively charged and are much lighter than alpha particles. Beta particles have moderate penetrating power and can be stopped by a few millimeters of aluminum.
3. **Gamma (γ) Rays**: These are a form of **electromagnetic radiation**, similar to X-rays but with higher energy. They are neutral and have no mass. Gamma rays are highly penetrating and require thick layers of lead or concrete to be significantly shielded.

#### *The Gold foil Experiment*

Building on the discoveries of radioactivity and the electron, **Ernest Rutherford** and his colleagues conducted a series of experiments that dramatically changed the understanding of atomic structure. The most famous of these is the gold foil experiment, also known as the **Geiger-Marsden experiment**, performed by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden under Rutherford's supervision in 1909.

At the time, the prevailing model of the atom was J.J. Thomson's "**plum pudding**" model, *which proposed that the atom was a sphere of uniformly distributed positive charge with negatively charged electrons embedded within it, like plums in a pudding*.  Rutherford's experiment was designed to test this model by observing how alpha particles interacted with a thin piece of gold foil.

---

Here's how the experiment was set up and what the key observations were:

- **Alpha Particle Source**: A radioactive source that emitted **alpha particles** (which were known to be positively charged helium nuclei) was placed in a lead box with a small opening. This created a narrow beam of alpha particles directed towards the gold foil.

- **Thin Gold Foil**: A very thin **sheet of gold foil** was placed in the path of the alpha particle beam. Gold was chosen because it is a very malleable metal and can be made into an extremely thin sheet, minimizing the chance of alpha particles interacting with more than one atom at a time.

- **Fluorescent Screen**: A circular fluorescent screen surrounded the gold foil. When an alpha particle struck the screen, it produced a tiny flash of light  that could be observed and counted. This allowed the researchers to track the paths of the alpha particles after they passed through (or were deflected by) the gold foil.
---

- Based on the plum pudding model, Rutherford and his colleagues expected that the positively charged alpha particles would pass straight through the thin gold foil with little or no deflection.  This was because, in the plum pudding model, the positive charge was thought to be spread out over the entire volume of the atom, and the electrons were too light to significantly deflect the much heavier alpha particles.

- The vast majority of alpha particles went straight through the gold foil without any deflection, as predicted. This suggested that atoms are *mostly empty space*. A small percentage of alpha particles were deflected from their original path by large angles. This was unexpected based on the plum pudding model. Remarkably, a very small number of alpha particles (about 1 in 20,000) were deflected backwards towards the source. Rutherford famously remarked that this was "It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper, and it came back at you!"

These observations could not be explained by the plum pudding model. The significant deflections, especially the backward scattering, indicated that the positive charge and most of the mass of the atom must be concentrated in a very small, dense region within the atom. Rutherford called this region the **nucleus**.


### *The discovery of the proton*

Rutherford's gold foil experiment provided strong evidence for a dense, positively charged *nucleus at the center of the atom*. However, it didn't explicitly identify the nature of that positive charge. The discovery of the **proton**, the positively charged particle within the nucleus, also involved Ernest Rutherford and further experiments building on the understanding of radioactivity and atomic structure.

- After the **gold foil experiment**, Rutherford continued his research, bombarding various gases with alpha particles. In 1917, while working with nitrogen gas, he observed that the alpha particles colliding with nitrogen atoms produced positively charged particles that traveled much farther than the alpha particles themselves. These particles were identified as **hydrogen nuclei**.

- Rutherford knew that hydrogen was the lightest element, and its atom consisted of just one electron and, presumably, a single unit of positive charge to balance the electron's negative charge. He hypothesized that the hydrogen nucleus was a fundamental particle and a constituent of the nuclei of other atoms. He proposed that when alpha particles struck nitrogen nuclei, they caused a nuclear reaction that knocked out a *hydrogen nucleus*.

This was a revolutionary idea – that one element could be transformed into another through nuclear bombardment, a process called nuclear transmutation. The equation for the nuclear reaction Rutherford observed is:

$$¹⁴N + ⁴He → ¹⁷O + ¹H$$
(Nitrogen nucleus + Alpha particle → Oxygen nucleus + Hydrogen nucleus)

Rutherford's detection of these hydrogen nuclei emanating from the nitrogen confirmed his hypothesis that the hydrogen nucleus was a fundamental positively charged particle present in the nuclei of other atoms. He named this particle the **proton**, derived from the Greek word for "first," reflecting his belief that it was a primary building block of all atomic nuclei. This discovery provided a specific identity to the positive charge that Rutherford had earlier located in the atomic nucleus.

### *The discovery of the Neutron*

The discovery of the neutron was the final piece of the puzzle in identifying the fundamental building blocks of the atomic nucleus. For some time after the discovery of the proton, scientists realized there were still unanswered questions about atomic mass and the composition of the nucleus. The discovery of the **neutron** is credited to **James Chadwick** in 1932.

- Following Rutherford's nuclear model, it was understood that the nucleus contained positively charged protons. However, *the total mass of the protons in most nuclei was less than the actual atomic mass of the element*. For example, a helium nucleus was known to have a charge of +2 (indicating two protons), but its mass was approximately four times the mass of a proton. This suggested the presence of other particles in the nucleus that contributed to the mass but not the charge.
- Rutherford himself had speculated about the existence of a neutral particle with a mass similar to that of a proton, which he called a "**neutron**." However, experimental evidence for this particle was elusive for several years.

The key to the neutron's discovery came from experiments conducted by other scientists in the late 1920s and early 1930s. They observed that when certain light elements, such as **beryllium, boron, or lithium**, were bombarded with alpha particles, a highly penetrating, uncharged radiation was emitted. This radiation was mysterious because it was not deflected by electric or magnetic fields (indicating no charge) but was very energetic and could knock protons out of materials like paraffin wax.

James Chadwick, working at the Cavendish Laboratory (where Rutherford was the director), meticulously investigated this mysterious radiation. He designed experiments to measure the energy of the particles in this radiation by observing how they transferred energy to protons in different materials. By **applying the principles of conservation of energy and momentum to the collisions between the incoming neutral radiation and the protons**, Chadwick was able to determine the mass of the neutral particle.

Chadwick's experiments conclusively showed that this penetrating neutral radiation consisted of particles with a mass very close to that of a proton but carrying no electric charge. This was the elusive neutron that Rutherford had hypothesized. Chadwick published his findings in 1932, providing the definitive evidence for the neutron's existence.

The discovery of the neutron completed the picture of the atomic nucleus as being composed of both protons and neutrons (collectively called **nucleons**). This discovery explained the existence of isotopes (atoms of the same element with different masses due to varying numbers of neutrons) and provided a more complete understanding of atomic mass and nuclear structure

## **Composition of an Atom and Isotopes**

Atoms, the basic building blocks of matter, are composed of even smaller particles. The two main regions of an atom are the **nucleus** and the **electron cloud**.

- The **nucleus** is the dense, central core of the atom. It contains **protons**, which have a positive charge, and **neutrons**, which have no charge and this provides the physical properties of elements.

- Surrounding the nucleus is the **electron cloud**, a region where negatively charged **electrons** are found. Electrons move rapidly within this region and provide the chemical properties of that element. 

![[Pasted image 20251126201611.png]]

 a key characteristic of atoms is that they are *electrically neutral*. This is because a neutral atom contains an **equal number of positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons**. Their opposite charges cancel each other out, resulting in a net charge of zero.

Ions are atoms that have gained or lost electrons, resulting in an overall positive or negative charge.

- **Negative ions (anions)** have more electrons than protons. For example, a particle with 1 proton and 2 electrons would be a negative ion with an overall charge of -1.
- **Positive ions (cations)** have fewer electrons than protons. For example, a particle with 3 protons and 2 electrons would be a positive ion with an overall charge of +1.

| Particle     | Symbol  | Actual Charge (Coulombs) | Relative Charge | Actual Mass (amu) | Actual Mass (kg) |
| ------------ | ------- | ------------------------ | --------------- | ----------------- | ---------------- |
| **Proton**   | p or p+ | +1.602 x 10⁻¹⁹           | +1              | 1.0073            | 1.673 x 10⁻²⁷    |
| **Neutron**  | n or n⁰ | 0                        | 0               | 1.0087            | 1.675 x 10⁻²⁷    |
| **Electron** | e or e⁻ | -1.602 x 10⁻¹⁹           | -1              | 0.00055           | 9.11 x 10⁻³¹     |
Notice how the relative masses of protons and neutrons are both approximately 1 amu, while the mass of an electron is significantly smaller. This is why most of an atom's mass is concentrated in its nucleus.

### *Atomic Number and Mass Number*

#### *Atomic Number (Z)*

The **atomic number (Z)** is one of the most fundamental properties of an atom.
It is defined as the number of **protons** in the nucleus of an atom. This number is unique to each element and determines its identity. For example, every atom with 6 protons is a carbon atom, and every atom with 8 protons is an oxygen atom.

The **atomic number** tells us **how many protons** are in the nucleus of an atom.

- Every element is defined by its number of **protons**.
- This number never changes for an element.  
    (For example, all hydrogen atoms have **1 proton**, all carbon atoms have **6 protons**, etc.)

It is therefore called the **atomic number** because it **identifies the type of atom** — it is the atom’s “identity tag.”

The atomic number is also equal to the number of electrons that are present in a **neutral atom** of an element
    E.g. the atomic number of lithium is 3, meaning that a neutral lithium atom has 3 protons and, therefore, also has 3 electrons

$$ Z = p^+ \text{ or } Z = e^- \text{ (in a neutral atom)} $$

#### *Mass Number (A)*

The **mass number (A)**, on the other hand, is the total number of **protons and neutrons** in an atom's nucleus. Protons and neutrons are collectively called **nucleons**. The mass number tells us about the total number of particles in the nucleus that contribute significantly to the atom's mass.

The **mass number** counts:
$$ \text{Mass Number (A)} = \text{Number of Protons} + \text{Number of Neutrons} $$
$$ \text{Mass number} = p^+ + n^0$$

It is the total number of **nucleons** (protons + neutrons) in the nucleus.

Since **protons and neutrons contribute almost all of the atom’s mass**, this number tells us the **mass of the nucleus** in whole-number form.



The name of an element, its atomic number and mass number can be represented by a shorthand symbol called **The Nuclear symbol**. For example, to represent neutral atoms of magnesium with 12 protons and 12 neutrons, the symbol is $^{24}_{12}\mathrm{Mg}$.
- The **atomic number is written as a subscript to the left** of the symbol of magnesium. 
- The **mass number is written as a superscript to the left of the symbol**. This method can be represented in general by the following illustration, in which X stands for any chemical symbol $^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}$
![[Pasted image 20251126210229.png|400]]
**To determine the number of protons, electrons, and neutrons in a neutral atom:**

1. The number of protons is equal to the atomic number (Z).
2. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons.
3. The number of neutrons is calculated by subtracting the atomic number (Z) from the mass number (A).

**To determine the number of protons, electrons, and neutrons in an ion:**

1. The number of protons is equal to the atomic number.
2. The number of electrons is calculated by subtracting the ion's charge from the atomic number (for cations) or adding the ion's charge to the atomic number (for anions).
3. The number of neutrons is calculated by subtracting the atomic number from the mass number.

### *Isotopes and Atomic Mass*

#### *Isotopes*

>[!Note]- **Isotopes**
>**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element that have the same number of **protons** but a different number of **neutrons**.  Since the number of protons defines the element, isotopes of an element all have the same atomic number (Z).  However, because they have different numbers of neutrons, they have different **mass numbers (A)**.

**For example, chlorine has two main naturally occurring isotopes:** $Cl-35$ and $Cl-37$. Both isotopes have the same number of protons (17) and electrons (17) but different mass numbers due to the different numbers of neutrons.

![[Pasted image 20251126211723.png]]

The way to represent an isotope is to write the **chemical** **symbol** (or the **word**) followed by a **dash** and then the **mass** **number**
- E.g. carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon containing 6 and 8 neutrons respectively
You can see that all three isotopes of hydrogen have 1 proton (their atomic number is 1), confirming they are all forms of hydrogen. However, the varying number of neutrons gives them different mass numbers.

Chemical reactions involve the interactions of **electrons**. Since isotopes of the same element have the *same number of protons and thus the same number of electrons in a neutral atom*, their electron configurations are the same. This leads to very similar **chemical reactivity**. However, their different masses due to the varying number of neutrons can slightly affect **physical properties** such as density, melting point, and boiling point, and also influence reaction rates in some cases, particularly in processes involving mass-dependent diffusion or reaction kinetics.

#### *Relative atomic mass Units* 

Measuring the actual mass of a single atom in standard units like kilograms is incredibly difficult due to their extremely small size.To work with atomic masses more conveniently, scientists use a relative scale based on a standard atom.

The standard atom used is the **carbon-12 isotope** (¹²C). This isotope is assigned a mass of exactly **12 atomic mass units (amu)**. *One atomic mass unit is therefore defined as one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom*.

>[!note] **Atomic mass Unit(a.m.u)**
> A standard unit of mass used to express the mass of atoms and molecules.
> It is defined as exactly **one-twelfth** of the mass of a carbon-12 atom which is equal to $1.660 × 10^{–24} g$

This unit is also sometimes called the **unified atomic mass unit (u)** or the **Dalton (Da)**

The atomic mass of any atom is determined by comparing it with the mass of the carbon-12 atom. The hydrogen-1 atom has an atomic mass of about 1/12 of that of the carbon-12 atom, or about 1 a.m.u. The precise value of the atomic mass of a hydrogen-1 atom is 1.007 a.m.u.

both protons and neutrons have a mass of approximately 1 amu. The mass of an electron is much, much smaller, almost negligible when considering the total mass of an atom (most of which comes from the nucleus). This reinforces why the mass number (protons + neutrons) is a good approximation of an atom's mass in amu.

- While the *mass number (A) is a whole number representing the count of protons and neutrons*, the actual atomic mass in amu is usually not a whole number (except for Carbon-12) due to small mass differences arising from nuclear binding energy and the small contribution of electrons. The mass number is a simple count of nucleons. The atomic mass unit is defined based on carbon-12.The actual masses of protons and neutrons are slightly more or less than exactly 1 amu, and the electrons, although very light, also contribute a small amount to the total mass Therefore, the atomic mass in amu is typically very close to, but not exactly, the mass number.

#### *Average Atomic Mass of Elements*

You might have noticed on the periodic table that the atomic masses listed for most elements are not whole numbers. For example, chlorine is listed with an atomic mass of approximately 35.45 amu. This is because the atomic mass shown on the periodic table is the **average atomic mass**.

Most elements exist naturally as a mixture of two or more isotopes. While each individual isotope has a mass number (a whole number), the naturally occurring sample of an element is a blend of these isotopes in specific proportions called **natural abundance**.

>[!note] **Average atomic mass**
>The average atomic mass is a **weighted average** of the masses of these isotopes, taking into account their relative abundances. 

$$ \text{Average atomic mass} = \sum \frac{(\text{atomic mass unit of the isotopes} \times \text{percentage abundance})}{\text{the sum of the percentage abundance}} $$

## **Atomic Models**

### *Dalton's atomic theory*

John Dalton, an English chemist and physicist, proposed his atomic theory in the early 19th century (around 1803-1808).  Based on his experiments and observations, particularly with gases, Dalton revived and expanded upon the ancient Greek idea of atoms. 

>[!Note] **Dalton's atomic model**
Dalton's atomic model is often referred to as the **"billiard ball" model** because he envisioned atoms *as tiny, solid, indivisible spheres*. He believed that atoms could not be broken down into anything simpler.

Dalton's atomic theory was a significant step forward in chemistry and was widely accepted for its time. Its simplicity was one of its strengths. It provided a framework for understanding chemical reactions and the behavior of matter based on the idea of fundamental, indivisible particles.

### *Thomson's Atomic Model*

J.J. Thomson, a British physicist, conducted experiments with cathode rays in the late 19th century. These experiments led to the groundbreaking discovery of the **electron** – a negatively charged particle much smaller than the atom. This discovery was a major challenge to Dalton's idea of the atom as an indivisible particle.

In 1904, based on his discovery of the electron and the understanding that atoms are electrically neutral, Thomson proposed his atomic model, often called the **"plum-pudding" model**

>[!Note] **Thomson's Atomic Model**
In the plum-pudding model, Thomson proposed that the atom is a *sphere of uniformly distributed positive charge, with negatively charged electrons embedded within it*, like plums in a pudding. He theorized that the amount of positive charge was equal in magnitude to the total negative charge of the electrons, resulting in a neutral atom.

While Thomson's model successfully incorporated the newly discovered electron and explained the overall neutrality of the atom, it had limitations. It didn't provide a clear explanation for the arrangement or movement of the electrons within the positive sphere.

### *Rutherford's Atomic Model*

Rutherford proposed a new model of the atom in 1911, often called the **"planetary" model** or **"nuclear" model**.

In this model, Rutherford proposed that:

>[!Note] **Rutherford's Atomic Model**
> -   
**The atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus at its center**. This nucleus contains most of the atom's mass.
> -   
**Electrons orbit the nucleus in a large, empty space**. Similar to planets orbiting the sun.
> - **The size of the nucleus is very small compared to the overall size of the atom**. Explaining why most alpha particles passed straight through during the gold foil experiment.

Rutherford's model was a significant improvement over Thomson's, as it explained the results of the *gold foil experiment*.  It correctly identified the presence of a nucleus where the positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated. However, Rutherford's model had a major limitation based on classical physics.

- According to **classical electromagnetic theory**, an electron orbiting a positively charged nucleus should continuously emit electromagnetic radiation and lose energy.
- This loss of energy would cause the electron to spiral inwards and eventually crash into the nucleus, leading to the collapse of the atom. This clearly doesn't happen, so Rutherford's model could not explain the **stability of the atom.** 

### *Bohr's Atomic Model*

**Niels Bohr**, a Danish physicist working with Rutherford, proposed his atomic model in 1913. Bohr's model aimed to address the stability issue of Rutherford's model by incorporating ideas from the emerging field of **quantum mechanics**.

Bohr's model is often described as a refinement of the planetary model, but with key differences based on quantized energy levels.  Here are Bohr's main postulates:

- **Electrons orbit the nucleus in specific, fixed orbits (energy levels) without emitting energy**. Electrons exist in stable, quantized energy states.
- **Each orbit has a definite energy and is called an energy shell or energy level**. Orbits are associated with specific energy values.
- **Electrons can only move between these allowed orbits by absorbing or emitting a specific amount of energy (a quantum).** Energy is absorbed to move to a higher level and emitted to move to a lower level.
- **The energy absorbed or emitted is equal to the difference in energy between the two orbits**. This explains the line spectra of elements.

Bohr's model successfully explained the line emission spectrum of hydrogen, a major triumph. It suggested that when an electron drops from a higher energy orbit to a lower one, it emits a photon of light with a specific energy (and thus a specific wavelength), corresponding to the energy difference between the orbits.

Bohr also proposed a rule for the *maximum number of electrons that can occupy each energy level or shell*. This is given by the formula $2n^2$, where '**n**' is the **principal quantum number representing the energy level** (n=1 for the first K-shell, n=2 for the second L-shell, and so on).

| Energy Level (n) | Maximum Number of Electrons (2n²) |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------- |
| 1                | 2(1)² = 2                         |
| 2                | 2(2)² = 8                         |
| 3                | 2(3)² = 18                        |
| 4                | 2(4)² = 32                        |

Using this rule, we can start to visualize the arrangement of electrons in atoms, known as **electron configuration**, within the Bohr model. Electrons fill the lowest energy levels first.

While Bohr's model was a significant improvement and introduced the concept of quantized energy levels, it still had limitations. It worked well for hydrogen (an atom with only one electron) but failed to accurately predict the spectra of atoms with more than one electron.

### *Quantum Mechanical Model*

The **Quantum Mechanical Model**, developed in the early 20th century by scientists like *Erwin Schrödinger and Werner Heisenberg*, arose because of the limitations of the Bohr model, particularly its inability to explain the behavior of multi-electron atoms and the wave-like nature of electrons.

Unlike the Bohr model, which depicted electrons orbiting the nucleus in fixed paths, the quantum mechanical model uses complex mathematical equations (like the Schrödinger equation) to describe the **probability** of finding an electron in a particular region of space around the nucleus. This region is called an **atomic orbital**

> **Atomic Orbital**
> >A region of space around the nucleus where there is a **high probability** of finding an electron.

Key features of the Quantum Mechanical Model include:

- **Electrons behave as both waves and particles (wave-particle duality).** This dual nature is fundamental to the model.
-   **Electron location is described by probability distributions**. We can't know the exact position and velocity of an electron simultaneously (Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle).
- **Electrons occupy specific energy levels, but within these levels, they are found in orbitals**. Energy levels are quantized, and orbitals have distinct shapes and orientations.
-   **Each atomic orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins (Pauli Exclusion Principle).** Understanding how electrons fill orbitals.

Atomic orbitals are grouped into **sublevels** (also called subshells) within each principal energy level (shell). These sublevels have different shapes and are denoted by letters: **s, p, d, and f**.

| Sublevel Type | Shape               | Number of Orbitals | Maximum Electron Capacity |
| ------------- | ------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------- |
| s             | Spherical           | 1                  | 2                         |
| p             | Dumbbell-shaped     | 3                  | 6                         |
| d             | More complex shapes | 5                  | 10                        |
| f             | Very complex shapes | 7                  | 14                        |
The arrangement of electrons in the various orbitals within an atom is called its **electron configuration**. The **ground-state electron configuration** is the arrangement of electrons in the lowest possible energy levels and orbitals.
