#  Chemical Bonding

### 1. **Why do atoms combine?**

Atoms combine to achieve a more stable electronic configuration and lower potential energy, typically by:
- **Filling their valence shells**: Atoms combine to achieve a complete outermost energy level, which usually corresponds to the octet rule (8 electrons in the valence shell for most atoms).
- **Lowering their potential energy**: A combination of atoms into molecules results in a system with less energy, which is more stable.
- **Forming bonds through attraction**: Atoms are attracted to each other through various types of bonding, such as ionic, covalent, or metallic bonds.

For example:
- Sodium (Na) donates one electron to chlorine (Cl) to form NaCl, an ionic bond.
- Two hydrogen atoms share electrons to form $H_2$, a covalent bond.


### 2. **Why are molecules more stable than free atoms?**

Molecules are more stable than free atoms because:
- **Bonding reduces energy**: Atoms in a molecule are held together by chemical bonds that lower the system's total potential energy.
- **Electron sharing or transfer stabilizes configurations**: By sharing or transferring electrons, atoms achieve noble gas configurations, which are inherently stable.
- **Electrostatic interactions**: Bonds like ionic or covalent bonds involve attractive forces that stabilize the molecule.

For example:
- Free hydrogen atoms ($H$) are highly reactive because they each have only one electron. When they form a $H_2$ molecule, the shared electrons provide stability.

---

### 3. **What forces act on two atoms as they come together?**

When two atoms approach each other:
1. **Attractive forces**:
   - The positively charged nucleus of one atom attracts the negatively charged electrons of the other atom.
   - This attraction leads to bond formation when the atoms are at an optimal distance.

2. **Repulsive forces**:
   - The electrons in the outer shells of both atoms repel each other due to their negative charges.
   - Similarly, the positively charged nuclei of the atoms repel each other.
   
   􀁨 repulsive force between the electrons of the atoms, since like charges repel
   
   􀁨 attractive force between the nucleus of one atom and the electrons of another 
   
   􀁨 repulsive force between the two positively-charged nuclei
   
   
1. **Balance of forces**:
   - At the right bond distance (called the **bond length**), the attractive and repulsive forces balance each other, resulting in a stable bond.

### 4. **Explain how the potential energy changes when two hydrogen atoms form a bond.**

The formation of a bond between two hydrogen atoms involves changes in potential energy:
![[Pasted image 20250109175947.png]]

1. **At large distances**:
   - The hydrogen atoms are far apart, and there is negligible interaction.
   - The potential energy is high because no bond has formed.

2. **As the atoms approach**:
   - The attractive forces between the nucleus of one atom and the electron of the other atom increase.
   - Potential energy decreases as the system becomes more stable.

3. **At the bond length**:
   - The attractive and repulsive forces balance.
   - The potential energy reaches its minimum value, representing the most stable configuration (a covalent bond in $H_2$).

4. **At very short distances**:
   - If the atoms get too close, the repulsive forces between the nuclei dominate.
   - Potential energy increases sharply as the system becomes unstable.

> The bond forms at a distance where the attractive forces slightly outweigh the repulsive ones, minimizing energy.

- The lowest point represents the bond energy, which is the energy released when the bond forms.

### The Octet Rule

- An atom is most stable when all its valence electron orbitals are full. In other words, the outer energy level of the atom contains the maximum number of electrons that it can. 
- A stable atom is also an unreactive one, and is unlikely to bond with other atoms
- **Atoms combine with each other only if the process leads to lowering of energy**. In the process each atom attains a stable outer octet(eight) of electrons. The **electron configuration and the strength of the nucleus-electron attraction(s)** determine the properties of an atom, the type and strength of chemical bonds form.

- All noble gases (except Helium 1s²) have an ns²np⁶ electron configuration.
- They are quite unreactive (some times termed as inert). because  all noble gases except He have *eight electrons* in their valence shells. Most atoms undergoing reactions also attain 8 valence electrons (ns²np⁶). This is known as **The Octet rule.**

>[!quote] the Octet Rule 
>states Atoms tend to gain, lose or transfer electrons until they have achieved an outer shell that contains an octet(eight) of electrons

### Types of Chemical Bonding

### 1. Why Do Metals Tend to Form Cations and Non-Metals Tend to Form Anions?

#### **Metals Forming Cations**
- **Electron Configuration:**
  - Metals typically have **1-3 electrons** in their outermost (valence) shell.
  - Losing these electrons allows metals to achieve a **stable noble gas configuration**.
    - Example: Sodium (Na):  
      $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$ → loses 1 electron → $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$ (stable configuration).
  
- **Low Ionization Energy:**
  - Metals have **low ionization energy**, meaning they lose electrons easily.
  - This is because the outer electrons are **loosely held** due to:
    1. Fewer protons exerting pull.
    2. Greater distance from the nucleus (large atomic radius).
  
- **Positive Charge (Cation):**
  - Losing electrons results in more protons than electrons, leading to a **positive charge**.

#### **Non-Metals Forming Anions**
- **Electron Configuration:**
  - Non-metals typically have **5-7 valence electrons**.
  - Gaining electrons allows non-metals to **complete their octet**, mimicking a noble gas configuration.
    - Example: Chlorine (Cl):  
      $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^5$ → gains 1 electron → $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6$.
  
- **High Electron Affinity:**
  - Non-metals have **high electron affinity**, meaning they readily attract electrons.
  - This is because of their:
    1. Smaller atomic radius (electrons are closer to the nucleus).
    2. Higher nuclear charge (more protons to pull electrons).

- **Negative Charge (Anion):**
  - Gaining electrons results in more electrons than protons, leading to a **negative charge**.

---

### 2. Why Do Two Non-Metals Combine to Form Compounds, but Two Metals Don’t?

#### **Two Non-Metals Forming Covalent Compounds**
- **Electronegativity:**
  - Non-metals have **high electronegativity**, meaning they strongly attract electrons.
  - When two non-metals interact, neither can completely donate electrons to the other.
  - Instead, they **share electrons** through **covalent bonding**.
    - Example: In $H_2O$, oxygen shares electrons with hydrogen atoms.
  
- **Stable Electron Configuration:**
  - Covalent bonds allow non-metals to share electrons and achieve a **full octet**.

#### **Why Two Metals Don’t Form a Compound**
- **Low Electronegativity:**
  - Metals have **low electronegativity**, so they don’t attract electrons strongly enough to share or transfer electrons between each other.
  
- **Delocalized Electrons:**
  - In metallic bonding, electrons are delocalized, forming a **"sea of electrons"** that surrounds positively charged metal ions.
  - This bonding doesn’t lead to discrete molecules or compounds but rather forms a metallic lattice structure.
  
- **Similar Properties:**
  - Metals have similar properties (low ionization energy, large atomic radius), making it energetically unfavorable for two metals to interact in a way that forms stable compounds.

### Conclusion
1. Metals do not combine and form compounds with each other because both of them are electron losers, i.e. they have a tendency to give away electrons to attain stability. Sharing of electros does not lead the metal atoms to stable electronic configurations.
2. **Two non-metals** combine to form **covalent compounds** because of their tendency to share electrons, while **two metals** form a metallic bond, which lacks discrete molecular structures and doesn’t form compounds in the traditional sense.

- Accordingly, atoms can combine to form three types of Bonds:

  - **Metal with Non-metal (Ionic bond)**
  - **Metal with metal (Metallic bond)**
  - **Non-metal with Non-metal (Covalent bond)**

## 1. Ionic Bonds (Electronvalent bond)

- It is formed by the electrostatic attraction between positive and negative ions.
  - It is formed when one atom gives off its electrons from one valence shell to the second atom.
  - The atoms losing electrons become a **cation (positive ion)**.
  - The atoms gaining electrons become an **anion (negative ion).**
  
  - When cations and anions attract together, they form an ionic solid.

- So, the bond formed as a result of electrostatic attraction b/n positive and negative ions is called an **electron valent or ionic bond.**

three steps involved in the formation of an electrovalent (or ionic) bond. These are:

-  removal of electron(s) from one atom. In this step, energy equal to the **ionization energy** is absorbed
-  gaining of electron(s) by the other atom. In this step, energy equal to the **electron affinity** is released
- combination of cations and anions. Columbic forces of attraction hold these ions together. In this step, energy equal to the **lattice energy** is released.

- **Ionic compounds** are usually formed between metal cations and non-metal anions.

### **Understanding the Energy Changes in the Formation of Lithium Fluoride (LiF)**

the formation of **lithium fluoride (LiF)**, focusing on the energy changes that occur during the electron-transfer process and the formation of the ionic solid. The goal is to show how despite the initial energy input required for the formation of ions, the final energy released when the ionic solid forms more than compensates for this, making the entire process exothermic.

---

### **1. Electron-Transfer Process for Lithium Fluoride (LiF)**

The formation of **LiF** involves the transfer of electrons between a lithium (Li) atom and a fluorine (F) atom. There are two main steps involved in this process:

#### Step 1: Ionization of Lithium
- A **gaseous lithium atom** (Li) loses an electron to form a **gaseous lithium ion** (Li⁺) and an electron. This process requires energy input because energy is needed to overcome the attraction between the electron and the nucleus of the lithium atom. The energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom is called **Ionization Energy (IE)**.

  $$\text{Li(g)} \rightarrow \text{Li}^+(g) + e^- \quad \text{(IE1 = +520 kJ)}$$

  - **Ionization energy (IE1)** is +520 kJ, meaning that it takes **520 kJ of energy** to remove one electron from a gaseous lithium atom.

#### Step 2: Electron Affinity of Fluorine
- A **gaseous fluorine atom** (F) gains the electron that was lost by the lithium atom, forming a **gaseous fluoride ion** (F⁻). This process releases energy because fluorine has a strong tendency to accept an electron (this is called **Electron Affinity (EA)**).

  $$\text{F(g)} + e^- \rightarrow \text{F}^-(g) \quad \text{(EA = -328 kJ)}$$

  - **Electron Affinity (EA)** is −328 kJ, meaning that **328 kJ of energy** is released when a fluorine atom gains an electron to form the fluoride ion.

---

### **2. Net Energy Change for Ion Formation**

When you combine both of the above steps, you get the following overall equation:

$$\text{Li(g)} + \text{F(g)} \rightarrow \text{Li}^+(g) + \text{F}^-(g)$$

- The **total energy change** for this process is:

  $$\text{IE1} + \text{EA} = +520 \, \text{kJ} - 328 \, \text{kJ} = +192 \, \text{kJ}$$

This means that **192 kJ** of energy is required to create the Li⁺ and F⁻ ions from the gaseous Li and F atoms.

---

### **3. Enthalpy of Formation of LiF(s)**

- Even though the **electron-transfer process** requires energy, the formation of **LiF(s)** from its constituent elements releases much more energy. This happens because the ions (Li⁺ and F⁻) attract each other to form an ionic solid, which is highly stable.

- The **standard heat of formation (ΔHf°)** of solid LiF is **−617 kJ/mol**. This means that when **1 mole of LiF(s)** is formed from its elements (Li and F), **617 kJ of energy** is released.

---

### **4. Steps Involved in the Formation of LiF Solid**

After the ions (Li⁺ and F⁻) are created in the gaseous state, they combine to form **LiF(s)**, the solid ionic compound. There are two significant steps in this process:

#### **Step 1: Formation of LiF(g)**
- The gaseous ions **Li⁺(g)** and **F⁻(g)** come together to form gaseous lithium fluoride (LiF(g)).

  $$\text{Li}^+(g) + \text{F}^-(g) \rightarrow \text{LiF(g)} \quad \Delta H = -755 \, \text{kJ}$$

  - This step is **exothermic**, releasing **755 kJ of energy** when Li⁺ and F⁻ ions combine to form gaseous LiF.

#### **Step 2: Formation of LiF(s)**
- Finally, the gaseous LiF molecules cool down and arrange themselves into a **crystalline solid**. The ions attract each other strongly due to their opposite charges, forming a solid lattice.

  $$\text{LiF(g)} \rightarrow \text{LiF(s)} \quad \Delta H = -1050 \, \text{kJ}$$

  - This step is also **exothermic**, releasing **1050 kJ of energy** as the ions arrange into a solid lattice structure.

---

### **5. Total Energy for the Formation of LiF(s)**

When combining the energy changes from the two steps of forming the solid, the total energy released is:

$$\Delta H_{\text{total}} = -755 \, \text{kJ} + (-1050 \, \text{kJ}) = -1805 \, \text{kJ}$$

Thus, a total of **1805 kJ** of energy is released when 1 mole of **LiF(s)** forms from its gaseous ions.

---

### **6. Lattice Energy (U)**

- The **lattice energy** refers to the energy released when gaseous ions come together to form a crystalline solid. For LiF, this energy is **1050 kJ/mol**. This indicates the strength of the ionic bonds in LiF.

---

### **Conclusion: Why Does the Process Happen?**

Although the electron-transfer process itself requires energy (192 kJ), the **lattice energy** of **1050 kJ** released when the ions form a solid is much greater than this. This makes the entire process highly exothermic, meaning energy is released overall, which is why the formation of LiF occurs spontaneously.

- **Lattice Energy** is crucial because it helps overcome the endothermic steps (the initial energy required to form the ions) and makes the formation of the solid ionic compound energetically favorable.

### **Summary of Key Points:**

1. **Ionization** of lithium requires energy (+520 kJ).
2. **Electron affinity** of fluorine releases energy (−328 kJ).
3. Overall, the formation of gaseous ions requires an energy input of **+192 kJ**.
4. However, the formation of solid **LiF** releases a total of **−1805 kJ**, making the process overall exothermic.
5. The **lattice energy** of LiF is **1050 kJ**, which is a major contributor to the exothermic nature of solid formation.

In essence, despite the initial energy input to form the ions, the strong ionic bonds in the crystalline structure of LiF result in a large release of energy, making the formation of LiF favorable.

### Understanding the Born–Haber Cycle and Lattice Energy Calculation

The Born–Haber cycle is a powerful tool used to calculate lattice energies indirectly, based on Hess's law. Lattice energy refers to the enthalpy change that occurs when gaseous ions form an ionic solid. The cycle involves breaking down the formation of an ionic solid into a series of individual steps, and Hess's law allows us to sum the enthalpy changes of these steps to find the overall enthalpy change for the formation of the ionic solid.

### Key Concepts:

- **Lattice Energy**: The energy required to separate 1 mole of an ionic solid into its constituent ions in the gas phase.
- **Hess's Law**: The total enthalpy change of a reaction is the sum of the enthalpy changes of the steps into which the reaction can be divided. 

---

### Born–Haber Cycle for Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

In this example, we will use the Born–Haber cycle to calculate the lattice energy of sodium chloride (NaCl). There are two possible routes for the formation of NaCl:

- **Direct Route**: Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s), with an enthalpy of formation $\Delta H_f^\circ = -411 \, \text{kJ/mol}$.
- **Indirect Route**: The formation of NaCl(s) through a series of five steps, each with its own enthalpy change.

---

### Steps in the Indirect Route of the Born–Haber Cycle

1. **Step 1: Vaporization of Sodium (Na)**  
   In this step, solid sodium is converted into gaseous sodium atoms. The enthalpy change for this process is called the heat of sublimation.
   - **Reaction**: Na(s) → Na(g)  
   - **Enthalpy Change** $\Delta H_{\text{step 1}}$: $+108 \, \text{kJ/mol}$

2. **Step 2: Dissociation of Chlorine Molecule (Cl₂)**  
   Chlorine molecules (Cl₂) dissociate into individual chlorine atoms. The bond energy for Cl₂ is used here.
   - **Reaction**: ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g)  
   - **Enthalpy Change** $\Delta H_{\text{step 2}}$: $+120 \, \text{kJ/mol}$ (This is half the bond energy of Cl₂, $\frac{1}{2} \times 240 \, \text{kJ/mol}$).

3. **Step 3: Ionization of Sodium (Na)**  
   Sodium atoms in the gas phase lose an electron to form Na⁺ ions.
   - **Reaction**: Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻  
   - **Enthalpy Change** $\Delta H_{\text{step 3}}$: Ionization energy $\text{IE}_1 = +496 \, \text{kJ/mol}$.

4. **Step 4: Electron Affinity of Chlorine (Cl)**  
   A chlorine atom in the gas phase gains an electron to form a chloride ion (Cl⁻). This process is exothermic and releases energy.
   - **Reaction**: Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g)  
   - **Enthalpy Change** $\Delta H_{\text{step 4}}$: Electron affinity $\text{EA} = -349 \, \text{kJ/mol}$.

5. **Step 5: Formation of NaCl(s) from Na⁺ and Cl⁻**  
   Finally, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in the gas phase come together to form solid NaCl. This step releases energy in the form of lattice energy, which we need to calculate.
   - **Reaction**: Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s)  
   - **Enthalpy Change** $\Delta H_{\text{step 5}}$: Lattice energy $U_{\text{NaCl}}$.
   
![[Pasted image 20240914211638.png]]


### Using Hess's Law to Calculate the Lattice Energy

We can now use Hess’s law to calculate the lattice energy of NaCl by summing the enthalpy changes of the individual steps. According to Hess’s law, the total enthalpy change of the direct route should be equal to the sum of the enthalpy changes of the individual steps in the indirect route.

- **Total Enthalpy Change for Direct Route**:  
  $\Delta H_f^\circ (\text{NaCl}) = -411 \, \text{kJ/mol}$.

- **Enthalpy Changes for Indirect Route**:  
  Sum of the enthalpy changes of all five steps:  
  $$\Delta H_{\text{total}} = \Delta H_{\text{step 1}} + \Delta H_{\text{step 2}} + \Delta H_{\text{step 3}} + \Delta H_{\text{step 4}} + \Delta H_{\text{step 5}}$$
  $$\Delta H_{\text{total}} = 108 \, \text{kJ/mol} + 120 \, \text{kJ/mol} + 496 \, \text{kJ/mol} + (-349 \, \text{kJ/mol}) + U_{\text{NaCl}}$$

- **Solving for Lattice Energy ($U_{\text{NaCl}}$)**:  
  We can now rearrange the equation to solve for the lattice energy of NaCl:
  $$U_{\text{NaCl}} = \Delta H_f^\circ (\text{NaCl}) - (\Delta H_{\text{step 1}} + \Delta H_{\text{step 2}} + \Delta H_{\text{step 3}} + \Delta H_{\text{step 4}})$$
  $$U_{\text{NaCl}} = -411 \, \text{kJ/mol} - (108 + 120 + 496 - 349) \, \text{kJ/mol}$$
  $$U_{\text{NaCl}} = -411 \, \text{kJ/mol} - 375 \, \text{kJ/mol} = -786 \, \text{kJ/mol}$$

Thus, the lattice energy of NaCl is $-786 \, \text{kJ/mol}$, meaning that 786 kJ/mol of energy is released when Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions combine to form solid NaCl.

---

### Significance of Lattice Energy and Ionic Solids

- **Ionic solids** exist only because the lattice energy (which is highly exothermic) is greater than the energy required to break apart the atoms and ions in the process of ion formation. This large exothermic lattice energy stabilizes the ionic crystal structure, making ionic compounds solid at room temperature.
  
- **Lattice Energy and Properties**: The magnitude of lattice energy influences properties like the **melting point**, **hardness**, **solubility**, and **stability** of ionic solids. A higher lattice energy generally means a higher melting point and stronger ionic bonds, making the substance harder and less soluble.


- **Traditional View**: Hess’s law is often used for calculating the total enthalpy change of a reaction, regardless of the pathway, as long as the starting and ending points are the same. It is a fundamental principle that reflects the **conservation of energy**.

By understanding and applying Hess's law, we can calculate values like lattice energy that are difficult to measure directly, providing valuable insights into the forces that hold ionic solids together.
  

## **Factors affecting the formation of Ionic Bonds**

#### **Ionization Energy (IE)**

- The lower the **IE**, the greater is the ease of the formation of cations.

#### **Electron Affinity (EA)**

- A higher **electron affinity** favors the formation of an ion.

- A low ionization energy of **metals** favors the formation of ionic bonds.
- A high electron affinity of non-metals favors the formation of ionic bonds.

#### **Lattice Energy (U)**

- When a cation or an anion gets closer, they get attracted to each other due to electrostatic force between them.

- This energy reduces the potential energy.

- If these electrostatic forces (Coulombic forces) get stronger than bond energy gets released and a more strong ionic bond is formed.

- Like Coulombic force, large lattice energy would favor the formation of an ionic bond.

- Thus, lattice energy is the measure of **Coulombic attractive force** between combining ions, meaning like Coulombic forces, lattice energy depends **directly** on the ionic charges $(q_1\times q_2)$ and **inversely** on the distance (*r*) between them.

$$ U = \frac{q_1×q_2}{r} $$

- If the total energy released is more than that which is absorbed, then the formation of an **ionic compound** is favored.

### Steps to writing a Lewis Formula

1. **Determine the total valence electrons:**
   - It is the sum of the valence electrons of each atom.
   - If there is a positive ion in the compound, subtract the charge, meaning that the compound has given two electrons.
   - If a negative ion, do the opposite for the same reason.

2. **Write the skeletal structure:**
   - When writing the skeletal structure, it is good to bear in mind that a more electropositive element often goes to the center, and it is called the **central atom**.
   - If it is more electronegative, the atom cannot have equal sides, meaning it cannot be at the center. Rather, all the terminal atoms must go there. These are called **terminal atoms**.
   - Arrange the atoms and distribute terminal atoms so that each (except H) has an octet.

3. **Place electron pairs around terminal atoms** so that each gets an octet except H

4. **If a central atom has fewer electrons**, a multiple bond is needed.
   - This involves moving lone pairs of electrons from a terminal atom to a region to become a bond, a double bond, or a triple bond.
