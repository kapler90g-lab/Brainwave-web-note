The three laws of chemical combination are fundamental principles that govern how elements combine to form compounds. 
## the law of conservation of mass 

The first law is the **Law of Conservation of Mass**, proposed by Antoine Lavoisier in 1789. This law states that mass is neither created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction; instead, it can only change forms. Therefore, the total mass of reactants before a reaction must equal the total mass of products after the reaction. This principle laid the groundwork for modern chemistry by establishing that chemical equations must be balanced.

## the law of Definite proportions

The second law is the **Law of Definite Proportions**, introduced by Joseph Proust in 1799. According to this law, a chemical compound always contains the same elements in fixed proportions by mass, regardless of its source or how it was prepared. This means that a given compound, such as water (H₂O), will always have a mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen that is consistent (approximately 1:8).

## the law of multiple proportion 

The third law is the **Law of Multiple Proportions**, formulated by John Dalton in 1804. This law states that when two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element will be in simple whole number ratios. For example, carbon can combine with oxygen to form both carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO₂), and the ratio of the masses of oxygen in these compounds is 1:2.

These laws have significantly advanced our understanding of chemical reactions and the nature of matter, forming the foundation for [[stoichiometry]] and modern chemical theory.
