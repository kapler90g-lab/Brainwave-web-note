
### Detailed Explanation of Millikan’s Oil Drop Experiment

Robert Millikan’s oil drop experiment, conducted in 1909, was a groundbreaking experiment that measured the charge of a single electron. The experiment's result, combined with Thomson’s charge-to-mass ratio, helped determine the mass of an electron. Here's a detailed breakdown of the theoretical and mathematical principles involved:

#### **The Experimental Setup:**

Millikan used small, electrically charged oil droplets suspended in an electric field to measure the charge on each droplet. The setup involved:
- A closed chamber with oil droplets sprayed into it.
- Two parallel metal plates, one above and one below the chamber, that created a uniform electric field when a voltage was applied.
- A microscope to observe the motion of the droplets.
- X-rays to ionize air molecules and charge the oil droplets.

#### **Step-by-Step Process:**

1. **Forces Acting on the Oil Droplet:**
   When the oil droplet is in the chamber, two primary forces act on it:
   - **Gravitational Force ($F_g$)**: This force pulls the droplet downward and is given by:
     $$
     F_g = mg
     $$
     where $m$ is the mass of the droplet and $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity.

   - **Buoyant Force ($F_b$)**: Since the droplet is in air, a buoyant force counteracts some of the gravitational force. The effective force pulling the droplet down is reduced.

2. **Motion of the Droplet Without an Electric Field:**
   Initially, no electric field is applied. The droplet falls due to gravity, but after some time, it reaches terminal velocity ($v_t$) because the viscous drag force ($F_d$) from the air balances the downward gravitational force. The drag force is given by Stokes' law:
   $$
   F_d = 6\pi r \eta v_t
   $$
   where:
   - $r$ is the radius of the droplet,
   - $\eta$ is the viscosity of air, and
   - $v_t$ is the terminal velocity.

   At terminal velocity:
   $$
   F_g = F_d
   $$

   Thus, the weight of the droplet (minus buoyancy) can be calculated from the terminal velocity.

3. **Applying an Electric Field:**
   After determining the terminal velocity, Millikan applied a known voltage ($V$) across the plates to create a uniform electric field ($E$), which exerts an upward force ($F_e$) on the charged droplet:
   $$
   F_e = qE
   $$
   where $q$ is the charge on the oil droplet and $E$ is the electric field strength. The electric field is related to the applied voltage and the distance between the plates ($d$) by:
   $$
   E = \frac{V}{d}
   $$

   When the electric force ($F_e$) equals the gravitational force ($F_g$), the droplet can be suspended in mid-air (i.e., it is in equilibrium). At this point:
   $$
   qE = F_g
   $$
   This equation allows for the calculation of the charge $q$ on the droplet:
   $$
   q = \frac{mg}{E} = \frac{mgd}{V}
   $$

4. **Repeating the Experiment:**
   Millikan repeated the experiment many times, measuring the charge on different oil droplets. He observed that the charges on the droplets were always a multiple of a fundamental value. He concluded that this smallest charge was the charge of a single electron, which he found to be:
   $$
   e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C}
   $$

#### **Theoretical Significance:**

Millikan’s experiment confirmed that:
- The charge on any object is quantized, meaning it is always an integer multiple of the electron's charge ($e$).
- The precise charge of a single electron was determined for the first time.

#### **Combining Millikan's and Thomson’s Results:**

Millikan’s value for the charge of an electron ($e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C) was later combined with J.J. Thomson’s earlier charge-to-mass ratio ($e/m$) to calculate the mass of the electron:
$$
\frac{e}{m} = 1.76 \times 10^8 \, \frac{\text{C}}{\text{g}}
$$

Rearranging the equation to solve for $m$:
$$
m = \frac{e}{\frac{e}{m}} = \frac{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C}}{1.76 \times 10^8 \, \frac{\text{C}}{\text{g}}}
$$

Calculating $m$:
$$
m = 9.11 \times 10^{-28} \, \text{g} = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \, \text{kg}
$$

Thus, the mass of an electron was determined to be **$9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg**.

### **Conclusion:**

The oil drop experiment provided crucial evidence for the quantization of electric charge and allowed for the precise measurement of the electron's charge. Combining this result with the charge-to-mass ratio from Thomson's experiments allowed scientists to calculate the mass of an electron. This was a key step in the development of atomic theory, as it demonstrated that atoms contain smaller, subatomic particles.
