### J.J. Thomson's Discovery of the Electron’s Charge-to-Mass Ratio (e/m)

**Overview**:
In 1897, J.J. Thomson conducted a series of experiments using a **cathode ray tube** to measure the **charge-to-mass ratio (e/m)** of the electron. This ratio was crucial in determining the nature of the cathode rays and proving that they were made of **negatively charged particles** (electrons). Here's the step-by-step theoretical and mathematical explanation of how he arrived at the value of the electron's charge-to-mass ratio.

---

### **Experimental Setup**

1. **Cathode Ray Tube**: 
   - Thomson used a **discharge tube** with an **anode** and **cathode** inside. A stream of **cathode rays** (electrons) was produced by applying a high voltage across the tube.
   
2. **Deflection Plates**: 
   - He applied a **magnetic field** and **electric field** to the cathode rays, observing how the rays were deflected by these fields.

3. **Objective**: 
   - By analyzing how the rays (electrons) were deflected, Thomson calculated the **charge-to-mass ratio** (\( e/m \)) of the electron.

---

### **Step 1: Deflection in an Electric Field**

When cathode rays (electrons) pass through an **electric field** created by two charged plates, they experience a force \( F_e \), which causes them to deviate from their straight-line path.

- **Force from Electric Field**: 
  - The force on the electron due to the electric field \( E \) is given by:
  
    $$F_e = e \cdot E$$

  - Where:
    - \( F_e \) = Electric force on the electron.
    - \( e \) = Charge of the electron.
    - \( E \) = Strength of the electric field.

- **Kinematic Effect**: 
  - The electron experiences **acceleration** perpendicular to its initial direction due to this force, which causes it to move in a **parabolic path**.
  - The distance of deflection \( d \) can be related to the force using Newton’s second law:
  

    $$F_e = m \cdot a$$


    Where \( a \) is the acceleration of the electron and \( m \) is its mass.

### **Step 2: Deflection in a Magnetic Field**

When the cathode rays pass through a **magnetic field** (\( B \)) that is perpendicular to their direction of motion, they experience a force \( F_m \) that bends their trajectory.

- **Force from Magnetic Field**: 
  - The force on a moving electron due to the magnetic field is given by the Lorentz force:
  

    $$F_m = e \cdot v \cdot B$$

  - Where:
    - \( F_m \) = Magnetic force on the electron.
    - \( e \) = Charge of the electron.
    - \( v \) = Velocity of the electron.
    - \( B \) = Strength of the magnetic field.

- **Circular Motion**: 
  - The magnetic force causes the electron to follow a **circular path**. The centripetal force required for circular motion is provided by the magnetic force:
  

    $$F_m = \frac{m \cdot v^2}{r}$$

  
  - Where \( r \) is the radius of the electron's circular path. Equating the forces gives:


    $$e \cdot v \cdot B = \frac{m \cdot v^2}{r}$$


    Simplifying:

    $$r = \frac{m \cdot v}{e \cdot B}$$


---

### **Step 3: Balancing Electric and Magnetic Fields (No Deflection)**

Thomson adjusted the **electric field** and **magnetic field** such that their effects **canceled each other out**, and the electron passed through the region **undeflected**. This condition allowed him to calculate the velocity of the electron.

- When the electric force \( F_e \) and the magnetic force \( F_m \) are equal and opposite, the electron is not deflected:

  
  $$ e \cdot E = e \cdot v \cdot B$$

- Simplifying for the velocity \( v \):


  $$v = \frac{E}{B}$$

---

### **Step 4: Calculating the Charge-to-Mass Ratio (e/m)**

Once the velocity \( v \) of the electron was determined from the condition of no deflection, Thomson used the magnetic deflection data to calculate the charge-to-mass ratio \( e/m \).

- Using the magnetic force equation again and substituting the value of \( v \):

  
  $$r = \frac{m \cdot v}{e \cdot B}$$
  

  Substituting $v = \frac{E}{B}$ into the equation:

 $$ r = \frac{m \cdot \frac{E}{B}}{e \cdot B}$$


  Rearranging for \( e/m \):

  
$$  \frac{e}{m} = \frac{E}{B^2 \cdot r}$$


---

### **Step 5: Thomson's Results**

By carefully measuring the values of the electric field \( E \), the magnetic field \( B \), and the radius of the electron’s path \( r \), Thomson was able to calculate the **charge-to-mass ratio** (\( e/m \)) of the electron. He found that:


$$\frac{e}{m} = 1.76 \times 10^{11} \, \text{C/kg}$$


This was a key discovery because it showed that cathode rays were made of **tiny, negatively charged particles** (electrons) with a much smaller mass than atoms, proving that atoms were divisible.

---

### **Key Concepts in Thomson's Experiment**

- **Cathode Rays**: A stream of **negatively charged particles** (electrons).
- **Electric Field Deflection**: Electric fields cause electrons to accelerate due to their negative charge.
- **Magnetic Field Deflection**: Magnetic fields bend the path of moving electrons in a circular motion.
- **Balancing Fields**: When electric and magnetic fields are balanced, the electron's velocity can be calculated.
- **Charge-to-Mass Ratio**: From the deflection data, Thomson calculated the ratio of the charge of the electron to its mass, proving the **existence of subatomic particles**.

---

### **Conclusion**

Thomson's calculation of the **charge-to-mass ratio** provided the first evidence that cathode rays were not just energy but were made of **tiny particles** with a negative charge (later called **electrons**). This discovery showed that atoms contained even smaller constituents, marking a major milestone in atomic theory.
